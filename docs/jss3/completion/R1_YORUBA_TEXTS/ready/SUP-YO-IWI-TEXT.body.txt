Objectives: say what iwì egúngún (also called ẹ̀sà) is and who chants it; explain why a poem's words alone do not show its genre; name features of ẹ̀sà delivery reported in a published study; read a short oríkì closely; and state the limits of a modern example.

READ FIRST — MODERN EQUIVALENT: the three lines below are an original oríkì composed by a researcher for a 2022 phonetics experiment, in which one trained chanter spoke it and then chanted it in ìjálá and in ẹ̀sà style. They are not a chant handed down in the egúngún tradition, were not performed at an egúngún festival, and are not the school's missing original text. We use them because the study prints the words with an English translation, reports how the same words are delivered in ẹ̀sà style, and is openly licensed.

What iwì egúngún is (school summary of section 3 of the study): ẹ̀sà, also called iwì egúngún (the article spells it Ìwí Egúngún), is chanted praise connected with Egúngún, the ancestral masquerade. Devotees chant praises in ẹ̀sà style to honour the spirits of departed ancestors. The study reports that the same words can be used in different genres, so the words alone do not identify a genre; the style of delivery does. Ìjálá is chanted with vibrato. Ẹ̀sà has no vibrato; it uses inserted and lengthened vowels, and a triply long vowel [ooo] is a recurrent mark of ẹ̀sà at the end of the first line. In the experiment the chanter put a long [ooo] at the start of line 1, which the written words do not contain, and chanted at a higher pitch than in ìjálá.

Text (lines 1, 2 and 4 of the four printed lines; line 3 is not reproduced here). Yoruba as printed in the study, example (3), "An original Yorùbá poem":
Line 1: Adédùntán Àbẹ̀jẹ́ ọmọ Bàbálọ́jà
Line 2: ẹyinjúu Ọmọladùṇ baríọlá ọmọba Lépolóyin
Line 4: dúdú wù mí, dúdú dá'mi l'ọ́rùn máa wolẹ̀ máa rọra olówó tí ń f'owó ṣàánú
Printed English translation:
Line 1: "Adeduntan, the child of Babaloja"
Line 2: "the eyeball of Omoladun, the honorable princess of Lepoloyin"
Line 4: "(your) blackness is alluring, walk cautiously (you) benevolent rich"
Teacher note: the printed spelling "Ọmọladùṇ" (with a dot under n) is kept exactly as published; it appears to be a typesetting slip for Ọmọladùn. Do not correct it silently in class; point it out.

Reading (school notes, not from the study): oríkì praises a person by name, family and epithets. Line 1 gives a name and a parent's name; line 2 praises with a comparison ("eyeball" for someone treasured) and gives the person's title as printed in the translation; line 4 turns to advice. Words of ẹ̀sà style such as the opening [ooo] are added in delivery and are not written in the text. Performance is not marked from this page: a teacher-approved ẹ̀sà recording or invited chanter is needed to hear the style.

Practice:
1. Say why this lesson calls the text a modern equivalent, and name who composed it. (2)
2. Give the other name for ẹ̀sà used in the scheme and say whom devotees honour when they chant it. (2)
3. Why can a listener not decide the genre of a poem from its words alone? (2)
4. Name two features of ẹ̀sà delivery reported in the study, and say where [ooo] occurs. (3)
5. Line 1 names a person and a parent. Copy the parent's name and say what the line does. (2)
6. Explain "the eyeball of Omoladun" in one sentence. (2)
7. Give two reasons this experiment cannot tell us how all egúngún chanters perform. (2)
Answers and marking guide: 1 composed for the experiment, not inherited from the egúngún tradition (1); Iyabode B. Alaga, third author (1). 2 iwì egúngún (1); ancestors / the spirits of the departed (1). 3 the same words can be used in several genres (1); style of delivery, such as vibrato or vowel lengthening, distinguishes them (1). 4 any two of: no vibrato; inserted or lengthened vowels; higher pitch than ìjálá (2); [ooo] at the end of the first line as a recurrent mark, or at the start of line 1 in the experiment (1). 5 Bàbálọ́jà (1); it praises Adédùntán by naming his family or parent (1). 6 a comparison for someone dear or precious (2; 1 for a literal-only answer). 7 one chanter only (1); the poem was newly composed and the study is a pilot (1). Total 15 marks written. No marks are given for performance.
Assessment and feedback: written knowledge is recorded separately from observed performance. This section teaches genre knowledge and close reading of one modern example; it does not replace study of an inherited egúngún text and does not certify that pupils can perform ẹ̀sà.

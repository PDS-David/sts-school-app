## 1. WHO establishment and operational dates
The source says WHO “was established in 1964 and became operational in 1948”. The 1964 date is incorrect.
- WHO's Constitution was adopted and signed on **22 July 1946**.
- The Constitution entered into force on **7 April 1948**, now marked as World Health Day.
- An Interim Commission performed preparatory functions until 31 August 1948; the permanent Organization succeeded it on **1 September 1948**.
- Safe student wording: **“The WHO Constitution was adopted in 1946, came into force on 7 April 1948, and the permanent Organization began on 1 September 1948.”**

## 2. Antiseptics, disinfectants and antibiotics
The source says “disinfectants are stronger antiseptic”. This is not the correct distinction.
- **Antiseptic:** antimicrobial substance formulated for use on living tissue/skin.
- **Disinfectant:** antimicrobial product used on inanimate objects and surfaces. It may not destroy bacterial spores.
- The terms are not simply “weak” and “strong” versions of the same product. Their intended use, composition, efficacy and tissue toxicity differ.
- Disinfectants must not be applied to skin unless a product is specifically labelled and approved for that use. Do not mix products or create dilution instructions in the student material; follow the product label and trained supervision.
- **Antibiotics** act against susceptible bacteria; they do not treat viral infections. Resistance means not every antibiotic works against every bacterium. The source's list of examples should not be read as a recommendation to self-medicate.

## 3. Water protection and treatment
| Source method | Correct principle / qualification |
|---|---|
| Alum | Coagulation/flocculation helps suspended particles clump and settle, improving clarity. Alum alone does **not** disinfect water or guarantee it is safe to drink. |
| Boiling | Heat can inactivate disease-causing microorganisms; used as an emergency household treatment when advised by public-health authorities. |
| Filtration | Removes particles and, depending on filter design, may remove some microorganisms. Ordinary cloth/rough filtration does not guarantee safe drinking water. |
| Chlorination | Properly controlled chlorine disinfection inactivates many disease-causing organisms and may leave a protective residual. It requires approved public-health procedures; no dose/concentration is supplied here. |
| Clean storage | Treated water can be re-contaminated by dirty containers or handling; use clean, covered storage. |

The source's ordering “add alum, boil, filter, add chlorine” should not be taught as a universal fixed sequence. Treatment steps depend on source-water quality and approved local guidance.

## 4. Refuse and sewage
- **Refuse/solid waste** includes discarded solid materials from homes, businesses and industries.
- **Sewage/wastewater** includes liquid waste containing human excreta and other wastewater.
- Safe waste management includes collection, treatment and disposal through approved systems. Uncontrolled dumping or open burning can create health and environmental hazards.
- Sanitary landfills and properly designed/operated incineration are managed facilities; “dumping in an isolated area” is not a safe general recommendation.
- Sewage should be treated before discharge or reuse. Untreated sewage can contaminate water and spread pathogens.

## 5. Vector control
The source lists draining swamps, clearing bushes, mosquito nets, insect repellents, insecticides and oil on stagnant water.
- Source reduction (removing or managing standing-water containers and other breeding sites) is a recognised vector-control measure.
- Insecticide-treated nets and repellents used according to their labels can reduce mosquito bites.
- Insecticide spraying and larviciding require approved public-health programmes and trained personnel; effectiveness depends on vector species, resistance and local conditions.
- “Spray oil on stagnant water” is not a safe universal instruction and should not be presented as a student action. Oils can affect non-target organisms and water quality.
- “Use of drugs” is not vector control. Medicines may be used for disease prevention or treatment under health-professional guidance, but they do not control the vector population.

## 6. Health organisations
- **WHO:** the United Nations specialised agency for health; provides international leadership, standards, technical support, monitoring and coordination.
- **UNICEF:** a UN agency focused on children; supports child health, nutrition, immunisation, education, protection and emergency assistance.
- **International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement:** includes the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC), and National Societies. The ICRC's origins date to 1863; the IFRC was founded in 1919. The source's generic “International Red Cross” label should be clarified rather than treated as one single institution.
- **UNESCO:** a UN specialised agency for education, science, culture and communication. It is not a health agency, although education, science and water-related work can support health indirectly.
- **Nigeria's Ministry of Health and university teaching hospitals:** national institutions involved in health policy, health services, clinical care, training and research. Their exact names and structures may change over time; use current official Nigerian government terminology in final student material.

## 7. WHO's health definition
The WHO Constitution defines health as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. The source should use this standard wording if the definition is included.

## Safe delivery decisions
1. Correct the WHO date to 1946 adoption / 7 April 1948 entry into force / 1 September 1948 permanent Organization.
2. Define antiseptic and disinfectant by where they are used, not by relative strength.
3. Separate water clarification (alum) from disinfection (boiling/chlorine/validated filtration).
4. Remove uncontrolled oil-on-water, unqualified chemical-spraying and “use of drugs” as universal vector-control instructions.
5. Distinguish refuse from sewage and avoid recommending uncontrolled dumping or open burning.
6. Distinguish WHO, UNICEF, ICRC, IFRC and UNESCO.
7. Keep all chemical concentrations, pesticide doses, mixing instructions and disinfection recipes out of student content.

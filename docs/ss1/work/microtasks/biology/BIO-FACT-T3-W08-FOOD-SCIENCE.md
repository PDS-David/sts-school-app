## 1. Review of food-preservation methods

| Method | Verified principle | Correction/qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Salting | Salt lowers water activity and creates osmotic conditions that inhibit many spoilage organisms. | It does not sterilise food or guarantee safety if salt levels/storage are inadequate. |
| Drying | Removes available water, lowering water activity and limiting microbial growth. | It does not necessarily kill all microorganisms or destroy toxins already produced. |
| Smoking | Combines drying with antimicrobial compounds in smoke; may also affect flavour and colour. | The source's “oxygen-deficient environment kills microbes” is not the main general principle. Smoking does not guarantee pathogen elimination. |
| Refrigeration | Low temperature slows the growth of many microorganisms and chemical reactions. | Refrigeration does not sterilise food; some microbes can still grow slowly. |
| Freezing | Stops or greatly slows microbial growth while food remains frozen. | Freezing does not reliably kill all pathogens; microbes may resume growth after thawing. |
| Pasteurisation | A validated heat treatment reduces specified pathogens and spoilage organisms in a food. | It is not sterilisation. The source's single parameter “72°C for 10 minutes” is not a universal pasteurisation schedule. |
| Canning/bottling | Combines appropriate thermal processing with a hermetically sealed container to prevent recontamination. | Not all canned foods receive the same process. Low-acid foods require validated processing; unsafe home processing can cause botulism. Do not provide home-canning instructions. |
| Food irradiation | Uses controlled **ionising radiation** (e.g. gamma rays, electron beams or X-rays) to reduce pests/pathogens and delay sprouting/ripening. | The source's “ultraviolet rays” is wrong for standard food irradiation. UV is mainly a surface/water/air treatment and penetrates food poorly. Irradiated food does not become radioactive. |
| Chemical preservatives | Approved additives can inhibit specific spoilage organisms or oxidation when used within regulated conditions. | “Harmless chemicals” is misleading. Safety depends on the additive, food, amount and regulatory approval; no additive or dose is recommended here. |

## 2. Pasteurisation temperature/time claim
The source gives 72°C for about 10 minutes. This is not a standard universal milk pasteurisation condition. Common validated milk processes include:
- Low-temperature long-time (LTLT): 63°C for 30 minutes.
- High-temperature short-time (HTST): 72°C for 15 seconds.
- Ultra-high-temperature (UHT) processing uses higher temperatures for very short times and is a different commercial process.

Actual schedules depend on food type, equipment, regulation and process validation. Do not turn these examples into home-processing instructions; the key fact is that pasteurisation uses a validated time/temperature combination and does not make food sterile.

## 3. Food irradiation versus UV
Food irradiation is an approved process using ionising radiation. It can reduce microbial load and insects and delay sprouting. UV-C is non-ionising radiation used in some air, water and surface disinfection systems; it has poor penetration through opaque food and is not the same as commercial food irradiation. Properly irradiated food does not become radioactive.

## 4. Safe student wording
- Preservation methods slow or prevent spoilage by controlling water availability, temperature, microorganisms, oxygen or other conditions.
- Each method has limits; preservation does not guarantee that food is safe if it was already contaminated or processed incorrectly.
- Follow current food-safety regulations and approved processes; no chemical additive, dose, irradiation setting or home-preservation recipe is included.

# Supplementary lesson: Artificial Vegetative Propagation (SS 1 Biology, Second Term, Week 1)

> **How to use this lesson:** This is newly written supplementary teaching for the five artificial vegetative propagation methods named in the school note. It explains those methods; it is not part of the original school note and does not change the original source wording.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. explain artificial vegetative propagation;
2. describe cutting, layering, marcotting, budding and grafting;
3. distinguish methods that use a detached plant part from methods that begin while the part is still attached to the parent;
4. distinguish budding from grafting; and
5. choose a suitable method when given a simple example.

## 1. What is artificial vegetative propagation?

**Vegetative propagation** is the production of a new plant from a vegetative part of a parent plant, such as a stem, root, leaf or bud, rather than from a seed.

**Artificial vegetative propagation** happens when people deliberately use a technique to produce a new plant from part of an existing plant.

The new plant is usually very similar genetically to the parent plant. Such a plant is often called a **clone**. This is useful when a grower wants to keep desirable features of a parent plant, such as its fruit quality or flower colour.

The school note lists five methods: **cutting, layering, marcotting, budding and grafting**. The methods are related, but they do not all work in the same way.

## 2. The five methods

### 2.1 Cutting

A **cutting** is a piece of a parent plant that is removed and encouraged to grow into a new plant. It may be a piece of stem, root or leaf, depending on the plant species.

For a common stem cutting:

1. A suitable piece of stem is separated from the parent plant.
2. The cutting is placed in a suitable moist growing medium.
3. New roots develop from the cutting.
4. A shoot continues to grow, and the cutting becomes an independent plant.

**Examples:** Cassava and sugarcane are commonly propagated from stem cuttings. Some ornamental plants, such as rose, can also be propagated this way.

**Remember:** In cutting, the piece is detached from the parent plant before it forms its new roots.

### 2.2 Layering

**Layering** is a method in which a stem is encouraged to form roots while it is still attached to the parent plant.

In simple ground layering:

1. A flexible, low branch is selected.
2. Part of the branch is bent down towards the soil.
3. A section of the branch is covered with soil, while its growing tip remains above the soil.
4. Roots develop on the covered section.
5. After the new roots have formed, the rooted section is separated from the parent and can grow as an independent plant.

**Examples:** Jasmine and bougainvillea can be propagated by layering.

**Remember:** The branch remains attached to the parent while roots are forming in the soil.

### 2.3 Marcotting (air layering)

**Marcotting**, also called **air layering** or **marcottage**, is a form of layering. It is useful when a branch cannot conveniently be bent down to the ground.

The main idea is:

1. A suitable branch remains attached to the parent plant.
2. A small section of the branch is prepared so that roots can develop there.
3. A moist rooting material is placed around that section and wrapped to keep it in place and retain moisture.
4. Roots develop at the wrapped section while it is still attached to the parent.
5. When a suitable root system has formed, the rooted branch is separated and planted as a new plant.

**Examples:** Air layering is used with some guava, citrus and litchi plants.

**Remember:** Marcotting is not a completely unrelated fifth biological process; it is a type of layering. The useful difference for this lesson is that ordinary simple layering roots a branch near or under the ground, while marcotting forms roots around a branch above the ground.

### 2.4 Budding

**Budding** is a method in which a single bud from a plant with desirable characteristics is joined to another plant, called the **stock** or **rootstock**.

The basic idea is:

1. A healthy bud is selected from the plant whose desired feature is to be retained.
2. The bud is fitted into a suitable cut in the stock.
3. The joined parts are held together while they unite.
4. If the union is successful, the bud grows and forms a shoot on the stock.

The **stock** provides the established root system. The inserted bud provides the new shoot that grows from the joined plant.

Budding is used with compatible plants, including some fruit and ornamental plants.

**Remember:** Budding uses one bud as the joining piece.

### 2.5 Grafting

**Grafting** is a method in which a piece of shoot from one plant is joined to the rooted part of another compatible plant.

The two main parts have special names:

- **Scion:** the shoot or stem piece taken from the plant with the desired features.
- **Stock (rootstock):** the rooted plant onto which the scion is joined.

The basic idea is:

1. A suitable scion and stock are selected.
2. Their prepared joining surfaces are placed together so that the living cambium regions can make close contact.
3. The union is supported while the tissues join.
4. When the graft succeeds, the stock supplies the root system and the scion grows as the upper part of the plant.

**Remember:** Grafting joins a shoot piece (the scion) to a stock. Unlike budding, the joining piece is not just one bud.

## 3. Compare the five methods

| Method | Plant part used | What happens before roots or union form? | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cutting | A detached stem, root or leaf piece | The piece is removed first and then develops into a plant | Detached piece forms a new plant |
| Layering | A branch or stem | It forms roots while still attached to the parent, usually at or under the soil | Root first, separate later |
| Marcotting | A branch or stem | It forms roots while still attached, in a moist covering above the ground | Air layering; a type of layering |
| Budding | One bud | The bud is joined to a compatible stock | One bud is the joining piece |
| Grafting | A scion (a piece of shoot) | The scion is joined to a compatible stock | A shoot piece is joined to the stock |

## 4. Important words

- **Vegetative part:** a non-seed part of a plant, such as a stem, root, leaf or bud.
- **Clone:** an organism produced asexually that is genetically very similar to the parent from which it came.
- **Stock/rootstock:** the rooted plant that receives a bud or scion.
- **Scion:** the shoot piece joined to a stock during grafting.
- **Cambium:** a thin layer of actively dividing plant tissue involved in producing new vascular tissues; close cambium contact helps a graft union form.

## 5. Common examination mistakes

1. Do not say that marcotting is the same as taking a cutting. In marcotting, the branch remains attached while roots form.
2. Do not say that layering and marcotting are entirely unrelated. Marcotting is a form of air layering.
3. Do not confuse the stock with the scion. The stock provides the root system; the scion is the shoot piece joined to it.
4. Do not describe budding as joining a whole branch. Budding uses a single bud.
5. Do not claim that every plant can be propagated successfully by every method. The method depends on the plant species and compatibility of the plant parts.

## 6. Worked identification examples

**Example A:** A farmer places a piece of cassava stem in a suitable growing medium. It develops roots and shoots.

- Method: **cutting**.
- Why: the stem piece was detached before it developed into a new plant.

**Example B:** A gardener bends a flexible jasmine branch down and covers part of it with soil. It is separated only after roots have formed.

- Method: **layering**.
- Why: the branch formed roots while it was still attached to the parent.

**Example C:** A grower wraps moist material around a section of an attached guava branch. Roots form there before the branch is removed.

- Method: **marcotting (air layering)**.
- Why: roots form on an attached branch above the ground.

**Example D:** A single bud from a desirable plant is inserted into a compatible stock.

- Method: **budding**.
- Why: one bud is the joining piece.

**Example E:** A shoot piece from a desirable plant is joined to the rooted stem of another compatible plant.

- Method: **grafting**.
- Why: a scion is joined to a stock.

## 7. Practice questions with answers and marking guide

### Question 1 — 3 marks
What is artificial vegetative propagation? State one reason why a farmer may use it.

**Model answer:** Artificial vegetative propagation is the deliberate production of a new plant from a vegetative part of an existing plant by a human-controlled method. A farmer may use it to reproduce plants with desirable features, such as good fruit quality.

**Marking:** Correct definition (2); one valid reason (1).

### Question 2 — 10 marks
Describe the five artificial vegetative propagation methods named in the school note.

**Model answer:**
- **Cutting:** a stem, root or leaf piece is detached from the parent and encouraged to develop into a new plant.
- **Layering:** a branch is made to form roots while still attached to the parent, usually by covering part of it with soil; it is separated after rooting.
- **Marcotting:** a form of air layering in which roots form around a section of an attached branch in a moist covering above the ground; the branch is separated after rooting.
- **Budding:** a single bud from a desirable plant is joined to a compatible stock and grows as a shoot.
- **Grafting:** a scion (shoot piece) from a desirable plant is joined to a compatible stock, which provides the root system.

**Marking:** Cutting correctly described (2); layering correctly described (2); marcotting correctly described (2); budding correctly described (2); grafting correctly described (2).

### Question 3 — 4 marks
Give two differences between simple layering and marcotting.

**Model answer:** In simple layering, a branch is usually bent down and part of it is covered with soil. In marcotting, the branch remains above the ground and a moist rooting material is wrapped around the selected section. Both methods allow roots to form while the branch is still attached to the parent.

**Marking:** Simple layering described (1); marcotting described (1); identifies ground-level/soil versus above-ground/wrapped rooting material (1); states that the branch remains attached until roots form in both (1).

### Question 4 — 4 marks
Distinguish between budding and grafting. Explain the terms stock and scion.

**Model answer:** Budding joins a single bud to a compatible stock. Grafting joins a shoot piece called a scion to a compatible stock. The stock is the rooted plant that receives the bud or scion. The scion is the shoot piece used in grafting.

**Marking:** Budding uses one bud (1); grafting uses a shoot piece/scion (1); stock correctly defined (1); scion correctly defined (1).

### Question 5 — 3 marks
Identify the method in each case and give a reason.

a. A piece of sugarcane stem is separated from the parent and grows into a new plant.  
b. A branch is rooted while still attached to the parent, with part of it covered by soil.  
c. A single bud is joined to a compatible rooted plant.

**Model answer:**  
a. Cutting — the stem piece is detached first.  
b. Layering — the branch forms roots while still attached and partly covered with soil.  
c. Budding — a single bud is joined to the stock.

**Marking:** Correct method and reason for (a) (1); for (b) (1); for (c) (1).

**Total: 24 marks.**

## 1. Botanical rank and group terminology
- The taxonomic hierarchy in the source should read **division (plants) / phylum (animals) → class → order → family → genus → species**, with kingdom above it in the traditional scheme. “General” is a spelling error for **genera**.
- **Binomial nomenclature** is the two-part scientific naming system (genus + specific epithet), not the hierarchy itself.
- “Graminae” should be written **Gramineae** (historical family name) or **Poaceae** (current accepted family name for grasses).
- “Enphorbinosae” appears to intend **Euphorbiaceae**.
- Thallophyta, Bryophyta and Tracheophyta are traditional school groupings. **Thallophyta** is not a natural modern plant division; the source diagram should remain unchanged as historical course material, with a separate modern-taxonomy note if approved.

## 2. Agricultural crop categories
Agricultural groupings depend on the harvested product/use, not botanical rank. Categories can overlap:
- Maize is a cereal and food crop; it is also a grass (Poaceae) and a monocot.
- Groundnut is a legume and an oil crop.
- Cassava is a root crop in common agricultural usage, though its harvested storage roots are botanically roots; yam is a tuber crop with underground storage stems.
- Tomato is botanically a fruit but is commonly classified as a vegetable crop for culinary/agricultural use.
- Carrot is a root vegetable; spinach and lettuce are leafy vegetables.
- Cocoa, coffee and tea are beverage crops; cotton is a fibre crop; rubber is a latex crop; oil palm and groundnut are oil crops.
The source's categories are practical, not mutually exclusive scientific taxa.

## 3. Life-cycle classification corrections
- **Annuals** complete their life cycle in one growing season (e.g. maize, cowpea, many tomato varieties).
- **Biennials** normally complete their life cycle over two growing seasons (e.g. carrot, beetroot, cabbage in the usual seed-production cycle).
- **Perennials** live for more than two years (e.g. mango, orange, oil palm).
Source corrections:
- Banana and plantain are **perennial herbaceous plants**, not biennials. Their pseudostems fruit once, but the plant persists through suckers/rhizomes.
- Pineapple is a **perennial** crop, even though a plant may take a long time to produce its first fruit and can produce ratoon crops.
- Yam is botanically a perennial vine grown as an annual crop in many farming systems; label the crop cycle and biological life span separately.
- “Vegetable” is not a life-cycle category: different vegetables may be annual, biennial or perennial.

## 4. Classification by size
The source's basic definitions are suitable with qualification:
- **Herbs:** usually small plants with soft, non-woody stems.
- **Shrubs:** woody plants, often with several stems branching near ground level.
- **Trees:** usually larger woody plants with a main trunk, although growth forms vary.
Examples in the source: waterleaf is an herb; hibiscus is commonly a shrub; oil palm is a tree (a palm, not a broad-leaved dicot tree).

## 5. Effects of agricultural practices
| Practice | Evidence-based correction / qualification |
|---|---|
| Bush burning | Can destroy soil organic matter and organisms, release smoke/greenhouse gases, expose soil to erosion and reduce habitat. Ash may temporarily raise soil pH or add mineral nutrients, but this does not make repeated burning sustainable. |
| Overgrazing | Occurs when grazing pressure exceeds pasture recovery/carrying capacity; removes vegetation, compacts soil, increases runoff/erosion and reduces regeneration. Manure can add nutrients, but does not cancel the damage from severe overgrazing. |
| Tillage | Can prepare a seedbed and improve short-term aeration, but intensive/repeated tillage can break soil aggregates, accelerate erosion, reduce soil organic matter and disrupt soil organisms. |
| Deforestation | Can reduce habitat and biodiversity, increase erosion/runoff and alter local water and carbon cycles. Do not state that every deforestation event causes a fixed rainfall reduction everywhere. |
| Fertilizer application | Supplies nutrients and can increase yields when matched to crop/soil need. Excess or poorly timed use can cause nutrient runoff/eutrophication, leaching, soil acidification or salinity. It does not automatically reduce humus or destroy soil structure. |
| Pesticides/herbicides | Can reduce target pests/weeds but may harm non-target organisms, contaminate soil/water, leave residues and select for resistance. Use integrated pest management and only legally registered products under trained, label-compliant supervision. No pesticide doses or application instructions belong in this student supplement. |

The source's “most ecologically friendly practice” question has no single universal answer without specifying the alternatives and context. Crop rotation, cover crops, reduced tillage, agroforestry and integrated pest management can all reduce impacts when appropriately designed.

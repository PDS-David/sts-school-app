## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. explain what a cycle in nature is;
2. describe how carbon moves between the atmosphere, plants, animals, decomposers, fuels and the sea;
3. explain the roles of photosynthesis, respiration, decay and combustion in the carbon cycle;
4. describe the water cycle, including plant uptake, transpiration, runoff, infiltration, groundwater and return to the atmosphere;
5. state why water is important to plants and animals.

## 1. What is nutrient cycling?

A **cycle** is a sequence of processes in which a substance moves between different parts of the environment and is used again. Matter is not simply used once and lost: atoms are transferred between living organisms and non-living parts of the environment.

This Week 6 lesson explains two cycles:
- the **carbon cycle**, which moves carbon through air, living things, water and long-term stores;
- the **water cycle**, which moves water between the atmosphere, land, water bodies and living things.

The school note mentions sulphur in its introductory definition, but does not teach a sulphur cycle in this week. Nitrogen is not part of the Week 6 scheme entry or teaching text. Neither is added to this lesson.

## 2. The carbon cycle

Carbon is part of carbohydrates, fats, proteins and other organic substances in living things. In the atmosphere, it is found mainly as carbon dioxide (CO₂). Plants and algae take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and use the carbon to make organic substances.

### 2.1 The main stages

Follow this sequence:

1. **Photosynthesis — atmosphere to plants.** Green plants take in carbon dioxide from the air through their leaves. Using light energy, they make sugars and other organic substances. Carbon becomes part of plant tissue.
2. **Feeding — plants to animals.** When an animal eats a plant, carbon in the plant's food passes into the animal's body. When one animal eats another, carbon can pass along the food chain.
3. **Respiration — organisms to atmosphere.** Plants, animals and decomposers respire. During aerobic respiration, carbon from food is released as carbon dioxide, which returns to the air or water.
4. **Death and decay (putrefaction) — dead material to decomposers and the environment.** Decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms and wastes. They respire and release carbon dioxide. Some carbon remains in soil or sediments for longer periods.
5. **Combustion — fuels to atmosphere.** Burning wood and fossil fuels such as coal, petrol and kerosene releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.
6. **Exchange with the sea — atmosphere ↔ sea.** Carbon dioxide moves between the atmosphere and seawater. The sea can absorb carbon dioxide from the air and can also release dissolved carbon dioxide back to the atmosphere.

### 2.2 Carbon cycle flow map

Read each arrow in the direction shown:

- **Atmospheric CO₂ → green plants** (photosynthesis)
- **Green plants → animals** (feeding)
- **Plants, animals and decomposers → CO₂ in air or water** (respiration and decay)
- **Dead organic matter → long-term carbon stores / fossil fuels → atmospheric CO₂** (burial over long periods, then combustion when fuels are burned)
- **Atmosphere ↔ sea** (dissolving into seawater and release back to the air)

**Important correction for learning:** the original school diagram contains a central arrow that appears to point from animals to plants. That direction conflicts with the accompanying written explanation, which says plants are eaten by animals. The correct feeding direction is **plants → animals**. The prose also describes exchange between the sea and atmosphere; this lesson shows that as a two-way exchange rather than inventing an unexplained sea-to-plant pathway.

### 2.3 Why is the carbon cycle important?

- Carbon is a component of the organic substances that make up living things.
- Carbon passes through food chains when organisms feed.
- Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the air and uses its carbon to build organic substances.
- Respiration, decay and combustion return carbon dioxide to the environment.
- Cycling carbon helps maintain the movement of carbon between organisms, the atmosphere, soil and water.

## 3. The water cycle

The **water cycle** is the continuous movement of water between the atmosphere, land, water bodies and living organisms. The Sun supplies energy for evaporation, and gravity helps water fall as precipitation and flow downhill.

### 3.1 The main stages

1. **Evaporation:** heat from the Sun changes liquid water in oceans, lakes, rivers and wet soil into water vapour.
2. **Transpiration:** plants absorb water through their roots. Some travels through the plant and leaves as water vapour, mainly through tiny openings called stomata. This loss of water vapour from plants is called transpiration.
3. **Condensation:** water vapour cools and changes into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals, forming clouds.
4. **Precipitation:** water falls from clouds as rain (or, in colder conditions, snow or hail).
5. **Runoff:** some rainwater flows over the land into streams, rivers, lakes and eventually the sea.
6. **Infiltration and percolation:** some water enters the soil and moves downwards. It may become groundwater stored in spaces and cracks in soil and rock.
7. **Groundwater flow:** groundwater moves slowly and may feed springs, rivers or the sea. It can also be taken up by plant roots.
8. **Return to the atmosphere:** evaporation from water and land, together with transpiration from plants, returns water vapour to the atmosphere. The cycle continues.

### 3.2 Water cycle flow map

**Water bodies and wet land → evaporation → water vapour → condensation → clouds → precipitation → land and water bodies.**

After precipitation, water can follow more than one route:
- **surface route:** rain → runoff → streams/rivers/lakes → sea → evaporation;
- **underground route:** rain → infiltration/percolation → groundwater → springs/rivers/sea → evaporation;
- **plant route:** soil water → root hairs → plant → transpiration → atmosphere;
- **animal route:** drinking/food → animal body → water released through excretion and breathing → environment.

These routes occur at the same time. Not every drop of rain follows every route.

## 4. Why water is important to living things

### 4.1 Importance to plants

Water:
- is needed for photosynthesis;
- dissolves mineral nutrients so roots can absorb them;
- helps seeds germinate;
- keeps many non-woody plant tissues firm (turgid), helping support the plant;
- helps cool the plant when water evaporates during transpiration;
- provides a medium in which many chemical reactions occur.

### 4.2 Importance to animals

Water:
- forms a large part of the body and blood;
- dissolves substances during digestion;
- transports nutrients and other substances around the body;
- helps remove metabolic wastes in urine and other excretions;
- helps regulate body temperature;
- provides a medium for many chemical reactions in cells.

## 5. Worked questions

**Example 1:** A plant takes in carbon dioxide and uses it to make sugar. Which carbon-cycle process is taking place?

**Answer:** Photosynthesis. It transfers carbon from atmospheric carbon dioxide into organic substances in the plant.

**Example 2:** A dead leaf is broken down by bacteria. Which carbon-cycle process is involved?

**Answer:** Decay (putrefaction). Decomposer organisms break down the dead material; their respiration releases carbon dioxide.

**Example 3:** Rainwater enters the soil and moves down to a water-bearing layer underground. Name the processes.

**Answer:** Infiltration is the entry of water into the soil; percolation is its downward movement through soil and rock. The stored water is groundwater.

**Example 4:** A student writes that “transportation” is the loss of water vapour from leaves. What is the correct biological term?

**Answer:** Transpiration. Transportation means carrying substances from one place to another; transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, especially through stomata.

## Key points to remember

- Photosynthesis moves carbon from atmospheric carbon dioxide into plants.
- Feeding transfers carbon from plants to animals.
- Respiration, decay and combustion return carbon dioxide to the environment.
- Carbon dioxide moves in both directions between the atmosphere and the sea.
- Evaporation and transpiration return water vapour to the atmosphere.
- Condensation forms clouds; precipitation returns water to Earth's surface.
- Some rainwater runs over land; some infiltrates and becomes groundwater.
- The source's “transportation” is retained as historical source wording; the correct plant process is **transpiration**.

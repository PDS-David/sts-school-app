# Supplementary lesson: The carbon and water cycles (SS 1 Biology, First Term, Week 6)

> **Scope:** The school source's Week 6 body teaches the carbon and water cycles. It names sulphur in the opening definition but does not teach a sulphur cycle; it does not teach a nitrogen cycle. This supplement covers only the two cycles actually taught. The original source text and diagrams remain unchanged.

## Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. explain what a nutrient/biogeochemical cycle is;
2. describe the main stages of the carbon cycle;
3. describe the main stages of the water cycle;
4. explain why both cycles are important to living things and ecosystems.

## 1. What is a cycle in nature?
A **biogeochemical cycle** is the movement and reuse of a substance or chemical element between living organisms and the non-living environment. Cycles help keep essential materials available for life.

The Week 6 source teaches two cycles: carbon and water.

## 2. The carbon cycle
Carbon is a major part of carbohydrates, fats, proteins and nucleic acids. Carbon moves between the atmosphere, living organisms, oceans, soils and rocks.

### Main stages of the carbon cycle

1. **Photosynthesis:** green plants, algae and some bacteria absorb carbon dioxide (CO₂) and use light energy to make organic molecules. Carbon becomes part of plant biomass.
2. **Feeding:** animals obtain carbon when they eat plants or other animals. Carbon moves through food chains and food webs.
3. **Respiration:** plants, animals and microorganisms break down organic food and release carbon dioxide back into the environment.
4. **Decomposition (decay/putrefaction):** decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms and wastes. Their respiration releases carbon dioxide; some carbon also enters soil organic matter.
5. **Combustion:** burning wood and fossil fuels transfers stored carbon to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
6. **Ocean exchange:** carbon dioxide moves between the atmosphere and ocean. The ocean can absorb CO₂ from the air and can also release CO₂. Marine organisms use dissolved carbon, and some carbon becomes stored in ocean sediments over long periods.

### Carbon-cycle flow sequence

**Atmospheric CO₂ → photosynthesis → plants → feeding → animals → respiration → CO₂**

**Dead plants/animals and wastes → decomposition → soil carbon + CO₂**

**Wood/fossil fuels → combustion → atmospheric CO₂**

**Atmospheric CO₂ ⇄ ocean dissolved carbon**

The arrows show transfers, not a single one-way path. Carbon can be stored in biomass, soils, oceans and fossil fuels for different lengths of time.

**Important correction:** The source says photosynthesis is the only process that removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, but it also describes exchange with the sea. Ocean uptake also removes CO₂ from the atmosphere. In the long-term biological carbon cycle, photosynthesis is the main biological process that takes CO₂ into organic matter.

## 3. Importance of the carbon cycle
- Carbon is needed to build carbohydrates, proteins, fats and nucleic acids.
- It makes carbon available to plants and then to animals through feeding.
- Respiration and decomposition return carbon to the environment for reuse.
- Carbon movement helps regulate the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere and ocean.
- Carbon storage in soils, oceans and rocks affects the climate over different timescales.

## 4. The water cycle
The **water cycle** is the continuous movement of water between the Earth's surface, underground, living organisms and the atmosphere.

### Main stages of the water cycle

1. **Evaporation:** heat from the Sun changes liquid water in oceans, lakes, rivers and soil into water vapour.
2. **Transpiration:** plants absorb water through roots and release water vapour mainly through stomata in their leaves. Evaporation and transpiration together are called **evapotranspiration**.
3. **Condensation:** water vapour cools and forms tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals, creating clouds.
4. **Precipitation:** water falls from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
5. **Infiltration and percolation:** some rainwater enters the soil and moves downwards to recharge groundwater.
6. **Runoff:** water flows over the land into streams, rivers, lakes and oceans.
7. **Groundwater flow:** underground water moves slowly through soil and rocks and may eventually enter rivers, springs or the sea.
8. **Storage:** water may be stored for a time in oceans, lakes, rivers, soil, groundwater, glaciers, ice and living organisms.

### Water-cycle flow sequence

**Sun's energy → evaporation from water bodies + transpiration from plants → water vapour → condensation → clouds → precipitation →**

- **runoff → streams/rivers → lakes/ocean**, or
- **infiltration → groundwater → springs/rivers/ocean**, or
- **soil water → plant roots → transpiration**.

Water returns to the atmosphere and the cycle continues.

**Important correction:** The source uses “transportation” where it means **transpiration**. Transpiration is the release of water vapour from plants, mainly through leaf stomata. The source's written explanation also describes underground water, so the groundwater route is included in this flow sequence even though the original water-cycle sketch does not show it clearly.

## 5. Importance of water

### Importance to plants
- Water is a raw material for photosynthesis.
- It dissolves mineral nutrients and helps transport them through the plant.
- It helps maintain cell turgidity, which supports non-woody plant tissues.
- It is needed for seed germination and many metabolic reactions.
- Transpiration helps cool leaves and contributes to water movement through the plant.

### Importance to animals
- Water forms a large part of body fluids and cells.
- It acts as a solvent and medium for many biochemical reactions.
- It transports nutrients, gases and waste products.
- It helps regulate body temperature.
- It supports digestion and excretion.

## 6. Worked examples
**Example 1:** A plant absorbs water through its roots and releases water vapour from its leaves. What is this release called?  
**Answer:** Transpiration.

**Example 2:** A dead leaf is broken down by bacteria and fungi. Which carbon-cycle process is taking place?  
**Answer:** Decomposition. The decomposers use organic matter and release carbon dioxide through respiration.

**Example 3:** Rain enters the soil and moves down to underground water stores. Name the processes.  
**Answer:** Infiltration (water enters the soil) and percolation (water moves down through soil and rock).

## Key points to remember
- Carbon moves through photosynthesis, feeding, respiration, decomposition, combustion and ocean exchange.
- Water moves through evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, runoff and groundwater flow.
- Decomposers return carbon from dead material to the environment.
- Transpiration is the correct term for water vapour released by plants.
- The Week 6 source does not teach a complete sulphur or nitrogen cycle; these are not added here.

## 1. Historical statement: Leeuwenhoek
The source says Leeuwenhoek was “the first person to discover microbes”. This is too broad:
- Robert Hooke published an earlier microscopic depiction of the fungus *Mucor* in *Micrographia* (1665).
- Antonie van Leeuwenhoek later used powerful single-lens microscopes to observe and describe living microscopic organisms, including protozoa and bacteria, in the 1670s and 1680s.
- A safe wording is: **“Leeuwenhoek was a pioneer of microbiology and among the first to observe and describe living bacteria and protozoa using microscopes.”** Do not erase Hooke's earlier contribution.

## 2. Groups and characteristics
- **Bacteria:** cellular, usually unicellular prokaryotes; they lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Bacterial cell walls commonly contain peptidoglycan, but not every species has the same envelope structure. Some bacteria have flagella; not all do.
- **Viruses:** acellular infectious agents containing DNA or RNA within a protein coat (some have an envelope). They do not have their own cellular metabolism and replicate only inside suitable host cells. They are studied in microbiology but are not cellular living organisms in the same sense as bacteria, fungi and protists.
- **Protists:** a diverse group of eukaryotes; many are microscopic and unicellular. The source's “single-celled animals” is an outdated oversimplification.
- **Fungi:** eukaryotes; yeasts are unicellular, while moulds and mushrooms are multicellular. Many fungi absorb nutrients from organic material; not all fungi are microscopic.
- **Algae:** a diverse photosynthetic group; many are microscopic, but some are large multicellular seaweeds. “All algae are microorganisms” is incorrect.
- **Cyanobacteria:** photosynthetic bacteria, not eukaryotic algae.

## 3. Bacterial shape terminology
| Source wording | Correct form / finding |
|---|---|
| “Streplococci” | **Streptococci**: spherical cocci arranged in chains. |
| “Staphylococci” | Cocci arranged in irregular clusters. |
| “Diplococci” | Cocci occurring in pairs. |
| “Bacilli” | Rod-shaped bacteria. A rod shape does not mean every bacillus causes typhoid. |
| “Spirilla” | Rigid spiral/helical bacteria; not simply “rod-shaped bacteria twisted into a spiral”. |
| “Spirochaetes” | Flexible, slender helical bacteria. |
| “Vibrios” | Curved/comma-shaped bacteria. |

Specific organism corrections:
- Syphilis is caused by **Treponema pallidum** (not “Treponema pallidum” with joined words).
- Cholera is caused by toxigenic **Vibrio cholerae** (not “Vibro cholerae” or “Vibro-cholerea”).
- Typhoid is caused by **Salmonella enterica** serovar Typhi; it should not be attributed to all rod-shaped bacilli.
- “Pneumococci” refers to *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, not a separate general shape group.

## 4. Air and airborne microorganisms
The source states that microorganisms are not capable of growing in air. This needs qualification:
- Air is usually a transport medium, not a nutrient-rich growth medium. Microorganisms generally need suitable moisture, nutrients and environmental conditions to multiply.
- Bacteria, fungi, viruses and spores can be carried in aerosols or dust. Some remain viable while airborne; others are damaged by drying, sunlight/UV, temperature or humidity.
- Microorganisms can settle onto surfaces and be carried again in dust or droplets.
- Exposing nutrient agar to air may collect some viable organisms that settle onto the medium. It does not represent every organism in the air, and viruses cannot form colonies on ordinary nutrient agar because they require living host cells.
- This fact review does not author a classroom air-culturing experiment. Unknown environmental cultures should not be opened or handled by students; any practical requires a separate approved safety plan.

## 5. Normal microbiota and antibiotics
The source says normal microbes live in the body without harm but can become dangerous when body resistance is low or normal microflora are “de-established” by antibiotics. A more accurate statement is:
- The **normal microbiota** are microorganisms that normally inhabit body sites. Many are beneficial or harmless under ordinary conditions.
- Some may cause opportunistic infection if they enter a normally sterile body site, if host defences are weakened, or if the normal microbial community is disrupted.
- Antibiotics can disrupt normal microbiota and select for resistant organisms, but antibiotics are not the only cause of microbiome disruption and do not automatically cause infection.

## 6. Vector and disease table
- **Female Anopheles mosquito → Plasmodium → malaria:** correct in principle. The mosquito is the vector; *Plasmodium* is the parasite.
- **Tsetse fly → Trypanosoma brucei → African sleeping sickness:** correct. The spelling should be *Trypanosoma brucei*; the vector is the tsetse fly.
- **Housefly → Vibrio cholerae → cholera and typhoid:** misleading. Houseflies may mechanically carry pathogens on their bodies and contaminate food, but they are not the biological vector for cholera or typhoid. Cholera is caused by *Vibrio cholerae* and spreads mainly through faecally contaminated water/food. Typhoid is caused by *Salmonella Typhi* and spreads through food/water contaminated by faeces of an infected person or carrier. A housefly is a possible mechanical carrier, not the pathogen's biological vector.
- **Aedes mosquito → yellow fever virus → yellow fever:** correct at broad level. Aedes mosquitoes transmit yellow fever virus; the particular vector species depends on the transmission cycle and region.

## 1. Association definitions

| Association | Effect on species A / B | Accurate definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Mutualism** | + / + | Both species benefit. The interaction may be obligatory or facultative depending on the species. |
| **Protocooperation** | + / + | Older school term for a mutually beneficial but non-obligatory interaction; both species can survive separately. Often treated as facultative mutualism in modern ecology. |
| **Commensalism** | + / 0 | One species benefits; the other is not significantly helped or harmed. |
| **Amensalism** | - / 0 | One species is harmed or inhibited; the other is not significantly affected. |
| **Parasitism** | + / - | A parasite benefits while the host is harmed; the parasite usually lives in or on the host and obtains resources from it. |
| **Predation** | + / - | A predator captures, kills and eats prey. Unlike typical parasitism, the prey is killed during the feeding interaction. |

**Terminology correction:** “Symbiosis” is used in some school texts as a synonym for mutualism, but modern biology commonly uses symbiosis more broadly for a close, persistent association between different species, which may be mutualistic, commensal or parasitic. Teach “mutualism” for the +/+ relationship and define symbiosis separately.

## 2. Source examples reviewed
- **Lichen (fungus + photosynthetic partner):** a sound example of mutualism. The photosynthetic partner supplies carbohydrates; the fungal partner provides structure, water/mineral retention and protection. The photosynthetic partner may be a green alga or cyanobacterium.
- **Protozoa and bacteria in termite gut:** a recognised mutualistic relationship in many wood-feeding termites; microbial symbionts help digest cellulose and receive habitat/food. Do not imply every termite has the same protozoan species.
- **Rhizobium and legume roots:** mutualism; bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia/ammonium compounds that the plant can use, while the plant supplies carbohydrates and a protected nodule environment. “Directly fixes nitrogen into the plant” is an oversimplification.
- **Rumen microbes and ruminants:** mutualism; microbial communities ferment plant fibre and provide fermentation products to the host, while receiving food and a stable habitat. Not every rumen microbe performs the same function.
- **Flowering plants and pollinators:** often mutualistic; pollinators obtain nectar/pollen and transfer pollen. Not every flower visitor is an effective pollinator.
- **Sea anemone and hermit crab; cattle and egret:** may be examples of facultative mutualism/protocooperation, but the strength of benefit depends on the species and context. Do not present every textbook example as universally obligatory.
- **Plover/crocodile “tooth cleaning”:** the popular story is not a dependable general example; omit it from a student answer key unless the teacher provides a reliable species-specific source.
- **Shark and remora:** commonly used as a commensalism example (remora gains transport/food scraps; shark is generally described as little affected). Real interactions can vary.
- **Oyster and crab:** the source's example is insufficiently specific to verify a general commensal relationship; replace with a clearer, teacher-approved example in supplemental teaching.
- **Humans and intestinal bacteria:** not a clean example of commensalism. Many gut microbes benefit the host through digestion, vitamin production and colonisation resistance, so the relationship is often mutualistic.
- **Penicillium and bacteria:** an amensal/antibiosis example in which a fungus produces an antibiotic that inhibits some bacteria. Historical source name *Penicillium notatum* is commonly associated with penicillin discovery; modern penicillin-producing strains are classified differently. Do not claim all Penicillium species produce penicillin.
- **Streptomyces griseus and bacteria:** a valid antibiotic-producing actinomycete example; it is known for streptomycin production. Keep the specific product clear.
- **Tapeworm and human; mistletoe and host plant:** parasitism examples, with the host harmed.
- **Hawk and chick; lion and goat:** predation examples.

## 3. Weekend assignment questions
- **Q1:** “A ____ relationship is one in which one member of the association benefits.” The options are parasitic, symbiotic, saprophytic and mutualistic. The wording is insufficient: both parasitism and commensalism involve one partner benefiting (but commensalism is omitted), and “symbiotic” is used inconsistently. **Do not publish a single answer key without revising the question.** A corrected item should ask specifically for “one organism benefits while the host is harmed” (parasitism) or “one benefits and the other is unaffected” (commensalism).
- **Q2:** Tick feeds on **blood**. A tick is an ectoparasite that feeds on host blood.
- **Q3:** “Commensal” is historically derived from the idea of “sharing a table/meal”; among the options, “sharing” is the intended answer, but it is not a full scientific definition.
- **Q4:** “Allochemics” is an older spelling/term; the modern term for chemicals used in interactions between different species is **allelochemicals**. Penicillin is an antibiotic, a specific antimicrobial substance.
- **Q5:** Lichen consists of a fungus and a photosynthetic partner (alga or cyanobacterium); answer **lichen**.

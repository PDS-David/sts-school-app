## Findings

### 1. “Consumers have better chance of survival than any other biotic group”
This is **not a scientifically valid general rule**. Consumers may feed on several food sources in a food web, which can provide alternative feeding pathways, but producers and decomposers also have essential roles and their survival depends on the habitat and resources. A producer population may persist where light, water and nutrients are available; decomposers obtain energy from dead organic matter. There is no universal ranking that consumers have the “best survival chance”. Remove or qualify this claim in supplemental teaching.

### 2. First law of thermodynamics
The first law states that energy is conserved: it cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be transferred or transformed from one form to another. In an ecosystem, sunlight is converted to chemical energy by photosynthetic producers; chemical energy is transferred through feeding relationships and transformed during metabolism. Energy does not disappear, but it can be dispersed as heat and become less available to do useful work.

### 3. Second law of thermodynamics and energy pyramids
Every energy transfer or transformation increases the dispersal of energy and reduces the amount available to do useful work. Organisms use chemical energy for metabolism; much is released as heat. Therefore, energy available generally decreases at successive trophic levels. A pyramid of energy represents energy available per unit area per unit time at each trophic level and is upright.

Do not say that energy is “destroyed” or “lost from the universe”; it is transferred and dispersed, often as heat to the surroundings.

### 4. “Usable form of energy in animals” — ATP
The best answer in the source's multiple-choice options is **ATP (adenosine triphosphate)**. ATP is the cell's immediate energy-transfer currency: hydrolysis of ATP can be coupled to cellular processes that require energy. ATP is not the original source of energy; energy from food is used in cellular respiration to regenerate ATP.

### 5. “1–10% of solar energy available to photosynthetic plants”
The source's option D (1–10%) is evidently the expected classroom approximation, but it should not be taught as a fixed universal percentage or as a law. The fraction of incoming solar radiation captured and stored by producers varies with ecosystem, season, latitude, vegetation, water availability and other factors. A student supplement should say that only a small fraction of incoming solar energy is captured as chemical energy by photosynthesis, and that the exact percentage varies.

## Safe answer-key guidance
- Consumers “better chance of survival”: no universally correct scientific answer; source statement is an overgeneralisation.
- First law: energy cannot be created or destroyed; it is transferred/transformed.
- Second law: energy transformations are not 100% efficient; useful energy becomes dispersed, often as heat.
- ATP: best answer for the immediate usable energy-transfer molecule in animal cells.
- Solar energy: select 1–10% only if the teacher retains the source's simplified expected option; annotate it as a rough, variable approximation, not a universal constant.

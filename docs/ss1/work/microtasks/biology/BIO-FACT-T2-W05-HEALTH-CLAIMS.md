### Table-level corrections
- The source omits B5, B6, folate and biotin from its nine-row table even though these are recognised vitamins. This report checks the nine rows actually printed; it does not silently expand or rewrite the source table.
- The source's Vitamin B12 row says “pernicious anaemia”. Use “megaloblastic anaemia and possible neurological effects” as the broad B12-deficiency description; reserve “pernicious anaemia” for the specific intrinsic-factor-related condition.
- The Vitamin E infertility/sterility and abortion statement is not a reliable general human deficiency description. Use the rare-deficiency qualification above.
- The Vitamin C claim “helps resist infection” is too broad for a keyed disease-prevention claim; the source's “prevents flu” wording is not supported as a guaranteed outcome.
- A symptom does not by itself prove a vitamin deficiency. Diagnosis requires appropriate clinical assessment.

## 2. Protein-deficiency paragraph (source block 426)
The source associates protein deficiency with retarded growth, weight loss and oedema (swollen legs). The association with severe malnutrition is recognised, but the simplified statement that kwashiorkor is simply caused by a lack of protein is outdated. Oedematous severe acute malnutrition is multifactorial and must be assessed clinically; oedema is an important sign, not a stand-alone diagnosis. In a student supplement, say that inadequate protein and energy intake, illness and other factors can contribute to malnutrition, and that kwashiorkor is an oedematous form of severe acute malnutrition. Do not teach the source's “cracked/split stomach” as a diagnostic feature.

## 3. Mineral-deficiency revision questions (source blocks 461–462)
- **Sodium:** severe low blood sodium (hyponatraemia) can cause nausea, headache, confusion, seizures or coma. It is not safe to teach a single simple deficiency disease from the source's question; low sodium may result from water balance disorders, illness or medicines and is not simply corrected by eating salt.
- **Calcium:** inadequate calcium over time can impair bone mineralisation and contribute to weak bones; in children, rickets usually involves vitamin D deficiency and may also involve inadequate calcium/phosphate. In adults, poor mineralisation can cause osteomalacia. Do not key “osteoporosis” as a direct one-cause calcium-deficiency disease.
- **Iron:** iron deficiency can cause iron-deficiency anaemia, with tiredness, weakness and reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. The source question's answer should be “iron-deficiency anaemia”.

## 4. Safe answer-key guidance
- Vitamin A — night blindness/xerophthalmia.
- Vitamin B1 — beriberi.
- Vitamin B2 — angular stomatitis/cheilosis, glossitis or dermatitis.
- Vitamin B3 — pellagra.
- Vitamin B12 — megaloblastic anaemia and neurological effects; pernicious anaemia is a specific cause.
- Vitamin C — scurvy.
- Vitamin D — rickets (children) / osteomalacia (adults).
- Vitamin E — rare deficiency; neurological dysfunction/haemolytic anaemia in susceptible cases; do not use infertility as a general key.
- Vitamin K — impaired clotting/bleeding.
- Mineral questions: sodium — hyponatraemia (qualified, not a simple dietary disease); calcium — impaired bone mineralisation/rickets or osteomalacia depending on context; iron — iron-deficiency anaemia.

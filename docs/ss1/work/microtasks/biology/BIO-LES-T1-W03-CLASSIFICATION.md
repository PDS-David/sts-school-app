# Supplementary lesson: Classification of Living Things (SS 1 Biology, First Term, Week 3)

> **Source note:** This is a newly written student lesson based on the school's Week 3 classification material. It is a supplement, not a replacement or verbatim copy of the original note. The source's spelling and layout defects are recorded in the source evidence; this lesson uses corrected scientific spelling where needed.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. explain classification and taxonomy;
2. arrange the main taxonomic ranks from the broadest to the most specific;
3. describe the traditional five-kingdom grouping;
4. outline the main groups used in the school's plant classification;
5. distinguish monocotyledons from dicotyledons;
6. classify plants by agricultural use and life span;
7. distinguish invertebrates from vertebrates and recognise major animal groups.

## 1. What is biological classification?

Living things are extremely varied. **Classification** is the systematic grouping of organisms according to shared features. It makes organisms easier to identify, compare and study.

**Taxonomy** is the branch of biology concerned with identifying, naming and classifying organisms. The school source introduces the system associated with the Swedish naturalist **Carolus Linnaeus**.

A classification group at any rank is called a **taxon** (plural: taxa). Classification is hierarchical: a large group contains smaller groups, and each smaller group contains organisms with more features in common.

## 2. The taxonomic hierarchy

Learn this order from the broadest group to the most specific:

**Kingdom → Phylum (animals) / Division (plants) → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species**

A useful memory aid is: **King Philip Came Over For Good Soup** (Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species).

- A **kingdom** contains several phyla or divisions.
- A **phylum** (or **division** in traditional plant classification) contains related classes.
- A **class** contains related orders.
- An **order** contains related families.
- A **family** contains related genera.
- A **genus** contains closely related species.
- A **species** is commonly defined at this level as a group of organisms that can naturally interbreed and produce fertile offspring. This definition has limits for organisms that reproduce asexually or do not interbreed, but it is useful for many familiar animals and plants.

**Important spelling:** the correct word is *Genus*. The original school diagram prints “Genius”; that is a spelling error, not a different taxonomic rank.

### Worked example: moving down the hierarchy

Suppose you are given the ranks in this mixed order: Species, Family, Kingdom, Genus. Put them in order from broadest to most specific:

1. Kingdom
2. Family
3. Genus
4. Species

The complete sequence includes the ranks between these four: Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.

Modern biological classification often includes a level above kingdom called **domain**. The traditional school sequence above begins at kingdom because that is the sequence taught in the source. Modern systems also revise group boundaries as new evidence, including DNA evidence, becomes available.

## 3. The traditional five kingdoms

The school note presents a traditional five-kingdom framework. It is useful for learning the broad differences below, but remember that modern classification has developed further and does not treat every old grouping as final.

| Kingdom | Main features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Monera (traditional grouping) | Prokaryotic organisms: cells do not have a membrane-bound nucleus. Many are single-celled. | Bacteria |
| Protoctista (traditional school term) | A varied group of mostly simple eukaryotic organisms; many are single-celled and live in water or damp places. | Amoeba, Euglena, some algae |
| Fungi | Eukaryotic organisms that obtain nutrients by absorption; they do not photosynthesise. Many grow as threads called hyphae, which together form a mycelium. | Mushrooms, bread mould, yeast |
| Plantae | Mostly multicellular organisms that photosynthesise; plant cells generally have cellulose cell walls. | Mosses, ferns, flowering plants |
| Animalia | Multicellular organisms that obtain food by eating or taking in other organisms; animal cells do not have cell walls. | Insects, fish, birds, mammals |

Do not memorise outdated claims as universal rules. For example, the source says all organisms in some of these groups reproduce in one particular way; in reality, reproductive methods vary. The table gives broad introductory features, not every exception.

## 4. Classification of plants

The school source uses a traditional plant-grouping framework. Some names are historical teaching categories; modern plant systematics may arrange these groups differently.

### 4.1 Main plant groups in the school framework

| Group | Main identifying features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Thallophyta (traditional grouping) | Simple plant-like organisms, especially algae; the body is not differentiated into true roots, stems and leaves. Many live in water. | Spirogyra and other algae |
| Bryophyta | Small, non-vascular plants. They lack true roots, stems and leaves and are commonly found in moist places. They reproduce by spores. | Mosses and liverworts |
| Pteridophyta | Vascular plants with true roots, stems and leaves; they reproduce by spores rather than seeds. | Ferns |
| Spermatophyta (seed plants) | Plants that reproduce by seeds and have vascular tissues. | Conifers and flowering plants |

The school note divides seed plants into two groups:

- **Gymnosperms:** their seeds are not enclosed inside an ovary that develops into a fruit. Many produce cones; examples include pines and other conifers.
- **Angiosperms:** flowering plants whose seeds develop inside an ovary; the ovary usually develops into a fruit after fertilisation.

### 4.2 Monocotyledons and dicotyledons

A **cotyledon** is a seed leaf in the plant embryo. The number of cotyledons is the key distinction in this school comparison.

| Feature | Monocotyledon (monocot) | Dicotyledon / eudicot (traditional school term: dicot) |
|---|---|---|
| Cotyledons | One | Two |
| Leaf veins | Usually parallel | Usually form a branching network |
| Roots | Usually fibrous or adventitious | Usually a main taproot with lateral roots |
| Stem vascular bundles | Usually scattered through the stem | Usually arranged in a ring |
| Flower parts | Often in threes or multiples of three | Often in fours or fives, or their multiples |
| Examples from the school note | Maize, grasses, palm | Mango, orange |

The source also compares flower colour and cortex width. These are not dependable general rules for identifying every monocot or eudicot, so do not use them as your main identification tests. The features in the table above are more useful, but even these are typical patterns rather than exception-free rules.

**Worked identification:** A young plant has one cotyledon, parallel leaf veins and a fibrous root system. These features strongly suggest that it is a monocot. State at least two observations to support your answer.

## 5. Classifying plants by agricultural use

Farmers and biologists may group plants by the product for which they are grown. This is a practical grouping, not the same as botanical taxonomy.

| Agricultural group | What the crop is grown for | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Cereals or grain crops | Edible grains, commonly rich in starch | Maize, millet, wheat, rice |
| Legumes or pod crops | Seeds or pods; many are important sources of plant protein | Beans, groundnut, cowpea |
| Root and tuber crops | Edible storage roots or stems | Cassava, yam, sweet potato |
| Vegetables | Edible leaves, stems, flowers or fruits used as vegetables | Okra, cabbage, tomato, pepper |
| Fruit crops | Edible fruits | Mango, pawpaw, guava, orange |
| Oil crops | Oil from seeds or fruits | Oil palm, groundnut, coconut |
| Fibre crops | Fibres used for textiles, ropes or sacks | Cotton |
| Beverage crops | Products used to prepare drinks | Cocoa, coffee, tea |
| Spices | Parts of plants used to add flavour | Ginger, cloves, nutmeg |

A crop can fit more than one practical category depending on which part is harvested and how it is used. For example, groundnut is a legume and is also grown for its oil.

## 6. Classifying plants by life span

- **Annual plants** complete their life cycle in one growing season, then die. Examples include maize and many beans.
- **Biennial plants** normally complete their life cycle over two growing seasons. They commonly grow leaves and store food in the first season, then flower and produce seeds in the second. Carrot is a familiar example.
- **Perennial plants** live for more than two years. Examples include mango trees and many shrubs.

Do not confuse a plant's life span with how long a farmer chooses to cultivate it.

## 7. Classification of animals

The school source divides the animal kingdom into two broad groups:

- **Invertebrates** do not have a backbone.
- **Vertebrates** have a backbone or vertebral column.

### 7.1 Features used to compare animal body plans

**Body symmetry** describes how body parts are arranged around an axis or plane.

- **Radial symmetry:** body parts are arranged around a central axis. More than one plane through the axis can divide the body into similar halves. Example: a sea anemone.
- **Bilateral symmetry:** one main plane divides the body into left and right halves that are broadly mirror images. Examples: an earthworm, fish and human.

**Body design** in the source refers to the digestive cavity:
- A **sac-like digestive cavity** has one opening that serves as the mouth and also as the exit for undigested material.
- A **tube-like digestive tract** has two openings: a mouth and an anus.

**Germ layers** are layers of cells formed during early animal development:
- **Diploblastic** animals develop from two main germ layers: ectoderm and endoderm.
- **Triploblastic** animals develop from three: ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm.

In triploblastic animals, the body cavity may be described as:
- **Acoelomate:** no body cavity between the gut and body wall.
- **Pseudocoelomate:** a body cavity that is not completely lined by mesoderm.
- **Coelomate:** a true coelom lined by mesoderm.

These are body-plan terms. They are not the same thing as the animal's habitat or its method of movement.

### 7.2 Important invertebrate groups

| Group | Main features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Porifera | Sponges; aquatic, attached or sedentary animals with pores; they lack true tissues and organs. | Sponges |
| Cnidaria (Coelenterata in older notes) | Mostly aquatic; radial symmetry; tentacles with stinging cells; a simple gastrovascular cavity with one opening. | Hydra, jellyfish, sea anemone, corals |
| Platyhelminthes | Flat, usually unsegmented worms; bilateral symmetry; no body cavity (acoelomate). Some are parasitic. | Planaria, tapeworm, flukes |
| Nematoda | Round, unsegmented worms; bilateral symmetry; pseudocoelom. | Ascaris, hookworm |
| Annelida | Segmented worms; true coelom; many have bristles or other structures for movement. | Earthworm, leech |
| Mollusca | Soft-bodied animals, often with a muscular foot and mantle; some have shells. | Snail, mussel, squid, octopus |
| Arthropoda | Jointed appendages, segmented body and a chitinous external skeleton; grow by moulting. | Insects, spiders, crabs, centipedes |

The source groups arthropods into familiar classes:
- **Insecta:** three pairs of legs; examples include grasshoppers, ants and termites.
- **Arachnida:** usually four pairs of legs; examples include spiders, scorpions and ticks.
- **Crustacea:** examples include crabs, crayfish and prawns.
- **Myriapoda:** many body segments and many legs; examples include centipedes and millipedes.

### 7.3 The five vertebrate classes in the school note

| Class | Features commonly used for identification | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Pisces (fish) | Aquatic vertebrates; breathe mainly with gills; usually have fins and scales; most are ectothermic. | Tilapia, shark |
| Amphibia | Usually have moist skin; many have an aquatic larval stage and an adult stage adapted to land and water. | Toad, frog, salamander |
| Reptilia | Dry, scaly skin; breathe with lungs; most are ectothermic. | Lizard, snake, crocodile |
| Aves (birds) | Feathers, beak, wings and lungs; warm-blooded. | Pigeon, duck, ostrich |
| Mammalia | Hair or fur at some stage; females have mammary glands that produce milk; breathe with lungs; warm-blooded. | Human, bat, whale, rat |

These are introductory patterns. Individual species can differ, so use several features rather than one feature alone.

## 8. How to classify an unfamiliar organism

Follow these steps:

1. Observe the organism carefully without harming it.
2. Record visible features: body covering, number and arrangement of limbs, body symmetry, leaves, roots, flowers or seeds, as appropriate.
3. Decide which broad group the features support.
4. Compare the organism with the descriptions in the classification tables.
5. Give the group name and state the evidence for your choice.

**Worked example:** An animal has a backbone, feathers, a beak and two wings. The backbone places it among vertebrates; feathers and a beak support class Aves (birds). The answer is **Aves**, supported by the observed feathers and beak.

## Key points to remember

- Classification groups organisms using shared features; taxonomy is the science of naming and classifying them.
- The school rank order is Kingdom → Phylum/Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species.
- The five-kingdom scheme is a traditional framework; modern classification has added domains and continues to change with evidence.
- Monocots usually have one cotyledon; eudicots usually have two.
- Plants can also be grouped by agricultural use and by life span.
- Invertebrates lack a backbone; vertebrates have one.
- Use several observable characteristics when identifying an organism.

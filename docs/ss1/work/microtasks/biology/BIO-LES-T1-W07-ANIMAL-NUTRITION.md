# Supplementary lesson: Animal nutrition, food substances and digestive enzymes (SS 1 Biology, First Term, Week 7)

> This lesson develops the school's Week 7 material and supplies keyed practice for the enzyme assignment. It does not reproduce the original diagrams or claim that every statement in the source is scientifically correct. Source formula and medical/deficiency claims requiring separate review are not repeated as facts.

## Learning objectives
You should be able to identify food-substance groups and their sources/functions; explain balanced diet; define enzymes and their characteristics; distinguish proteases, amylases and lipases; and explain the simplified protein digestion sequence.

## 1. Food and nutrients
Food supplies nutrients and energy. A **nutrient** is a substance needed for energy, growth, repair or normal body functions.

The school source groups food substances under six headings: carbohydrate, protein, fats and oils, mineral salts, vitamins, and “water and roughages”. For clear learning, remember that water and dietary fibre (roughage) are two distinct components, even though the source groups them together.

| Food substance | Main functions | Common sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | Major energy source for many body cells. | Yam, cassava, rice, maize, bread, potatoes and cereals. |
| **Proteins** | Growth and tissue repair; making enzymes, some hormones and other body molecules. | Beans, cowpea, groundnut, soybeans, eggs, fish, milk and meat. |
| **Fats and oils (lipids)** | Concentrated energy store; insulation; cell membranes; help absorb vitamins A, D, E and K. | Groundnut, palm and coconut oils; butter, fish and some meats. |
| **Mineral salts** | Needed for bones/teeth, nerve and muscle activity, fluid balance and other processes; each mineral has its own role. | Varied foods including vegetables, legumes, dairy foods, fish, meat, eggs and iodised salt. |
| **Vitamins** | Organic micronutrients needed in small amounts for normal metabolism, growth and health. | Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, eggs, dairy foods, fish and other varied foods. |
| **Water** | Solvent and medium for reactions; transports substances; helps regulate temperature and supports excretion. | Drinking water and water in foods. |
| **Dietary fibre (roughage)** | Adds bulk to food in the digestive tract and supports normal bowel movement. | Vegetables, fruits, beans and whole grains. |

## 2. Carbohydrates
Carbohydrates include sugars, starch and fibre.
- **Monosaccharides** are single sugar units, such as glucose and fructose.
- **Disaccharides** contain two monosaccharide units, such as sucrose, maltose and lactose.
- **Polysaccharides** are long chains of sugar units, such as starch, glycogen and cellulose.

Plants commonly store carbohydrate as **starch**. Animals commonly store it as **glycogen**, especially in the liver and muscles. Cellulose forms much of the plant cell wall; humans do not digest cellulose with their own digestive enzymes, so it acts as dietary fibre.

## 3. Proteins
Proteins are made from smaller units called **amino acids**. Digestion breaks dietary proteins into amino acids and small peptides that can be absorbed. Protein sources include beans, cowpea, soybeans, groundnut, eggs, fish, milk and meat. Amino acids are used to build and repair tissues and to make enzymes, some hormones and other proteins.

## 4. Fats and oils
Fats and oils are lipids. They provide concentrated energy, form part of cell membranes, help insulate the body and help absorb vitamins A, D, E and K. Many dietary triglycerides are broken down into fatty acids and monoglycerides during digestion. Plant sources include groundnut, palm, coconut and soybean oils; animal sources include butter, dairy fat, fish and meats.

## 5. Mineral salts, vitamins, water and fibre
Minerals are inorganic nutrients; vitamins are organic nutrients. Both are needed in relatively small quantities. A varied diet helps provide them. Some vitamins dissolve in fat (A, D, E and K); others, including vitamin C and B-group vitamins, dissolve in water. Deficiency symptoms require careful assessment; one symptom alone does not prove a particular deficiency.

Water supports chemical reactions, transport, temperature regulation and excretion. Dietary fibre is not digested into glucose like starch; it adds bulk and supports normal bowel function.

## 6. Balanced diet
A **balanced diet** provides the energy and nutrients a person needs in suitable amounts and proportions for their age, activity, growth and health. It includes suitable amounts of carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, water and fibre. Balance is considered across the overall diet, not by requiring every meal to contain identical amounts of each nutrient.

Example: rice, beans, vegetables and clean drinking water can provide a mixture of carbohydrate, protein, fibre, vitamins, minerals and water. The exact nutritional value depends on portions and preparation.

## 7. Enzymes
An **enzyme** is a biological catalyst. Most enzymes are proteins. A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being permanently used up.

Main characteristics:
- enzymes are usually specific to particular substrates or reactions;
- small amounts can catalyse reactions;
- each enzyme has an optimum temperature range and optimum pH;
- unsuitable conditions, especially excessive heat or extreme pH, can change an enzyme's shape and reduce its activity;
- enzymes are not permanently consumed by the reaction.

Human digestive enzymes commonly work best near body temperature, about 37 °C, but optima differ. Pepsin works in the acidic stomach, while enzymes in the small intestine work in a more alkaline environment. Do not claim that every enzyme has the same optimum pH or temperature.

The substance an enzyme acts on is its **substrate**. The substrate binds at a region called the **active site**. The enzyme helps convert the substrate into product(s), then can act again:

**Enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + product(s)**

## 8. Main digestive enzyme groups

| Enzyme group | Substrate | Main result | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proteases** | Proteins and peptides | Smaller peptides and ultimately amino acids | Pepsin in the stomach; pancreatic proteases in the small intestine. |
| **Amylases** | Starch | Smaller carbohydrates, including maltose and short sugar chains | Salivary amylase in the mouth; pancreatic amylase in the small intestine. |
| **Lipases** | Fats (triglycerides) | Fatty acids and monoglycerides | Mainly in the small intestine, including pancreatic lipase. |

Amylase does not digest every sugar. Enzymes such as maltase, sucrase and lactase act on particular disaccharides at the small-intestinal surface.

## 9. Protein digestion: worked sequence
The school diagram gives this simplified sequence:

**Protein → Peptone → Polypeptide → Amino acid**

In the stomach, pepsin begins protein digestion and produces shorter peptides. In the small intestine, pancreatic and intestinal peptidases continue the breakdown. The final products available for absorption include amino acids and small peptides. The sequence is a teaching simplification, not a claim that every protein follows only three perfectly separate stages.

**Worked example:** If asked which enzyme group digests protein, answer **proteases**. If asked for the small building units of proteins, answer **amino acids**.

## Key points
Food provides nutrients for energy, growth, repair and body functions. The school's six headings group water and roughage together; scientifically, they have different roles. Proteins are built from amino acids. Proteases digest proteins, amylases digest starch, and lipases digest fats. Enzymes are biological catalysts with substrate specificity and optimum conditions.

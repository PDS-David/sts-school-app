# Supplementary lesson: Irritability, plant responses, movement and cyclosis (SS 1 Biology, Second Term, Week 4)

> This lesson develops the school's Week 4 topic. It supplies clear definitions and answers the clinically named klinostat gap using a verified source. It is a conceptual lesson, not a practical instruction sheet.

## Learning objectives
Define irritability and stimulus; distinguish taxis, nastic responses and tropisms; describe movement and cyclosis; identify movement structures in familiar organisms; explain the purpose of a klinostat.

## 1. Irritability and stimulus
**Irritability** (sensitivity) is the ability of an organism or part of an organism to detect a change and respond to it. A **stimulus** is a change in the internal or external environment that can cause a response. A **response** is the change in activity produced by the stimulus.

Examples of stimuli include light, gravity, touch, temperature, chemicals and water availability.

## 2. Three types of plant and organism responses

### 2.1 Taxis (tactic response)
**Taxis** is a directional movement of a whole organism towards or away from a stimulus.
- **Positive phototaxis:** movement towards light.
- **Negative phototaxis:** movement away from light.
- **Chemotaxis:** movement in response to a chemical gradient.
- **Positive chemotaxis:** movement towards a chemical stimulus.

Example: a motile microscopic organism may swim towards a favourable light level or chemical source.

### 2.2 Nastic response
A **nastic response** is movement of a plant part in response to a stimulus where the direction of the movement does not depend on the direction from which the stimulus comes.
- **Thigmonasty / haptonasty:** a touch response, such as leaflets of Mimosa folding when touched.
- **Nyctinasty:** daily opening or closing movements associated with the day–night cycle.

### 2.3 Tropism
A **tropism** is a directional growth response of a plant organ towards or away from a stimulus.
- **Phototropism:** growth response to light. Shoots commonly show positive phototropism.
- **Gravitropism (geotropism):** growth response to gravity. Roots commonly show positive gravitropism (towards gravity); shoots commonly show negative gravitropism (away from gravity).
- **Hydrotropism:** growth response to water.
- **Thigmotropism:** growth response to touch, for example tendrils winding around a support.

Remember the distinction: taxis moves a whole organism; tropism is directional growth of a plant part; nastic movement is not directed by the direction of the stimulus.

## 3. Movement
**Movement** is a change in position of an organism or part of an organism. Animals move to obtain food, avoid danger, find mates and respond to their surroundings. Plants may move parts of their bodies even though they do not walk from place to place.

## 4. Movement structures in selected organisms

| Organism | Structure used for movement | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Amoeba | Pseudopodia | Temporary extensions of cytoplasm help the cell move and engulf food. |
| Paramecium | Cilia | Numerous short cilia beat in coordinated waves. |
| Euglena | Flagellum | A long whip-like structure moves the cell through water. |
| Hydra | Body and tentacles | Can glide, bend, contract, somersault or swim. |
| Earthworm | Circular/longitudinal muscles and setae (chaetae) | Muscles change body length; setae grip the substrate. |
| Fish | Fins and body muscles | Body undulation and fins provide thrust, steering and balance. |
| Toad/frog | Hind limbs | Powerful limbs are used for hopping and swimming. |

**Correction of terminology:** Amoeba uses pseudopodia for locomotion; cyclosis is cytoplasmic streaming and should not be treated as the name of its locomotory organ.

## 5. Cyclosis (cytoplasmic streaming)
**Cyclosis** is the streaming movement of cytoplasm within a cell. It helps move materials around the cell. It can be observed in some plant cells, such as cells of aquatic plants. It is not the same as an organism moving its whole body from place to place.

## 6. What is a klinostat?
A **klinostat** (also spelled **clinostat**) is a slowly rotating device used to investigate the effect of gravity on plant growth. It turns a seedling or plant organ around a horizontal axis so the direction of gravity relative to the specimen changes continuously. This averages the one-sided directional effect of gravity and reduces the usual bending response.

- A stationary horizontal seedling usually shows roots bending downwards and shoots bending upwards.
- A rotating horizontal seedling commonly grows without the same consistent one-sided curvature.

The klinostat does not remove gravity or create true weightlessness. Light should be kept uniform or excluded in a gravity investigation so that phototropism does not confuse the result.

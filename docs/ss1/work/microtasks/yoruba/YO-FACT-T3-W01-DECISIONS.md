## 1. EDE — ọ̀rọ̀-orúkọ

### Decision 1.1 — retain the ten categories, but teach that they overlap

The source lists ten categories: person, non-person, animate, inanimate, tangible, place, abstract, countable, uncountable and time. These are retained because they are the categories in the supplied SS1 material. The lesson explicitly explains that they are descriptive groupings, not ten mutually exclusive grammatical classes. For example, `ìwé` can be non-person, inanimate, tangible and countable.

**Evidence:** source paragraphs 73–88.

### Decision 1.2 — retain the source's three sentence functions, with a terminology note

The source calls the functions `olúwa`, `àbọ̀` and `ẹ̀yàn`. The first two are explained as the performer/subject and the receiver/object. The source explains `ẹ̀yàn` as adding further information to a noun, illustrated by `ẹja yíyan` and `ìwé mẹ́wàá`. The lesson preserves the source term and describes it as an added qualifier/complement; it does not silently substitute a different grammatical taxonomy.

**Evidence:** source paragraphs 90–106.

**Still requires local review:** confirm that the school's preferred grammatical explanation for `ẹ̀yàn` matches the source's use.

### Decision 1.3 — add a sourced explanation of oríkì

The scheme lists `oríkì` under the EDE topic, but the source body does not explain it. Add a short definition as praise appellations/praise poetry associated with people, lineages, places and other subjects. Keep it distinct from an ordinary personal name.

**Evidence:** Academy of American Poets, “Oríkì”; Karin Barber, “Oríkì,” in *The Routledge Companion to Performance-Related Concepts in Non-European Languages*; *The Cambridge History of African and Caribbean Literature*, “Oral Genres”.

## 2. ASA — ìsọmọlórúkọ

### Decision 2.1 — retain the four source categories

Retain `àmútọ̀runwá`, `àbíkú`, `inagijẹ` and `àbísọ`, with short definitions and examples.

**Evidence:** source paragraphs 112–179.

### Decision 2.2 — use independently corroborated birth-circumstance examples

Use `Ìgẹ̀`, `Àìná`, `Òjó`, `Dàda` and `Ajàyí` as the core examples. Published Yoruba naming studies corroborate the birth-circumstance categories and note regional variation. Avoid the source's duplicated `Oke` entries because the source gives two different explanations for the same form.

**Evidence:** Reuben Olúwáfẹ́mi Ìkọ̀tún, “New Trends in Yorùbá Personal Names among Yorùbá Christians”; Kayode (2022), “The Cultural Context of Naming Systems Amongst the Yoruba of South West Nigeria”; University of Ibadan repository study on Yoruba naming.

### Decision 2.3 — frame àbíkú names as cultural belief, not medical fact

Use examples such as `Dúrójáyé`, `Rọ́tímí`, `Málọmọ́`, `Kósókó` and `Bámítálẹ̀` as names documented in studies of Yoruba naming. Explain that they express a traditional belief, parental grief, appeal or hope. Do not claim that a child is spiritually returning, and do not use the lesson as a medical explanation for infant illness or death.

**Evidence:** Odebode and Onadipe (2011), “Politeness Phenomenon in Abiku Names among the Yoruba Africans”; University of Ibadan repository study on naming; Boston University study, “Home Before Naming: Naming Practices and Yoruba Characterization in Femi Osofisan's Selected Dramatic Texts”.

### Decision 2.4 — do not silently tone-mark the source's inagijẹ list

The source supplies `Eyinafe`, `Ajisafe`, `Peleyeju` and `Oginni` without tone marks. Their exact local pronunciations and tone patterns are not established by the supplied source. The lesson teaches the category and uses clearly labelled classroom-created descriptive examples. The original spellings remain evidence only until a competent Yoruba reviewer approves their tone marks.

### Decision 2.5 — keep àbísọ examples provisional

The source links names to family position, occupation, religion and birth events. The lesson uses a small set of familiar examples but states that meanings and tones can vary by family and region. Do not generalise one family's explanation to every bearer of a name.

## 3. LITIRESO — ewì àpilẹ̀kọ

### Decision 3.1 — teach the defined devices

The source defines or illustrates `àwìtúnwì`, `àsọdùn`, `òwe`, `àkànlò èdè`, `àfiwé` and `ìfọ̀rọ̀dárà`. The lesson teaches the devices with original or clearly attributed examples.

**Evidence:** source paragraphs 182–193.

### Decision 3.2 — do not invent a definition for àkúdé gbólóhùn

The source lists `àkúdé gbólóhùn` but supplies no definition or usable example. Leave it as an explicit reviewer dependency until a reliable Yoruba literature source or subject expert explains it.

### Decision 3.3 — keep the wordplay example unmarked pending review

The source's `funfun` wordplay example has no tone marks. Preserve the raw source form in the evidence record; do not add guessed tone marks or teach a corrected form until reviewed.

### Decision 3.4 — use an original practice poem, not a prescribed text

The new “Ọ̀nà Ẹ̀kọ́” poem is an original teaching example for identifying theme and devices. It is not represented as a WAEC/NECO set poem. The official selected poem/text, if required by the school, remains a separate dependency.

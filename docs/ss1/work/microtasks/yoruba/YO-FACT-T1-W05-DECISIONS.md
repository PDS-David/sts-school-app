## 1. Tone definition

**Source defect:** The source defines tone as moving the voice up and down, but does not explain that tone is a phonological feature on a syllable and can distinguish word meaning.

**Proposed replacement:** Tone is the pitch level or pitch movement used when pronouncing a syllable. Yorùbá has three level tones—high, mid and low—and tone can distinguish the meanings of words with the same segmental letters.

**Evidence:** National Language Centre, *Orthographies of Nigerian Languages, Manual I*, Yoruba Orthography by Professor Ayọ Bamgbóṣé, pp. 43–54, explicitly identifies high, mid and low tones and gives the minimal contrast `bá` (meet), `ba` (weave), `bà` (impinge upon).

**Disposition:** Replaced in the Week 5 EDE lesson draft.

## 2. Source abbreviations for the three level tones

**Source defect:** The source uses `M`, `D` and `R` after high, low and mid tone, but does not define the abbreviations. They do not match the usual H/M/L notation.

**Proposed replacement:** Use plain names and standard labels: High (H), Mid (M), Low (L). The high tone is marked with an acute accent; the low tone with a grave accent; the mid tone is unmarked in a teaching manual that marks every tone.

**Evidence:** Bamgbóṣé's National Language Centre manual gives the standard tone names and exact accent conventions. The manual records that the JCC-approved orthography was adopted in June 1974 by Ministries of Education and WAEC for Yoruba examinations.

**Disposition:** Replaced in the EDE lesson draft; no unexplained M/D/R abbreviations are carried forward.

## 3. Unmarked examples for high, low and mid tone

**Source defect:** The source lists `mimo`, `titi`, `jide`, `kola`, `ogede`, `isale`, `iwa`, `rere`, `igba` and `akin` without tone marks or underdots. Several have multiple possible lexical readings.

**Proposed replacement:** Use the published minimal contrast `bá`, `ba`, `bà`, whose meanings are documented in the National Language Centre manual. Do not infer tones for the source's unmarked list.

**Evidence:** Bamgbóṣé, Yoruba Orthography, p. 50 (three tone examples and definitions).

**Disposition:** Replaced in the EDE draft. The unmarked source examples remain intact in the source DOCX and are not represented as corrected.

## 4. Rising and falling contour-tone labels

**Source defect:** The source uses `V` and `^` as rising/falling tone marks and then says those marks are no longer used in modern Yorùbá writing. It does not explain the current orthographic convention.

**Proposed replacement:** Teach rising and falling as pitch movement, but do not use `V`, `^` or a tilde as general modern spelling marks. For a long vowel with more than one tone, write the vowel sequence with each tone marked. The manual's examples are `oòrùn` (sun), `òórùn` (smell) and `gbóòórùn` (notice a smell).

**Evidence:** Bamgbóṣé's manual explains that the tilde was abandoned because it cannot identify which of the possible tone combinations is intended; it gives the doubled/tripled vowel examples and says each tone should be represented.

**Disposition:** Replaced in the EDE lesson draft.

## 5. Source examples of contour tones

**Proposed replacement:** Use the official examples `oòrùn`, `òórùn` and `gbóòórùn` from the National Language Centre manual. Do not guess tone marks for the original ASCII forms.

**Evidence:** Bamgbóṣé, Yoruba Orthography, pp. 50–51.

**Disposition:** Replaced in the EDE draft; original forms remain preserved.

## 6. Marriage proverbs

**Source defect:** The source lists two marriage proverbs without tone marks, underdots, translations or explanations. The first string (`Ati gbeyawo ko to pon, owo obe lo soro`) is not independently verified in the consulted published proverb sources. The second (`Bi aya ba moju oko tan, alarina a yeba`) is independently documented, but the source spelling is incomplete.

**Proposed treatment:** Do not invent a translation for the first string. Use two published marriage sayings in the student supplement:
- `Ẹni fúnni l'ọmọ parí oore` — the person who gives a child in marriage has shown great favour;
- `Bí aya bá m'ojú ọkọ tán, alárinà a yèba` — once wife and husband understand each other, the intermediary steps aside.

**Evidence:** Yemisi M. Famakinwa (2019), “Presupposition and Sentence Synonyms as Semantic Devices in Selected Yorùbá Proverbs,” *Inkanyiso*, 11(1), pp. 88–89, proverbs 12 and 13, with English equivalents and discussion.

**Disposition:** A replacement ASA lesson is drafted. The original first saying remains uncorrected pending local confirmation; the replacement set is documented and requires teacher acceptance.

## 7. Mutual-assistance proverbs

**Source defect:** The source lists four mutual-assistance proverbs in unmarked ASCII. The final two (`Ka ro so mo di...` and `Ko kunrin rejo...`) cannot be safely reconstructed from the supplied text alone.

**Proposed treatment:** Preserve the source strings but do not invent tones or translations for the two ambiguous forms. Replace the student-facing examples with four independently documented proverbs:
- `Àgbàjọ ọwọ́ la fi ń sọyà; àjẹjẹ ọwọ́ kan kò gbé ẹrù dé orí` — one hand cannot lift a load to the head; cooperation is needed;
- `Ká fi ọwọ́ wẹ ọwọ́ ni ọwọ́ fi ń mọ́` — the hands become clean by washing one another;
- `Ọkọ gbégbá kí n gbágbòn ní í múlé gún` — sharing household duties makes the home peaceful;
- `Igi kan ò lè dá igbó ṣe` — one tree cannot make a forest.

**Evidence:** Ayo Ojebode and Wole Oladapo (2014), “The Power of Truth-Driven Propaganda,” *Research in African Languages & Linguistics*, 13, p. 6, documents the mutual-assistance sayings and explains their collective meaning. Famakinwa (2019), pp. 88–89, documents the shared-responsibility proverb.

**Disposition:** A replacement ASA lesson is drafted with translations, explanations and exercises. The exact tone marks of the proverb forms should be confirmed by the school's Yoruba reviewer before package promotion.

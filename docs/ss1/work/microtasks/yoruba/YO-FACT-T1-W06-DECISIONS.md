## 1. Syllable definition

**Source defect:** The source definition at the Week 6 EDE opening contains corrupted/unclear wording (`ege oro ti okere julo ... ori isemii kan soso`). It describes a syllable as a piece of speech but does not state its phonological structure or tone-bearing role clearly.

**Proposed replacement definition:** A syllable is a unit of pronunciation organised around one nucleus. In Standard Yorùbá, the nucleus is a vowel or a syllabic nasal, and each syllable bears a tone.

**Evidence:** University of Georgia's Yoruba phonology description and Oyinloye's phonological analysis describe V, CV and N syllables and treat the syllable as the tone-bearing unit. The National Language Centre manual supplies the orthographic treatment of vowels and syllabic nasals.

**Disposition:** Replaced in the EDE lesson draft; the original source sentence remains preserved.

## 2. `Batani / Ihun Silebu`

**Source defect:** The source puts `Batani / Ihun Silebu` in one heading but does not define whether these are synonyms or distinct technical terms.

**Proposed treatment:** Use `ìhun sílébù` for syllable structure and teach the three patterns V, CV and N. Use `àpẹẹrẹ` for an example or pattern illustration. Do not teach `batani` as a separate technical term until the subject reviewer confirms its intended meaning and spelling.

**Evidence:** The published phonological analyses support V/CV/N. The National Language Centre orthography manual establishes the approved spelling and tone conventions but does not establish `batani` as a technical term for syllable structure.

**Disposition:** The lesson avoids inventing a definition for `batani`. Local subject review remains required for this source term.

## 3. Four-column syllable table

**Source defect:** The source table has four columns, with `APA` repeated as both the first and fourth heading. `ALEYE` and `APEERE ORO` are the other headings. The rows for `Odo Silebu`, `Apaala`, `Abere Silebu` and `Apekun Silebu` have overlapping or inconsistent descriptions and examples.

**Proposed treatment:** Replace the malformed four-column table in the student-facing supplement with a correctly aligned three-pattern table:
- V: vowel nucleus alone;
- CV: consonant onset followed by vowel nucleus;
- N: syllabic nasal nucleus.

Explain the onset and nucleus where they exist. Do not invent a consonant coda for Standard Yorùbá syllables. The `n` in a nasal-vowel spelling marks vowel nasalisation; it is not automatically a coda. The source's `Apekun`/`Kun` example cannot be used to prove that a consonant coda exists.

**Evidence:** University of Georgia's Yoruba phonology source and Oyinloye's analysis establish V, CV and N as the three structures and describe Yorùbá syllables as open. The National Language Centre manual explicitly distinguishes orthographic `n` for vowel nasalisation from the syllabic nasal.

**Disposition:** A corrected student-facing V/CV/N explanation is drafted. The original four labels are retained in this evidence register and require teacher confirmation before any claim is made about their intended Yoruba equivalents.

## 4. EDE lead-ins and spelling

**Source defects:** The lead-in sentences for V, CV and N examples contain missing/incorrect word boundaries and incomplete conjunctions. Several words are unmarked ASCII where Yorùbá orthography requires underdots or tone marks.

**Proposed treatment:** Replace the lead-ins with short, grammatical teaching sentences that introduce one pattern at a time. Use the 1974 JCC-approved orthography:
- `ẹ`, `ọ` and `ṣ` are distinct letters and must retain their underdots;
- high tone uses acute, low tone uses grave;
- mid tone is unmarked when all tones are marked in a teaching manual;
- a syllabic nasal receives a tone mark where it could be confused with an orthographic `n` indicating vowel nasalisation.

**Evidence:** National Language Centre, *Orthographies of Nigerian Languages, Manual I*, Yoruba Orthography by Professor Ayọ Bamgbóṣé, pp. 43–54. The manual records the June 1974 JCC adoption for Ministries of Education and WAEC examinations.

**Disposition:** The EDE draft uses the approved orthography and includes a dedicated tone/underdot section. Lexical tone for source examples is not inferred from the unmarked DOCX.

## 5. Five consonant-plus-vowel segmentations

**Source defect:** The source lists `Ke`, `Je`, `Fe`, `Gba`, `Sun` with floating arrows and no tone marks. `gb` is not explained as one consonant letter/sound, and `Sun` does not show whether `n` marks vowel nasalisation.

**Proposed replacement examples:**
- `ké` → `k + é` (CV; cry/shout);
- `fò` → `f + ò` (CV; jump);
- `fẹ́` → `f + ẹ́` (CV; want);
- `gbà` → `gb + à` (CV; accept; `gb` is one consonant);
- `sùn` → `s + un` (CV; sleep; `n` marks vowel nasalisation, not a coda).

**Evidence:** University of Texas at Austin's *Yorùbá Yé Mi* introductory textbook lists monosyllabic CV verbs including `fò`, `fẹ́` and `sùn`. The University of Georgia grammar source describes simple Yoruba verbs as monosyllabic CV forms. The National Language Centre alphabet lists `gb` as one of the 25 letters.

**Disposition:** The EDE draft replaces the five arrow diagrams with explicit text segmentation. These are newly selected examples, not claims about the exact intended tones of the original unmarked forms.

## 6. Syllabic-nasal examples

**Source defect:** The source's four examples (`Ko m ko`, `Gbangba`, `Ogedengbe`, `Gbanjo`) are not segmented consistently. Their plain ASCII spelling does not distinguish syllabic `m/n` from an `n` used to mark vowel nasalisation.

**Proposed replacement examples:**
- `Bímbọ́lá` → `Bí | m | bọ́ | là`;
- `aláńgbá` → `a | lá | ǹ | gbá`;
- `ó ḿbọ̀` → `ó | ḿ | bò`;
- `ó ńké` → `ó | ń | ké`;
- `ng ò lọ` → `ng | ò | lọ` (the approved orthography also allows `n` as an alternative to `ng` for the first-person singular syllabic nasal).

**Evidence:** University of Wisconsin's pronunciation reference provides the syllabifications of `Bímbọ́lá` and `aláńgbá`. The National Language Centre manual states that syllabic nasal is written `m` before `b`, `n` before other consonants, and `ng` before vowels, with `n` accepted as an alternative to `ng` in that environment. The manual also requires tone marking on a syllabic nasal where it could be confused with vowel-nasalisation `n`.

**Disposition:** The EDE draft includes the verified examples and the nasalisation distinction. The four source examples are not silently corrected; they remain preserved in the source archive.

## 9. Authoritative orthography decision

The 1974 JCC-approved Yoruba orthography, as reproduced in the National Language Centre manual, is the default convention for the replacement lesson:
- 25-letter alphabet includes `ẹ`, `ọ`, `ṣ` and `gb`;
- high tone = acute; low tone = grave; mid tone = unmarked when all tones are shown;
- a macron may be used for an all-mid-tone word only when the writer is selectively marking some tones;
- syllabic nasals use `m`, `n` or `ng` according to position; tone marks should distinguish syllabic nasal from vowel-nasalisation `n`.

This is a recorded published expert/education authority decision, not a claim that the school's local subject teacher has signed off the new lesson.

# Week 4 corrected supplement: Legitimacy, sovereignty and democracy

**Class:** SS 1  
**Subject:** Government  
**Term/week:** First Term, Week 4  
**Use:** This original supplement replaces the Week Four teaching row for delivery. It does not reproduce the assigned textbook pages.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, a student should be able to:
1. explain legitimacy and distinguish legal validity, public acceptance, government performance and external recognition;
2. explain sovereignty, its dimensions and types, and distinguish Bodin's historical doctrine from modern international law; and
3. explain democracy, distinguish direct and representative forms, identify democratic features and assess arguments and criticisms without treating a value judgement as an objective fact.

## 1. Legitimacy

**Legitimacy** concerns whether a government or political authority is regarded as having a rightful claim to govern. It has several related but distinct dimensions.

- **Legal or constitutional validity:** whether the government entered office and exercises its powers according to the applicable Constitution and laws.
- **Public or sociological acceptance:** whether people generally accept the authority as rightful. Acceptance may be influenced by custom, consent, trust and political participation.
- **Performance:** whether the government delivers public services, manages public affairs and responds to people's needs. Good performance may strengthen public support, but performance does not replace legal requirements.
- **External recognition:** whether other states or international organisations recognise a government or state. This is an external diplomatic question; it is not the same as whether people inside the country accept their government.

A government may be legally established yet have weak public support. A government may also be popular but still act unlawfully. Legitimacy therefore cannot be measured by only one factor.

### Possible factors that influence legitimacy

The source lists leadership, shared national symbols, popular participation, foreign diplomacy, good government and public support. These can influence legitimacy, but they are not a universal checklist.

1. **Leadership and performance:** competence, integrity, accountability and the ability to respond to public needs may strengthen confidence.
2. **Shared symbols and identity:** common civic symbols and public institutions may help build a sense of belonging, while respecting diversity.
3. **Popular participation:** lawful participation, representation and meaningful opportunities to express views can strengthen acceptance.
4. **Foreign relations and recognition:** diplomatic recognition may support a government's external standing, but does not by itself prove domestic acceptance.
5. **Good government:** fairness, transparency, respect for rights and responsible use of public resources may strengthen public trust.
6. **Popular support:** public acceptance matters, but it should be assessed alongside constitutional validity, rights and the quality of political institutions.

An election result is important in a representative democracy, but the candidate or party with the most votes is not automatically legitimate in every possible circumstance. The election must follow the constitutional and legal rules, and the broader system must respect rights and lawful limits.

## 2. Sovereignty

**Sovereignty** is the supreme public authority of a state within its territory and its independence in external relations. In modern international law, sovereign equality does not mean that a state is exempt from obligations it has accepted under international law.

### Jean Bodin's historical theory

The French political thinker **Jean Bodin** lived from approximately **1529/30 to 1596**. His major work on sovereignty was *The Six Books of the Commonwealth* (1576). Bodin described sovereignty as the “absolute and perpetual power” of a commonwealth.

Bodin's formulation belongs to a particular period in European political thought. He argued that sovereign authority was not bound by ordinary civil laws made by the sovereign, while recognising limits from natural and divine law. His doctrine is important in the history of sovereignty, but it should not be taught as a complete description of every modern state.

The United Nations Charter recognises the **sovereign equality** of its members in Article 2(1) and requires members to fulfil their Charter obligations in good faith under Article 2(2). Sovereignty and international obligations therefore coexist. A state does not lose its sovereign status simply because it has legal duties under a treaty or the UN Charter.

### Dimensions and types of sovereignty

The textbook lists six types. They describe different dimensions and should not be treated as six unrelated ways of acquiring power.

1. **Legal sovereignty:** the legally recognised authority to make and enforce laws through the institutions established by the Constitution.
2. **Political sovereignty:** the ultimate political influence associated with the people or electorate in a democratic system. This is not always identical to the legal institution that formally enacts a law.
3. **Internal sovereignty:** the state's authority within its territory over public institutions and persons subject to its jurisdiction. It is not limited only to the state's nationals.
4. **External sovereignty:** the state's independence and equal legal status in relations with other states. It does not exempt the state from international obligations.
5. **De facto sovereignty:** actual or effective control over a territory and its public institutions. It describes what happens in practice.
6. **De jure sovereignty:** legal status or entitlement under law. It describes what is legally recognised or claimed as lawful.

**De facto and de jure are not simply two methods of acquiring sovereignty.** They describe factual control and legal status. A government may exercise effective control without being legally recognised as entitled to govern; legal entitlement and actual control may therefore diverge.

### Classical characteristics of sovereignty

Classical writers describe sovereignty through characteristics such as:

- **Supremacy:** within the state's constitutional order, public authority is exercised through the highest legal framework.
- **Permanence:** the state continues even when a particular government or group of officeholders changes.
- **Indivisibility:** classical theory treats sovereignty as one ultimate authority. In a federation, however, the Constitution distributes powers among levels and institutions; this does not mean every power is held by one central office.
- **Absoluteness:** Bodin used this idea to describe the independence of the sovereign from ordinary civil laws made by that sovereign. It is a historical doctrine, not a statement that modern states have no constitutional or international limits.
- **Comprehensiveness:** public law applies within the state's jurisdiction, subject to constitutional rights, limits and lawful exceptions.
- **Inalienability:** sovereignty is not simply transferred like private property. States can nevertheless enter treaties and accept international obligations while retaining sovereign status.

The statement that a state “receives order from none” is too absolute if presented as modern international law. The UN Charter recognises sovereign equality and also requires good-faith performance of Charter obligations.

## 3. Democracy

**Democracy** is a system of government in which political authority ultimately derives from the people. Citizens participate directly or choose representatives through elections. Democratic government also requires more than voting: rights, lawful limits, accountability and opportunities for participation matter.

Abraham Lincoln used the familiar description **“government of the people, by the people, for the people”** in his **Gettysburg Address of 19 November 1863**. It is a concise statement associated with popular government, not a complete technical definition of every democratic institution.

### Types of democracy

1. **Direct democracy:** citizens take part directly in deciding public questions, rather than making all decisions through elected representatives. It is most workable where the group and the issue allow meaningful direct participation.
2. **Representative or indirect democracy:** citizens elect representatives to make public decisions on their behalf. Representatives remain subject to constitutional limits, elections, public scrutiny and accountability.

Modern states may combine representative institutions with some direct-participation mechanisms. Neither form automatically guarantees good government; institutions and protections matter.

### Features of democracy

Important features include:

- regular, genuine and competitive elections;
- inclusive political participation and the principle of one person, one vote;
- protection of fundamental rights and freedoms;
- majority decision-making within constitutional limits;
- protection of minorities and people who disagree with the majority;
- opposition parties and peaceful competition for office;
- an independent judiciary and fair legal process;
- separation or distribution of public powers;
- freedom of expression and a free press; and
- accountability and transparency in public office.

**Majority rule is not unlimited rule.** A democratic majority must respect constitutional rights and the lawful protections owed to minorities and individuals.

### Arguments for democracy and criticisms

The statement that democracy is “the best form of government” is a **normative judgement**: it expresses a view about what kind of government is most desirable. It is not an objective fact that can be settled by one definition.

**Arguments in favour** include public participation, consent, accountability, the possibility of peaceful changes of government, protection of rights and the ability to challenge public decisions.

**Criticisms and challenges** include slow decision-making, the cost of elections and public institutions, short-term political incentives, misinformation, unequal influence from wealth, electoral malpractice and the risk that a majority may disregard minority rights. These are possible problems, not inevitable features of every democracy.

The source's claim that democracy is dangerous in an “illiterate society” is an overgeneralisation and should not be taught as fact. The quality of democratic participation is influenced by access to reliable information, civic education, fair institutions, rights and accountability. Literacy level does not make a whole population inherently unfit for democratic government.

## 4. Quick recap

- Legitimacy includes legal validity and public acceptance; performance and external recognition are related but separate factors.
- Sovereignty concerns supreme authority within a territory and independence in external relations, alongside international obligations.
- Jean Bodin (1529/30–1596) published *The Six Books of the Commonwealth* in 1576 and advanced a historical doctrine of absolute and perpetual sovereignty.
- De facto sovereignty concerns actual control; de jure sovereignty concerns legal status or entitlement.
- Direct democracy involves citizens deciding directly; representative democracy involves elected representatives.
- Majority rule must operate with rights, constitutional limits and minority protections.
- “Democracy is the best form of government” is a normative claim that should be assessed through arguments and counterarguments.
- Lincoln delivered the Gettysburg Address on 19 November 1863.

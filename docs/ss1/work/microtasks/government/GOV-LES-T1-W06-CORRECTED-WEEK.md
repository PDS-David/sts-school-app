# Week 6 corrected supplement: Socialism, communism, capitalism and class terms

**Class:** SS 1  
**Subject:** Government  
**Term/week:** First Term, Week 6  
**Use:** This original supplement replaces the Week Six teaching row for delivery. It does not reproduce the assigned textbook pages.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, a student should be able to:
1. explain socialism as a broad family of political and economic ideas and identify features associated with particular models;
2. distinguish the theoretical communist ideal from historical communist-party states;
3. explain capitalism and its varied historical and political forms; and
4. use Marxist and dependency-theory class terms accurately, without treating contested labels as neutral descriptions of every poor or unemployed person.

## 1. Socialism

**Socialism** is a broad family of political and economic ideas that seek greater social control over important productive resources and a more equal distribution of economic power or benefits. Socialist traditions differ about who should own or manage resources, how decisions should be made and how much role markets should have.

Socialist ideas and movements existed before Karl Marx. Early nineteenth-century figures and movements included Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier and Robert Owen. Marx later developed an influential critique of capitalism and a theory of historical change, but he did not invent every form of socialism.

### Features associated with some socialist models

1. **Social, public or cooperative ownership:** some productive resources may be owned or managed by the state, communities, workers or cooperatives. The balance differs among socialist traditions.
2. **Social planning or coordination:** public institutions or collective bodies may coordinate investment and production. Some models use central planning; others allow markets.
3. **Redistribution and social provision:** policies may aim to reduce severe inequality and provide services such as education, health care or social protection.
4. **Worker participation:** some models seek greater worker influence in workplaces or economic decisions.
5. **Economic security and employment:** full employment or secure livelihoods may be policy aims in particular models, not automatic results or universal defining features.
6. **Distribution according to need:** this is an ideal associated with some socialist and communist traditions; it is not a rule followed by every policy or every movement described as socialist.

These features should be attributed to particular models. Socialism is not one single system in which every productive asset is always owned by the central government. **Social democracy** and regulated mixed economies are related political traditions or arrangements, but they are not simply synonyms for every form of socialism.

## 2. Karl Marx

**Karl Marx lived from 1818 to 1883.** He was a German philosopher, economist and political writer whose work strongly influenced Marxism, socialist movements and communist-party movements.

Marx's influence should not be confused with authorship of all socialist thought. Socialist movements existed before his major writings. Marx's work is especially associated with historical materialism, analysis of class relations and criticism of capitalist production. Different scholars and political movements have interpreted his work in different ways.

## 3. Communism: theoretical ideal and historical practice

**Communism** has more than one use in political discussion. It is important to distinguish the theoretical ideal from governments led by communist parties.

### The theoretical ideal

In Marxist theory, communism refers to a future society in which class divisions have disappeared and the state, understood as an instrument of class domination, is no longer needed. The ideal is associated with common ownership of productive resources and production organised for human needs rather than private profit. Some formulations describe distribution according to need.

This is a theoretical aspiration. It should not be presented as a description of the institutions of every country ruled by a communist party.

### Historical communist-party states

During the twentieth century, several states were governed by communist parties and developed different state-socialist institutions. These included public ownership of major industries, economic planning and one-party political systems, but arrangements varied by country and period.

The existence of communist-party states is a historical fact. Whether any of them fully realised the theoretical classless and stateless ideal is a separate question. Therefore, the statement that “no country has ever practised communism” is too absolute unless the speaker first defines communism as the theoretical end-state. Equally, describing every historical communist-party state as having achieved the theoretical ideal is inaccurate.

## 4. Capitalism

**Capitalism** is an economic system in which productive assets are substantially privately owned, goods and services are exchanged through markets, wage labour is important, and investment is often guided by the expectation of profit and capital accumulation.

### Features commonly associated with capitalist economies

1. **Private ownership:** individuals or organisations may own businesses, land, equipment and other productive assets, subject to law.
2. **Markets and exchange:** prices and market transactions help coordinate many economic decisions.
3. **Wage labour:** many people work for wages or salaries for employers.
4. **Investment and capital accumulation:** businesses and investors may reinvest resources to expand production or returns.
5. **Profit and enterprise:** expected returns can influence business decisions and innovation.
6. **Competition and regulation:** firms may compete, while governments regulate markets to different degrees.

These features vary across capitalist economies. A minimally regulated or **laissez-faire** economy is one model, not the definition of every capitalist system. Actual capitalist economies may include taxation, public services, public enterprises, labour protections and extensive market regulation.

### Capitalism is not the same as democracy

Capitalism is an economic system; democracy is a political system. They are different concepts. Capitalist economies have existed under democratic and authoritarian governments. A market economy does not automatically guarantee political rights, and a democratic system does not require one single economic model.

Capitalist institutions and markets developed over several centuries. Capitalism did not begin only after the collapse of socialism in the twentieth century. Some state-socialist systems collapsed or changed near the end of the twentieth century, but socialism did not disappear worldwide and there was no single global transition.

## 5. Class terminology

The following terms come mainly from Marxist analysis and political-economy writing. They describe relationships to productive assets, labour and economic power. They should not be used as insults or as automatic labels for every person in a social group.

- **Bourgeoisie:** in Marxist analysis, a class associated with ownership or control of capital and productive assets. It is not simply a synonym for every wealthy person.
- **Proletariat:** wage workers who depend mainly on selling their labour and do not control the productive assets needed for independent production. The term describes a class relation, not a judgement about a person's character.
- **Peasantry or peasants:** rural agricultural producers whose relationships to land and labour vary. Some may own small plots, rent land, share crops or work as rural labourers. “Peasant” is not a synonym for all poor people or all wage workers.
- **Lumpenproletariat:** a historically contested and loaded term in Marxist writing. It has been used for groups outside stable wage-labour relations, but it is not a neutral synonym for all unemployed, underemployed or poor people. It should not be used to stigmatise a population or portray people as inherently criminal or dangerous.
- **National bourgeoisie:** in some dependency and political-economy writing, a domestic capitalist class with its own base of accumulation and interests in the national economy. The term is analytical and its use is debated.
- **Comprador bourgeoisie:** in some dependency and political-economy writing, a domestic capitalist fraction that acts as an intermediary or is closely linked to foreign capital. This is not a neutral synonym for criminals or for every business person who trades internationally.

The source's class list has inconsistent numbering. This replacement organises the concepts by their definitions; the original numbering defect remains recorded in the source evidence and is not silently rewritten in the source document.

## 6. Comparing the three economic ideas

| Question | Socialism | Communism (theoretical ideal) | Capitalism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main emphasis | Greater social control over productive resources and distribution | Classless, stateless ideal with common ownership and production for need | Private ownership, markets, wage labour and capital accumulation |
| Ownership | May be public, social, cooperative or mixed, depending on the model | Common ownership of productive resources in the theoretical end-state | Substantial private ownership of productive assets |
| Economic coordination | May involve planning, markets or a mixture | Collective organisation for common needs in the theoretical ideal | Markets and investment decisions play a major role |
| Political system | Varies by movement and country | The theoretical ideal is not the same as historical party-state institutions | Can coexist with democratic or authoritarian political systems |
| Main caution | Not every socialist model is centrally planned or wholly state-owned | Do not equate party-states with the fully realised theoretical ideal | Do not define every capitalist economy as laissez-faire or democratic |

## 7. Quick recap

- Socialism is a broad family of ideas; socialist movements existed before Marx.
- Karl Marx lived from 1818 to 1883 and was an influential theorist, not the originator of all socialism.
- The theoretical communist ideal is classless and stateless; historical communist-party states are a separate subject of study.
- Capitalism involves substantial private ownership, markets, wage labour and capital accumulation, but regulation varies.
- Capitalism and democracy are different concepts; capitalist economies can exist under different political regimes.
- Bourgeoisie, proletariat, peasantry, lumpenproletariat, national bourgeoisie and comprador bourgeoisie are analytical terms that require careful definitions.
- The term lumpenproletariat is contested and should not be used to stigmatise unemployed or poor people.

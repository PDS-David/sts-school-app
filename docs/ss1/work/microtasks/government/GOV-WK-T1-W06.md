## Socialism — lines 470–482

The source defines socialism mainly through government ownership of most productive resources, central planning and redistribution. That describes a state-centred model, not the full range of socialist thought. Socialist movements and ideas predate Marx: Gareth Stedman Jones's Cambridge history traces European socialism from the 1790s and identifies early nineteenth-century movements associated with Saint-Simon, Fourier and Robert Owen. Marx was an influential later theorist, not the originator of all socialism.

The source's features—state ownership, central planning, full employment, equality and distribution according to need—must be attributed to particular socialist models or aspirations, not stated as universal features of every form of socialism. Distinguish socialism from social democracy and from regulated mixed economies; do not collapse all three into one definition.

## Karl Marx — line 473

The source gives the dates as 1816–1883. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy records Karl Marx as **1818–1883**. Marx's work strongly influenced Marxism and communist thought, but the history of socialism predates him. Correct the date and avoid saying he formulated all socialist ideas.

## Communism — lines 483–498

The source's statement at line 486 that no country has ever practised communism is too absolute without defining the term. Archie Brown's Oxford Handbook chapter distinguishes:
- historical communist-party governments and state-socialist models, including varied systems in the Soviet Union, China and Yugoslavia; and
- the theoretical aspiration, in principle, to a **stateless, classless communist society**.

The existence of communist-party states is a historical fact; whether any state fully realized the theoretical classless/stateless ideal is a different question. The source's description of communism as state ownership of everything conflates historical state-socialist institutions with the theoretical end-state. Present the distinction neutrally and do not endorse or condemn an ideology.

## Capitalism — lines 499–509

The source says capitalism emerged after the fall of socialism “in the 1900s”. This chronology is wrong: capitalist institutions and markets predate the twentieth century by centuries. Some state-socialist systems collapsed or transformed near the end of the twentieth century, but socialism did not disappear globally and there was no single worldwide transition.

The source also treats minimal government as a defining feature. That is closer to a laissez-faire ideal; actual capitalist economies vary in regulation, public ownership, taxation and social provision. The SAGE *Understanding Comparative Politics* text notes that contemporary capitalist political economies exist in both democratic and authoritarian states, while a pure laissez-faire economy is difficult to find. Capitalism and democracy are therefore not identical or inherently inseparable: they are different economic and political concepts whose relationship is debated.

## Class terminology — lines 510–517

The source's list and numbering are preserved exactly in the source evidence. Its definitions need careful correction:
- **Bourgeoisie** and **proletariat** are class concepts in Marxist analysis, principally related to ownership/control of productive assets and wage labour.
- **Peasants** are rural agricultural producers with varied relationships to land and labour; they are not simply a synonym for all poor people or for wage workers. Scholarship on Marx's peasantry notes that the class position is debated and cannot be reduced to a single label.
- **Lumpenproletariat** is a historically contested and loaded Marxist term. It is not a neutral synonym for all unemployed or underemployed people and should not be used to label a population as a security threat.
- **National bourgeoisie** and **comprador bourgeoisie** are analytical terms in political-economy/dependency writing. In a common usage, the former refers to a domestic capitalist class with its own accumulation base, while the latter refers to intermediary or associated fractions linked to foreign capital. The source's moralized/criminal characterization of the comprador category is not a neutral definition and should not be repeated as fact.

## External fact-check references consulted

1. **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Karl Marx”** (first published 2003; substantive revision 27 March 2025). Gives Marx's dates as 1818–1883 and discusses his theory of history and communist future: [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marx/).

Documents pre-Marxian socialist movements and early figures including Saint-Simon, Fourier and Owen: [chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-history-of-modern-european-thought/european-socialism-from-the-1790s-to-the-1890s/175123179C134A5EC505AE787A91632D).

Distinguishes communist-party systems in power from the theoretical aspiration to a stateless, classless society: [chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34324/chapter-abstract/291335150).
4. **SAGE, *Understanding Comparative Politics*** (2023), chapter 2, p. 55. Notes that capitalist political economies exist in both democracies and authoritarian states, and that pure laissez-faire economies are rare in contemporary practice: [chapter PDF](https://uk.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/upm-assets/144021_book_item_144021.pdf).

Reviews the contested and historically variable use of the lumpenproletariat concept and cautions against simply equating it with all unemployed people: [report](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC135245/JRC135245_01.pdf).

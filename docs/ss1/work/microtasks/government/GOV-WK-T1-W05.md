## Communalism — lines 384–398

The source presents communalism as a political/economic model in which land and important resources are collectively owned and shared for common benefit. Its feature list includes no class divisions, no private property, barter and universal cooperation.

This is an idealized model, not a description of every community or a present Nigerian legal order. African land-tenure studies describe varied and overlapping arrangements: common property, individual property and other tenure rights may coexist in the same community or landscape. The corrected lesson should use “communalism” as a model for discussion, not as a universal claim about African society. It should not say that all communities prohibit individual property or use barter as their only means of exchange.

## Feudalism — lines 403–417

The source's Western European medieval framing is broadly recognizable, and fiefs and lord-vassal relations are central to one influential definition. However, “feudalism” is a contested retrospective label; historians use it in different ways and caution against treating all medieval Western Europe as one uniform system. The Oxford Bibliographies entry notes that fiefs were real but less ubiquitous than older generalizations suggested.

A fief could be land or a right granted by a lord in a relationship involving loyalty and service. The source's simplified picture of a king granting land directly to subjects should not be presented as universal; many lord-vassal relationships were layered and varied by period and region. The term “vassal” refers to a person in a lord-vassal relationship, not simply any peasant or subject.

### Serf and vassal are not synonyms

The source uses “serf” and “vassal” interchangeably. This is inaccurate:
- A **vassal** was a person bound to a lord through a feudal relationship, with reciprocal obligations; such relationships often involved members of the landed elite.
- A **serf/villein** was an unfree peasant tenant/worker whose status and obligations were tied to an estate or lordship.
- Serfdom is not simply slavery or ordinary paid service. A vassal was not necessarily a serf, and a serf was not automatically a vassal.

Oxford Handbook discussions distinguish feudal contracts among the noble elite from the labour of peasants on landed estates. The supplement must preserve this distinction.

### Industrialisation and the decline of feudalism

The source states that industrialisation brought about the collapse of feudalism. This is too simple:
- Mark Bailey's study documents the decline of serfdom in late medieval England from around 1300–1500, before the Industrial Revolution, and reviews multiple explanations including manumission, economic pressures, peasant resistance and migration.
- Jason W. Moore's environmental history analyses a crisis of feudalism and the rise of capitalism in the sixteenth century, again preceding industrialisation.
- These studies concern specific regions and debates; they do not establish one simultaneous end-date for every feudal institution in Europe.

The corrected lesson should distinguish the decline of serfdom from broader debates about the transition from feudal institutions to capitalist economies. Industrialisation may be relevant to later transformations, but it was not the single event that ended feudalism everywhere.

## Merits and demerits — lines 418–431

The source lists order, protection and a supposed foundation for capitalism as merits; it lists exploitation, autocratic lordship and political fragmentation as demerits. These should be taught as attributed historical interpretations or arguments, not universal facts:
- “Feudalism laid the foundation for capitalism” is a debated transition thesis, not a settled linear sequence.
- “Orderliness” and “security” may describe particular local arrangements but do not erase coercive hierarchy or variation.
- Claims of constant war and the absence of national government should be limited to particular contexts rather than generalized across all medieval Western Europe.
- The statement about “freed slaves” entering contracts with lords needs careful historical checking; it should not conflate slavery, serfdom and vassalage.

No sensational or graphic detail is needed to teach the historical distinctions.

## External historical references consulted

1. **Constance B. Bouchard, “Feudalism,” Oxford Bibliographies in Medieval Studies** (6 February 2012). Explains the contested and retrospective use of “feudalism,” the varied meanings attached to the term, and cautions that medieval fiefs were less ubiquitous than older accounts assumed: [entry](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62384/reference-article-abstract/555072719).
2. **Anthony Crubaugh, “Feudalism,” in *The Oxford Handbook of the Ancien Régime*** (18 September 2012), pp. 219–235. Describes a common feudo-vassalic model involving fiefs, reciprocal obligations and the distinction between the noble vassal relationship and peasant labour: [chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34328/chapter-abstract/291349368).
3. **Mark Bailey, *The Decline of Serfdom in Late Medieval England: From Bondage to Freedom*** (Boydell & Brewer, 2014). Chapter 4 reviews the decline of English serfdom and multiple explanations, including manumission, economic pressure, resistance and migration: [chapter summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/decline-of-serfdom-in-late-medieval-england/causes-of-decline/6C18BD9083C1C0201DA4BEE8A8F968B6).
4. **Jason W. Moore, “The Crisis of Feudalism: An Environmental History,” Organization & Environment** (first published September 2002). Analyses a sixteenth-century crisis in feudal relations and the transition to capitalism, illustrating why the change cannot be attributed solely to industrialisation: [article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1086026602153008).
5. **J.W. Bruce, “African tenure models at the turn of the century: individual property models and common property models,” FAO Land Reform 2000**. Describes diverse and overlapping tenure arrangements, including open access, common property and individual property: [article](https://www.fao.org/4/x7069t/x7069t03.htm).
6. **Professor Lionel Cliffe, “The Struggle for Land in Africa,” Leeds African Studies Bulletin 64** (December 2001). Warns against a single generalized African communal-land pattern and distinguishes collective ownership from collective use: [University of Leeds article](https://lucas.leeds.ac.uk/article/the-struggle-for-land-in-africa-lionel-cliffe/).

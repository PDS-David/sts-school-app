# Indirect Rule and Colonial Impacts in Western and Eastern Nigeria

**Class:** SS1  
**Subject:** Government  
**Term:** Second Term  
**Week:** Two

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Explain how indirect rule operated in parts of Western Nigeria and describe the Oyo example accurately.
2. Explain the variety of political institutions in Eastern Nigeria and the role of warrant chiefs.
3. Describe the main causes and historical significance of the 1929 Women's War.
4. Distinguish Mary Slessor's missionary work from the colonial system of indirect rule.
5. Discuss possible merits and limitations of indirect rule using regional examples.
6. Identify important constitutional, electoral, banking and currency developments with their correct dates.
7. Explain selected positive and negative effects of colonial rule without treating every region or community as the same.
8. Evaluate claims about colonial economic development using evidence rather than sweeping statements.

## 1. Indirect rule in Western Nigeria

Indirect rule was a system in which British colonial officials governed through selected African rulers, councils and local authorities. The British supervised the system and retained final colonial power. The details were not identical in every part of Nigeria.

Many Yoruba communities had established political institutions before British rule. The Oyo Empire, for example, had an Alaafin, but the Alaafin was not supposed to exercise unlimited power. The Oyo Mesi was an important council that could check the ruler's authority. Other institutions, including the Ogboni in some Yoruba settings, also had political and social influence.

This example teaches an important point: a traditional ruler did not necessarily govern alone. Councils, lineage groups, religious institutions and other authorities could limit a ruler's power. Political arrangements also differed among Yoruba states and communities, so the Oyo system should not be used as a description of every Yoruba polity.

British colonial officials sometimes selected or strengthened particular rulers as Native Authorities. In Western Nigeria, a formal Native Administration system was introduced in 1917. Research on colonial councils shows that the British sometimes gave selected rulers powers that did not fully reflect earlier checks and balances. In the Oyo example, the Alaafin's position could be presented as stronger than it had been under the older political arrangement.

The result was mixed. Using existing rulers could make communication and administration easier, but changing the balance between a ruler and the councils around him could create tension. Indirect rule did not simply preserve traditional government unchanged; it could reshape institutions to suit colonial administration.

## 2. Indirect rule in Eastern Nigeria

Eastern Nigeria also had varied political systems. Many Igbo communities were organized through village assemblies, councils of elders, lineage groups, age grades, title associations and other local institutions. Authority could be shared among several groups rather than concentrated in one king.

However, it is inaccurate to say that every community in Eastern Nigeria had no traditional ruler. Some places, including Onitsha and Oguta, had more centralized or monarchic institutions. The political structure of one town should not be used to describe every Igbo community.

British officials often found it difficult to govern communities where no single ruler had authority over everyone. In some places they appointed warrant chiefs. A warrant chief was a person given official authority by the colonial administration, often through a warrant or appointment. Warrant chiefs were not automatically the same as customary leaders chosen or accepted by the community.

The system caused problems in some communities because:

- an appointed chief might not have the local legitimacy of a customary authority;
- a chief could be given powers that had previously been shared among councils and other groups;
- some warrant chiefs were involved in collecting taxes and enforcing colonial orders;
- people could find it difficult to hold an appointed chief accountable through their usual community institutions.

These problems did not mean that every warrant chief acted in the same way or that every Eastern community had the same experience. The effects depended on local institutions, the conduct of officials and the authority given to each chief.

## 3. The 1929 Women's War

The protest commonly called the Aba Women's Riot is also known as the Women's War or Women's Rebellion of 1929. The wider name is useful because protests took place in more than one town and involved women from several communities in the region.

The protest had several connected causes. Women already had concerns about market conditions, the conduct of some warrant chiefs, native courts and colonial administration. A census and rumours that women might also be taxed increased fear and anger. The fear of a new tax was a powerful trigger, but it should not be presented as the only cause.

Women organized meetings, communicated across communities and challenged officials and warrant chiefs. Their actions showed political agency: women were not simply reacting to decisions made by men; they organized, debated and took collective action to defend their interests.

The Women's War exposed weaknesses in the way colonial officials used appointed chiefs and courts in parts of Eastern Nigeria. It contributed to official reviews and changes in local administration. It should not be described as proof that indirect rule failed in exactly the same way in every Eastern Nigerian community. It was a major protest against particular institutions and practices at a particular time.

## 4. Mary Slessor: missionary work, not indirect rule

Mary Slessor was a Scottish Presbyterian missionary who arrived in the Calabar area in 1876 and died in 1915. She opposed the killing of twins in some communities and worked with families and local converts. Her work is an example of missionary activity and social change.

It is incorrect to say that the colonial system of indirect rule itself ended the killing of twins through Mary Slessor. Missionary work and colonial administration were different activities. Local mothers, relatives, community members and African converts also made choices and contributed to change. A fair historical account should not present one European missionary as the only person responsible for changes in African communities.

This distinction matters when assessing the merits of indirect rule. An event that happened during the colonial period was not automatically caused by indirect rule.

## 5. Possible merits and limitations of indirect rule

Indirect rule had possible administrative advantages for the colonial government:

- It allowed officials to work through people who knew local languages and customs.
- It could reduce the number of British officers required for day-to-day administration.
- Where existing rulers and councils had local support, it could make communication and the delivery of some decisions easier.
- It could allow parts of an existing administrative structure to continue.

These points describe possible advantages, not proof that colonial rule was fair or that all local people benefited.

Indirect rule also had important limitations:

- Colonial officials retained final authority, so local rulers were not fully independent.
- Some rulers were strengthened, changed or appointed in ways that weakened older checks and balances.
- Warrant chiefs in some Eastern communities lacked the legitimacy of customary authorities.
- Tax collection and enforcement could make local authorities unpopular.
- People who disagreed with a ruler or chief might have limited ways to challenge decisions made under colonial supervision.
- The system differed from one region to another; it cannot be judged from only one example.

A balanced answer should explain both the administrative reasons for indirect rule and the effects it had on people and local institutions.

## 6. Constitutional and political developments

The Clifford Constitution of 1922 introduced a limited elective principle into the colonial legislature in Nigeria. The first elections under this arrangement took place in 1923. The elected representation was very restricted: three seats were allocated to Lagos and one to Calabar. The franchise also required a high annual income, commonly stated as £100, and the elective arrangement applied in the South rather than throughout the whole country.

These elections were not universal democracy. Most adults could not vote, and the colonial administration still held extensive power.

The Nigerian National Democratic Party (NNDP) was founded by Herbert Macaulay in 1922, according to the Central Bank of Nigeria's biographical account. The party took part in the political activity surrounding the 1923 elections. It is useful to distinguish the party's formation in 1922 from the first election under the Clifford Constitution in 1923.

These developments helped create new opportunities for political organization, but the opportunities were limited by the colonial constitution and the narrow franchise. Nigerian political activity and leadership were important in shaping the new institutions.

## 7. Banking and currency: use the correct timeline

Banking and currency developments should not be mixed together.

- The Bank of British West Africa was founded in 1894 as a commercial bank.
- The West African Currency Board was established in 1912 to organize a common currency system for British West African territories.
- The Currency Board began issuing currency in 1913. Its notes followed later, with note issues beginning in 1916.

A source reference to a “Colonial Bank” established in 1917 has not been reliably identified. Do not guess which institution the source meant. The accurate, verifiable examples above are enough to explain the development of banking and currency without inventing a bank or date.

## 8. Positive changes associated with the colonial period

Several changes expanded during the colonial period, although their reach and benefits were uneven:

1. **Constitutional and electoral institutions:** the Clifford Constitution of 1922 and the limited elections of 1923 created a small elected element in the colonial legislature.
2. **Political organization:** Nigerian political leaders and parties, including the NNDP, organized campaigns and pressed for greater representation.
3. **Civil-service administration:** colonial departments, courts and administrative offices expanded. African clerks, interpreters, teachers and other workers took part, although senior authority remained heavily controlled by colonial officials.
4. **Education:** mission and government schools expanded in some areas. Access was uneven, and local families, teachers, religious organizations and African communities played important roles.
5. **Transport and communication:** railways, roads, ports, postal services and telegraph networks expanded. These could improve travel and communication, but much infrastructure was also designed to move export goods and support colonial administration.
6. **Banking and currency:** commercial banking and the West African Currency Board introduced new financial institutions and a common currency system.

These changes should not be described as gifts that colonial rule alone created. African communities and political leaders acted within, adapted and challenged colonial institutions. The benefits were also distributed unevenly.

## 9. Negative effects and continuing debates

Colonial rule also had serious costs and long-term effects.

**Economic dependence and unequal trade:** Colonial economic policy often encouraged the export of raw materials and cash crops and the import of manufactured goods. Roads, railways and ports frequently connected production areas to export routes. This pattern could strengthen foreign merchant interests and leave local producers with less control over prices and investment.

**Cultural and language effects:** English, French and Portuguese became important in different West African administrations and education systems. These languages remain part of the region's history. Language differences can create practical challenges, but they do not make conflict inevitable. ECOWAS was established by the Treaty of Lagos on 28 May 1975 and works across English, French and Portuguese official languages to support regional cooperation.

**Removal of African rulers:** British authorities deported or exiled rulers who resisted colonial expansion and trade demands. Jaja of Opobo was deported in 1887. He was later moved to the West Indies and died at Tenerife in July 1891 while being sent back toward Nigeria. Oba Ovonramwen of Benin was deposed and sent into exile at Old Calabar in 1897; he died in exile in 1914. These were different events, and their dates and locations should not be confused.

**Industrial development:** It is too broad to say that no industries existed in West Africa during colonial rule. Nigeria had important pre-existing craft and manufacturing activities, including textile production. Research on northern Nigerian textiles finds that colonial trade and railway connections affected local producers differently: some areas experienced decline, while textile production remained resilient in other districts. A more accurate conclusion is that industrial development was uneven and that colonial economic priorities often favoured export production and trade over broad-based local industrial diversification.

**Cultural imperialism and political change:** Colonial schools, courts and official languages influenced local institutions and social life. These changes could create new opportunities, but they could also weaken or reshape local practices and place colonial standards above African institutions. Their effects varied by region and community.

## Summary

Indirect rule used selected African rulers and local institutions under British colonial supervision. Its operation differed between Western and Eastern Nigeria. The Oyo example shows that some rulers were checked by councils, while the use of warrant chiefs in parts of Eastern Nigeria could create problems of local legitimacy. The 1929 Women's War showed women's political agency and opposition to colonial practices. Mary Slessor's missionary work should not be confused with indirect rule.

Colonial rule brought constitutional, administrative, educational, transport and financial changes, but access and benefits were uneven. It also shaped export economies, altered local institutions and led to the removal of some African rulers. Good historical analysis uses accurate dates, recognizes local differences and considers both evidence and limitations.

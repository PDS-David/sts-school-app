## State — lines 642–654

The source describes a state as a politically organized population occupying a defined territory under an organized government. This is broadly consistent with statehood literature, but the source's phrase “free from external control” should be understood as external independence, not complete freedom from international obligations.

The 1933 Montevideo Convention on Rights and Duties of States provides a widely cited formulation:
- Article 1 lists a permanent population, defined territory, government and capacity to enter relations with other states.
- Article 3 states that a state's political existence is independent of recognition by other states.

The source lists recognition, membership/citizenship and law as additional characteristics. These may be important aspects of state administration and international relations, but they are not separate Article 1 qualifications. In particular, under the declaratory approach, recognition is not a constitutive requirement for statehood. The lesson should distinguish the conventional textbook list from the treaty formulation and acknowledge that statehood tests are debated in international law.

### Permanence — line 653

The source says that a state cannot cease to exist unless conquered in war. This is incorrect. International law recognizes state succession through dissolution, merger/unification, separation and other territorial changes. The Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia ceased to exist through dissolution; Yemen was formed by unification. Distinguish continuity of a state through changes of government from the possible extinction or replacement of a state as an international legal person.

## Nation — lines 655–659

The source identifies shared culture, origin, descent and political consciousness as elements of nationhood. It then says a nation possesses all attributes of a state except sovereignty. That is not a valid general definition:
- A **nation** is a political community bound by shared identity, history, public culture and mutual commitment/self-understanding. Nations may be civic, ethnic or mixed in character.
- A **state** is a legal-political entity with territory, population, government and international legal personality.
- A **nation-state** is a particular arrangement in which a state's political boundaries are understood to correspond substantially with a national community.

David Miller's *On Nationality* distinguishes nations from states and ethnic groups. Some nations do not have sovereign states, while many states contain multiple national communities. Shared descent is not a necessary condition for nationhood.

## Political culture — lines 660–674

The source's definition of political culture as the beliefs, values, attitudes, feelings and ideas that shape political behaviour is supported by the classic comparative-politics literature.

The source teaches all three orientations:
- **Cognitive:** knowledge and beliefs about the political system, institutions and officeholders.
- **Affective:** feelings toward the political system and its institutions.
- **Evaluative:** judgments about government performance, institutions and policies.

The order in the source is cognitive, evaluative, affective; the three concepts are all present. This corresponds to the cognitive, affective and evaluative orientation framework associated with Almond and Verba's *The Civic Culture* (1963).

### Determinants and Nigerian federalism — lines 668–674

The source identifies ethnicity, historical development, colonial experience, socio-economic structure and political instability as influences. These are plausible contextual factors, but they must not be treated as deterministic:
- Ethnic identity does not mean every member of a group has the same political culture.
- Urban/rural differences in education and participation are not fixed characteristics of all residents; access, resources, institutions and local histories matter.
- Political instability may affect trust and political attitudes, but effects vary by context.

The source's line 671 claim that Nigeria's federal structure and political beliefs/values were “copied from Britain” is too broad. British colonial administration and constitutional reforms influenced the development of Nigerian federalism, but it was not a simple copy of Britain's own constitutional system. The formal federal system was introduced through the **Lyttleton Constitution of 1954**, following constitutional conferences in 1953–1954. Colonial administrative structures mattered, while Nigerian nationalist groups, regional leaders and local political interests also shaped the constitutional process. The Senate Committee on Constitution Review's history and recent Oxford/Cambridge scholarship support this more balanced account.

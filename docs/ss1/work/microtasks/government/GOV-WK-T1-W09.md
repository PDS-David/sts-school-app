## Political socialization — lines 709–713

The source defines political socialization as learning the values, attitudes and beliefs of a political system and transmitting political culture across generations. This is broadly sound but should be widened:
- Political socialization is a **lifelong** process, beginning in childhood and continuing through adulthood.
- It occurs through formal instruction and informal observation, discussion, media and participation.
- It can reinforce existing orientations or lead people to revise them; it does not necessarily produce uniform loyalty, patriotism or agreement.
- It affects people as they learn about political life, not only those who already hold citizenship.

## Agents — lines 714–722

The source lists seven agents: family, school, peer groups, political parties, mass media, religious groups and pressure groups. The list is a useful introduction; their influence varies across individuals, communities and political systems. The media section names newspapers, radio, television and magazines; contemporary digital media can be added as an updated example without changing the source record.

### Religious groups — line 720

The source states broadly that politics and religion are inseparable and that religious groups direct members toward a political course. This should be qualified:
- Religious organizations can influence values, public debate, political knowledge and civic participation.
- Their roles differ by institution, context and issue; they do not all take the same political position.
- Members are not a uniform voting bloc and retain individual freedom of conscience and association.
- Nigeria's Constitution (1999, as revised 2023) provides that the state shall not adopt a religion as state religion (s.10), protects freedom of thought, conscience and religion (s.38), association (s.40), and freedom from specified forms of discrimination (s.42).

The lesson must describe religion as one possible agent of political socialization, not as a compulsory political authority or a universal rule. It should avoid naming or stereotyping religious communities and should present participation in public life neutrally.

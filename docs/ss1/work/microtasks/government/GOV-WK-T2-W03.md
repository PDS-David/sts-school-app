## Advantages of colonial rule — children 153–158

The source lists educational expansion and exposure to new ideas. University College Ibadan was established on 18 January 1948 as a college of the University of London and became an independent university in 1962. That date is correct. The corrected lesson should not imply that colonialism created education or knowledge from nothing: mission, Islamic and indigenous educational traditions predated or coexisted with colonial institutions.

## Disadvantages of colonial rule — children 160–161

The source discusses colonial cultural hierarchy, rural neglect and economic restructuring. These are important critiques, but:
- Portrayals of African cultures as inferior should be attributed to colonial racial/cultural ideology, not repeated as factual descriptions.
- Claims of rural neglect and urban concentration need specific territory, period and evidence.
- The source's assertion that Africans were “not ready” for the world economy is paternalistic. West Africa had precolonial local, regional and long-distance trade and diplomatic networks; colonial rule reoriented and intensified particular export relationships rather than introducing economic exchange for the first time.

## British/French comparison table — child 162

The table contrasts “INDIRECT RULE” with “POLICY OF ASSIMILATION” and asserts that:
- British administration used indirect rule while French administration used direct rule.
- British administration used African traditional chiefs, while French administration did not.
- Traditional rulers were the pivot of British indirect rule but not French assimilation.
- British administration used traditional laws/customs, while French administration did not.
- British indirect rule was not expensive, while French assimilation was expensive.

These contrasts are too categorical:
- “Assimilation” was an official French colonial ideology; it is not identical to every instance of direct administration.
- French administrations also used African chiefs and intermediaries in practice, though their roles and relationship to the colonial state differed.
- British administration was not uniformly indirect in every territory or period.
- Both empires adapted practice to local institutions, administrative resources and political circumstances.
- Cost comparisons require evidence from a named colony and period.

Michael Crowder's 1964 study notes that both systems could use chiefs, while the role of chiefs differed. Recent comparative research finds that empire-level policies interacted with precolonial institutions and local conditions. The table's layout and text disposition remain assigned to GOV-VIS-T2; this indexing note records only its content and conceptual limitations.

## Functions of the colonial governor — children 164–170

The source lists ceremonial functions, reporting to the Secretary of State, policy formulation, assent to bills, head-of-government responsibilities, emergency powers and the prerogative of mercy. These are plausible powers in particular colonial constitutional arrangements, but “the Governor in West Africa” is too broad. British West African colonies had distinct constitutions and legislative arrangements, and the governor's powers changed over time. A corrected lesson must name the colony, constitutional instrument and period for each power rather than presenting one uniform regional office.

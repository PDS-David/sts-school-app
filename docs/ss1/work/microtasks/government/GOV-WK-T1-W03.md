## Power and its forms

- **Power**, line 186: the source explains the capacity to influence or control decisions and the conduct of others. This objective is taught.
- **Forms of power**, line 189: political, physical, military and economic forms are introduced. The source's political-power account conflates the Constitution's allocation of institutional powers with electoral selection. These are related but different: a constitution establishes and distributes public powers; elections select some officeholders, while other offices are filled through constitutional appointment processes.
- The source's description of military power is a broad textbook category, not a statement of unlimited domestic authority. Keep any explanation at a civic, constitutional level.

## Sources of power

The source lists the Constitution, inheritance, coercion, charisma, position of authority and wealth. The following distinctions are required:
- A constitution allocates and limits public powers; an election is one means of selecting certain officeholders. They are not interchangeable descriptions of the same source.
- Inheritance can ground traditional authority in hereditary systems, but it is not a universal or ordinary source of public authority in every society.
- The source describes expert knowledge under “position of authority”. Expertise is more accurately a form of technical authority; it is not the same as holding an office.
- Charisma and wealth can influence political power, but the source's examples should be attributed and not treated as universal laws.
- The claim at line 198 that political power in Africa is derived from military overthrow is an overgeneralisation. Research documents coups as recurring historical episodes in particular countries and periods, but also describes uneven distribution, distinct waves and declines between waves. It does not justify treating coups as the continent-wide or normal source of political power. The corrected lesson must remain historical and non-operational.

## Authority and Weber

The source defines authority at line 207 as an accepted right to command or direct. That is a useful distinction from mere ability to compel compliance: Weber's account of legitimate domination considers whether a command is accepted as a valid norm.

At line 212 the source attributes three types to Max Weber. The attribution is broadly correct, but the precise formulation is **three ideal types of legitimate domination/authority**:
1. Traditional legitimacy — grounded in established custom.
2. Charismatic legitimacy — grounded in belief in a leader's exceptional personal qualities.
3. Legal-rational legitimacy — grounded in impersonal rules and the legality of office.

The source's “legal authority” should be corrected to **legal-rational authority**. These are ideal types and can appear in combination; they are not a complete list of every form of authority.

## Nigerian traditional rulers and military authority

The source's traditional-authority examples include Obas, Emirs and Obis. These are valid examples of Nigerian traditional titles, but their powers and formal roles are not uniform. The African Peer Review Mechanism's 2009 Nigeria Country Review Report describes traditional rulers' powers as disparate, notes that the 1999 Constitution did not allocate them a formal constitutional role, and explains that their political significance and local roles vary. This is a historical contextual source, not a current state-law survey. Any present-day claim about a traditional ruler's formal powers must be checked against the applicable state's current law.

The source's military-authority category must not imply a general or unlimited right to police the public. The 1999 Constitution (rev. 2023) separately establishes the Nigeria Police Force (s.214) and the Armed Forces (s.217). Section 217(2) sets out defined Armed Forces purposes, including external defence and territorial integrity, and aid to civil authorities to restore order when called upon by the President subject to legislation. This is a constitutional description only; no operational guidance is included.

The briefing records recent regional events while noting the longer-term decline in coups since 2000 and the rise in the last few years: [briefing](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9861/).

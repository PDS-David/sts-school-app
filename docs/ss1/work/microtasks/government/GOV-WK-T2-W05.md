## Clifford Constitution — children 212–234

The 1922 Nigeria (Legislative Council) Order in Council specifies the Governor as President, 26 official members, 15 nominated unofficial members and four elected unofficial members: three for Lagos and one for Calabar. The source's statement that three elected members represented Calabar is incorrect. The term “white officials” is imprecise; the Order describes official members by their colonial offices.

The Legislative Council's jurisdiction was confined to the Southern Provinces, including Lagos; the Governor legislated for the North by proclamation. The four elected seats were restricted to Lagos and Calabar, with a male franchise requiring annual income of £100 and residence in the relevant municipal area for at least one year. The first elections were held in 1923. The elective principle was introduced in Nigeria, not “for the first time in Africa.”

The source says the constitution established a new Executive Council. A national Executive Council already existed before 1922 and had no Nigerian members. The major constitutional change was the new Legislative Council replacing the Nigerian Council; do not conflate the two bodies.

The source groups the NNDP, NYM and NCNC, and later newspapers, as if they all emerged directly from the 1922 constitution. The NNDP followed in 1923; the NYM was formed in 1934; the NCNC in 1944; Lagos Daily News was founded in 1925 and the West African Pilot in 1937. The elective principle stimulated later political activity, but these organizations were founded at different times and were not created by the constitution itself.

## Richards Constitution — children 236–255

The constitution was made in 1946 and came into operation on 1 January 1947. It established a central Legislative Council for Nigeria and regional councils/assemblies for the North, West and East. The regional bodies were consultative/advisory and did not possess the same legislative authority as the central Legislative Council. The central council could legislate for the whole country.

The constitution's stated aims included promoting unity, accommodating regional diversity and increasing African participation. The source should distinguish those aims from its regional structure and later political consequences. It introduced regionalism and expanded regional representation, but it is too simple to say that it alone caused later ethnic suspicion or disunity.

The restricted elective franchise remained limited to Lagos and Calabar; the income threshold was reduced from £100 under Clifford to £50 under Richards. The source is broadly right that other Nigerians remained without direct electoral representation at this stage, but it should state the precise constitutional arrangement.

## Macpherson Constitution — children 257–276

The source correctly identifies the broad regional structure: Northern and Western legislatures were bicameral, while the Eastern legislature was unicameral. The central Council of Ministers comprised twelve members, four from each region. The source's regional franchise distinction is also broadly accurate: in the North voting was limited to male taxpayers, while women could vote in the East and West under the relevant regional arrangements.

The constitution followed extensive consultation, including the 1950 Ibadan conference. It increased Nigerian participation and gave regional assemblies legislative responsibilities, but “regional autonomy” should not be confused with full sovereignty or complete independence.

The Action Group was founded in 1951; it should not be presented as a direct result of the constitution. The source dates Anthony Enahoro's motion for self-government in 1956 to 1 April 1953; the motion was moved on **31 March 1953**. The subsequent 1953 Kano crisis contributed to the breakdown of political cooperation and constitutional negotiations. The Macpherson Constitution was replaced by the Lyttleton Constitution in 1954; the crisis should not be presented as an instantaneous one-day “total breakdown.”

## External references consulted

1. **Nigeria (Legislative Council) Order in Council, 1922**, primary constitutional instrument. Specifies the Governor as President, 26 official members, 15 nominated unofficial members, three elected Lagos members and one elected Calabar member: [full text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Nigeria_%28Legislative_Council%29_Order_in_Council_1922).
2. **Nigeria Senate Committee on Constitution Review, “Constitution History.”** Summarizes the Clifford, Richards and Macpherson constitutional sequence and the first elective system in Nigeria: [official timeline](https://sccr.gov.ng/?page_id=119).
3. **National Library of Nigeria / National Open University of Nigeria, POL 221: Nigerian Government and Politics I.** Course material on the Clifford, Richards and Macpherson constitutions: [repository record](https://nigeriareposit.nln.gov.ng/items/0b7754e5-981d-4a76-b598-efa2798649e5).
4. **“The Electorate and Elections,” *Nigerian Political Parties: Power in an Emergent African Nation*, De Gruyter.** Records the restrictive £100 Clifford franchise and the reduction to £50 under Richards: [chapter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1525/9780520339064-010/html).
5. **Jide Akanji, “The Elective Principle and Electoral Politics in Nigeria,” INEC conference paper.** Confirms the four elected seats (three Lagos, one Calabar), £100 income threshold and one-year residence requirement: [paper](https://www.inecnigeria.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Conference-Paper-by-Jide-Akanji.pdf).
6. **UK Parliament, Hansard, “Constitutional Developments in Nigeria,” 21 October 1953.** Describes the Macpherson-era Central Executive, with twelve ministers, four from each region: [Hansard debate](https://hansard.parliament.uk/lords/1953-10-21/debates/428268c2-ebb0-477a-98ad-d4ec7aabd87c).
7. **“British Colonial Policies and the Challenge of National Unity in Nigeria, 1914–2014,” *Southern Journal for Contemporary History*.** Discusses the Macpherson Constitution's 27-month operation, the March 1953 Enahoro motion and the subsequent crisis: [article](https://journals.co.za/doi/10.18820/24150509/SJCH47.v1.3).
8. **Anthony Enahoro, “Motion for Nigerian Independence by Chief Anthony Enahoro, 31st March, 1953.”** Provides the original motion text and date: [transcription](https://aclasses.org/motion-for-nigerian-independence-by-chief-anthony-enahoro-31st-march-1953/).

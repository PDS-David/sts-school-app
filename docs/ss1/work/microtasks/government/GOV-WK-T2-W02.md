## Western Nigeria — children 91–100

The source discusses the Alaafin of Oyo, limits on royal power, educated elites, taxation and regional political institutions. The Oyo example is useful but should be specific:
- The Alaafin's authority in the Oyo Empire was checked by institutions including the Oyo Mesi and other political/religious structures.
- Oyo's political arrangements should not be generalized to all Yoruba polities.
- The source's claim that most Yoruba people were Christians and that Christianity was less conservative than Islam is an unsupported religious stereotype. Yoruba religious life has included Christianity, Islam and indigenous traditions; religion should not be treated as a fixed explanation for political behaviour.
- The source's description of the absence of centralized administration should be qualified: centralization and local authority varied across Yoruba states and communities.

## Eastern Nigeria — children 102–111

The source correctly identifies the British appointment of warrant chiefs as a major feature of colonial administration in parts of Eastern Nigeria, but its explanation is too uniform:
- Many Igbo communities used decentralized political arrangements involving village councils, lineages, age grades and associations.
- Other communities, including Onitsha, Oguta and Umueri, had monarchic or more centralized institutions. “No traditional rulers” is therefore too broad.
- Warrant chiefs were colonial appointees and should not be equated with every customary authority.
- The source's claim that Christianity caused indirect rule to fail is unsupported and should be removed.

### The 1929 Women's War

The source links the failure of indirect rule to the “Aba women riot of 1929.” Historical accounts describe the Women's War/Rebellion as a multi-cause protest involving taxation, fears that taxation would be extended to women, warrant chiefs, native courts, market grievances and colonial administrative practices. It should be taught as an expression of women's political agency and resistance, not as a single-cause event or proof that the system failed uniformly across all Eastern Nigeria.

## Merits and demerits of indirect rule — children 113–125

The source lists administrative cost savings, use of local authorities, training of rulers, elite alienation, increased power of some rulers, divisions between rulers and educated elites, and the role of warrant chiefs. These effects varied by place and period:
- Indirect rule could use existing authorities, but colonial officials also changed, subordinated or created authorities.
- The appointment of warrant chiefs in communities without equivalent centralized offices created legitimacy problems.
- “Preserving traditions” was not neutral or complete; colonial rule altered institutions and power relations.
- Claims about low cost, ruler training, autocracy and divide-and-rule should be presented as possible effects, not universal outcomes.

The claim at child 117 that the indirect-rule system ended twin infanticide through Mary Slessor is incorrect. Mary Slessor was a Scottish Presbyterian missionary who arrived in Calabar in 1876 and died in 1915. She opposed the killing of twins in some communities, but her missionary work is not an outcome of indirect rule and should not be attributed to colonial administrative policy. A corrected lesson should acknowledge local families and community actors and avoid a colonial-saviour narrative.

## Positive impacts — children 127–132

The source lists elections, civil-service development, political parties, currency/banking and Western education. These developments need dated and qualified treatment:
- The 1922 Clifford Constitution introduced a limited elective principle in Nigeria. The first elections took place in 1923; representation was restricted to three seats for Lagos and one for Calabar and subject to a high income qualification.
- The Nigerian National Democratic Party was formed in 1923; political parties developed through Nigerian political activity as well as the colonial constitutional framework.
- The West African Currency Board was established in 1912 and began issuing currency in 1913.
- The Bank of British West Africa was founded in 1894. The source's reference to a “Colonial Bank” established in 1917 is not supported as written and needs the exact institution identified before correction.
- Formal education and civil-service institutions expanded under colonial rule, but their reach was uneven and interacted with pre-existing African institutions and local initiatives. Do not say colonialism alone created political development or education.

## Negative impacts — children 134–139

The source discusses economic dependence/exploitation, cultural imperialism, language differences, deportation of leaders and limited industrial development. These are significant historical topics but require qualification:
- Colonial economies were often organized around export production and metropolitan demand, but the absolute statement that no industries were built is too broad; industrial development varied by territory and sector.
- English/French language differences shaped institutions and communication, but ECOWAS was established in 1975 to promote regional integration and operates across English, French and Portuguese official languages. Language differences do not make antagonism inevitable.
- The slave trade, colonial forced labour and the later deportation of African rulers are distinct historical processes and should not be collapsed into one event.
- The source's claim that Oba Ovonramwen was exiled in 1896 is inaccurate: he was exiled to Calabar after the British punitive expedition against Benin in 1897 and died in 1914.
- Jaja of Opobo was deported in 1887, later permitted to return, and died on the journey home at Tenerife in 1891—not in the West Indies.

## External historical references consulted

1. **American Historical Association, “Riot or Rebellion? The Women’s Market Rebellion of 1929”** (1 May 2004). Provides historical context and contemporary accounts linking the protest to taxation, warrant chiefs and native courts: [resource](https://www.historians.org/resource/riot-or-rebellion-the-womens-market-rebellion-of-1929/).
2. **Afolabi A.S., “Tax and Women: A Review of the 1929 Owerri Province Insurrection in Colonial Nigeria,” *Social Evolution & History* 21(2)** (2022), pp. 13–32. Identifies multiple causes and the role of women, markets, native courts and warrant chiefs: [article](https://www.sociostudies.org/journal/articles/3342358/).
3. **Ebere Nwaubani, “Igbo Political Systems,” *Lagos Notes and Records* 12(1)** (2006). Emphasizes the diversity and complexity of precolonial Igbo political systems: [article](https://www.ajol.info/index.php/lnr/article/view/46479/0).
4. **Toyin Falola, “The Land Has Changed: History, Society and Gender in Colonial Eastern Nigeria,” University of Calgary Press.** Explains diversity in Igbo political arrangements, including decentralized and monarchic communities: [chapter](https://ucp.manifoldapp.org/read/the-land-has-changed-history-society-and-gender-in-colonial-eastern-nigeria/section/59069966-12d9-4ce2-af83-866036fcc227).
5. **Dictionary of African Christian Biography, “Mary Slessor (B)”**. Records her missionary work in Calabar, her work with families and twins, and her death in 1915: [biography](https://dacb.org/stories/nigeria/slessor2-mary/).
6. **Cambridge University Press, “Removing Rulers in the Niger Delta, 1887–1897,” *Imperial Incarceration*.** Documents Ovonramwen's exile to Old Calabar in 1897 and death in 1914; also records Jaja's exile and death at Tenerife in July 1891: [chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/imperial-incarceration/removing-rulers-in-the-niger-delta-18871897/A98E79E85212C0EB9C06C961C4E9BA12).
7. **Oxford Research Encyclopedia of African History, “Ozurumba Mbanaso or King Jaja of Opobo”** (25 February 2019). Confirms Jaja's exile in the West Indies and death while trying to return home in 1891: [entry](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61663/chapter-abstract/553488432).
8. **Nigeria Senate Committee on Constitution Review, “Constitution History.”** Confirms the 1922 Clifford Constitution and its limited first electoral system: [official timeline](https://sccr.gov.ng/?page_id=119).
9. **West African Currency Board historical study**, based on WACB annual reports. Records establishment in 1912 and currency issuance beginning 26 June 1913; the same study notes the Bank of British West Africa was founded in 1894: [study](https://sites.krieger.jhu.edu/iae/files/2017/04/Krus-Schuler_Currency_Board_Financial_Statements_2014.12.21.pdf).
10. **ECOWAS, “History” and “Basic Information.”** Confirms establishment by the Treaty of Lagos on 28 May 1975 and the region's three official languages: English, French and Portuguese: [history](https://www.ecowas.int/history-2/); [basic information](https://www.ecowas.int/basic-information/).

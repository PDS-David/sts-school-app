# Week 5 corrected supplement: Communalism and feudalism

**Class:** SS 1  
**Subject:** Government  
**Term/week:** First Term, Week 5  
**Use:** This original supplement replaces the Week Five teaching row for delivery. It does not reproduce the assigned textbook pages.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, a student should be able to:
1. explain communalism as an idealized political and economic model and describe its possible features without treating one pattern of landholding as universal;
2. explain feudalism as a contested historical category associated with some medieval Western European arrangements, including fiefs and lord-vassal obligations;
3. distinguish a vassal from a serf or villein and distinguish both from a modern proletarian/wage worker; and
4. assess claims about the merits, criticisms and decline of feudalism using dated, region-specific historical evidence.

## 1. Communalism as a model

**Communalism**, as used in this lesson, is a political and economic model that emphasises common ownership or management of important resources, cooperation and the use of resources for shared benefit. It is an idealized model for comparing ways of organising economic and community life. It should not be treated as a single system followed by every African community or as a description of current Nigerian land law.

### Possible features of the communal model

- **Common or shared rights:** some land or resources may be held or managed collectively by a community or group.
- **Cooperation:** members may contribute to shared work or community projects.
- **Common interest:** decisions may consider the needs of the group as well as those of individual households.
- **Shared use or distribution:** the community may have rules for access to shared resources and for distributing benefits.
- **Customary institutions:** local rules and recognised community institutions may help determine who can use particular resources.
- **Exchange:** barter may occur, but it is not necessarily the only form of exchange. Communities may also use money and participate in wider markets.

The source's idealized list includes the absence of private property and social classes. Those claims should not be generalised to all communal arrangements. Land-tenure research documents overlapping rights: common, family, household, individual, state and other interests may coexist in one area. A person may have individual use or inheritance rights within a wider customary system of community ownership or oversight.

**Important distinction:** communal land tenure is not automatically the same as a complete communal political/economic system. A community may recognise shared rights to some land while also recognising private homes, household farms, individual use rights or commercial exchange. Actual arrangements vary by place, community, history and applicable law.

## 2. Feudalism as a historical category

**Feudalism** is a term historians use for certain social, political and economic arrangements, especially in parts of medieval Western Europe. The term is retrospective and contested: historians do not all use it in exactly the same way, and the institutions varied by region and period. It should not be described as one uniform system practised identically throughout Europe or the world.

In a narrow feudo-vassalic model, relationships among lords and vassals involved grants of land or rights, loyalty and service. Political authority could be layered rather than concentrated in one central institution. These relationships existed alongside other institutions, local customs and forms of government.

### Fief, lord and vassal

- A **lord** was a person who held authority or rights within a particular lordship and could grant a fief or other benefit under the relevant arrangement.
- A **fief** was a grant of land, income or another right from a lord to a vassal. The form of a fief and the duties attached to it varied by place and period; it was not always simply an unrestricted gift of land.
- A **vassal** was a person who entered a recognised relationship with a lord, commonly involving loyalty and specified services in return for a fief, protection or other benefits. Vassals were often members of the landed elite, although relationships differed.

A simplified picture in which every king directly grants land to every subject is misleading. Relationships could be layered: a lord might have a superior lord, and not every person in a medieval community was a vassal.

### Serfs or villeins are not vassals

A **serf** or **villein** was a peasant whose legal and customary status tied their work and obligations to an estate or lordship. A serf might owe labour, rent or other customary dues and could face restrictions on movement or choice. Serfdom was not identical to chattel slavery or ordinary paid service; it was a distinct legal and customary status, and its precise form varied by time and place.

A **vassal**, by contrast, was a participant in a lord-vassal relationship, often involving landholding or other rights and reciprocal obligations. A vassal was not simply a landless farm worker. Serfdom and vassalage were different statuses and should not be used as synonyms.

### Serfs and modern proletarians are also different

A **proletarian**, in modern social and economic analysis, is a wage worker who depends mainly on selling their labour for wages and does not control the productive assets needed for independent production. A serf's obligations arose from a medieval estate and customary/legal status; a proletarian's position arises from a wage-labour relationship in a market economy. Neither term should be used as a synonym for the other.

## 3. Features often associated with feudal arrangements

The following are features associated with some feudo-vassalic arrangements, not a checklist that applied identically everywhere.

1. **Land-based relationships:** land, income or rights could be granted in return for service or loyalty.
2. **Hierarchy:** relationships could be organised in layers of lords, vassals and other dependants.
3. **Reciprocal obligations:** a lord and vassal could owe each other duties, although the obligations and balance of power varied.
4. **Local or fragmented authority:** political and judicial powers could be divided among local lords, rulers, towns, churches and other institutions.
5. **Peasant obligations:** serfs or villeins could owe labour, rent or customary dues connected with an estate.
6. **Social hierarchy:** status and access to land or authority were unequal, although arrangements varied over time and place.

## 4. Merits and criticisms: arguments that need context

Historical accounts sometimes present feudal arrangements as providing local order, protection, access to land or a framework of reciprocal duties. These are arguments about possible effects in particular settings, not universal benefits. Protection offered by a lord could coexist with unequal obligations and restrictions on dependants.

Criticisms include unequal status, exploitation, heavy or compulsory obligations, limited freedom for some peasants, and divided or competing centres of authority. Claims that feudal society was always at war or that no national government existed anywhere are too broad. Conditions differed between regions and across centuries.

The claim that feudalism straightforwardly laid the foundation for capitalism is also debated. Historians study different transitions from feudal institutions to capitalist economies; there was no single inevitable path or one event that transformed all of Europe at the same time.

## 5. The decline of serfdom and changes in feudal institutions

Industrialisation did **not** by itself cause the end of feudalism everywhere. The decline of serfdom and the broader transformation of feudal institutions happened at different times in different places.

For example, historical research documents a substantial decline of serfdom in late medieval England from approximately **1300 to 1500**, before the Industrial Revolution. Historians examine several interacting explanations, including changes in labour demand and wages, demographic and economic pressures, manumission, migration, peasant resistance, legal changes and the gradual commutation of customary obligations.

This English example cannot be used as a single timetable for all of Europe. The decline of serfdom is also not identical to the much broader and debated transition from feudal institutions to capitalist economies. Industrialisation was relevant to later economic transformations, but it was not the sole cause or one simultaneous endpoint for all medieval arrangements.

## 7. Quick recap

- Communalism is presented here as an idealized model of shared resources and common benefit, not a universal description of communities or current law.
- Communal tenure can coexist with individual, household and other rights.
- Feudalism is a contested historical term for varied arrangements, especially in parts of medieval Western Europe.
- A fief was a grant of land, income or rights; a vassal entered a lord-vassal relationship with reciprocal obligations.
- Serfs/villeins were peasant tenants with estate-related obligations; they were not the same as vassals.
- A modern proletarian is a wage worker; that status is different from medieval serfdom.
- Claims about order, protection, capitalism, war and insecurity must be attributed and limited to particular contexts.
- The decline of English serfdom from about 1300–1500 preceded industrialisation and had multiple causes.

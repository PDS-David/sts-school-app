## Lesson body
COLONIAL ADMINISTRATION: HISTORICAL BACKGROUND AND INDIRECT RULE IN NORTHERN NIGERIA

Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- describe the purpose and limits of the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885;
- explain why the later partition and establishment of colonial administrations took place over time;
- give dated examples of West African independence;
- identify economic, political, strategic and ideological motives for colonial expansion;
- distinguish pre-existing slavery and the transatlantic slave trade from later colonial forced-labour systems;
- explain indirect rule, Lord Lugard's role in Northern Nigeria, and the administrative and fiscal purposes of Native Authorities;
- describe differences between emirate and non-emirate political institutions without religious or literacy stereotypes.

1. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND: THE BERLIN CONFERENCE

The Berlin Conference took place in Berlin from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885. It was hosted by German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck and attended by representatives of fourteen states. The conference discussed trade and navigation in the Congo and Niger regions and agreed rules that European powers would use when making and notifying claims to African territory.

The conference did not draw all the borders of Africa or simply divide every West African country among the participants at one meeting. Its fourteen participants were not all colonial powers in West Africa. It created an international framework for European claims and helped reduce disputes among the European powers. The later partition of Africa took place over time through diplomatic agreements, treaties, military conquest, occupation and the creation of colonial administrations. African societies were not represented at the conference table.

The Berlin Conference therefore forms part of the history of the Scramble for Africa, but it is not the same event as the later conquest and administration of each territory.

2. PARTITION, COLONIAL RULE AND INDEPENDENCE

European colonial control expanded at different times and in different ways. Some coastal territories were occupied before the Berlin Conference; other areas were conquered or brought under colonial administration later. Liberia remained formally independent, although its history and external relationships were affected by European and American power.

There was no single date when all West Africa became colonial or when all colonial rule ended. Independence came at different times. Examples include:
- Ghana: 1957;
- Nigeria: 1960;
- Sierra Leone: 1961;
- The Gambia: 1965;
- Guinea-Bissau: declared independence in 1973 and received Portuguese recognition in 1974;
- Cabo Verde: 1975.

These years show why the source's broad description of colonial rule ending in the 1950s is inaccurate. The timing and path to independence differed among territories.

Walter Rodney's book, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, argues that colonial extraction and unequal economic relationships contributed to African underdevelopment. This is a major historical and political-economic interpretation. When using it, attribute the argument to Rodney and support specific claims with evidence; do not present one sentence as a complete explanation of every territory's history. This Week One lesson introduces the argument only; it does not claim to complete the scheme's balanced colonial-impacts objective, which is treated in Week Two.

3. REASONS FOR EUROPEAN COLONIAL EXPANSION

European powers had several motives, and their importance varied by empire and territory.

Economic motives
- Industrial economies sought raw materials such as palm produce, cocoa, rubber, minerals and other commodities.
- Colonial territories could be treated as markets for manufactured goods.
- Governments and companies sought control over trade routes, investment, land and revenue.

Political and strategic motives
- European states competed for territory, prestige and strategic positions.
- Control of ports, rivers and transport routes could support commerce and military power.
- Governments sought to prevent rival European powers from gaining influence in areas they considered important.

Religious and ideological motives
- Missionary organisations sought to spread Christianity and establish schools, churches and medical work.
- Some European politicians and writers claimed that colonisation was a “civilizing mission”. This was a colonial ideology that portrayed European culture as superior and was used to justify domination. It was not an objective fact that African societies lacked civilisation or history.
- Missionary activity and colonial government sometimes cooperated, but they were not the same institution and did not always have identical aims.

The First Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the eighteenth century, commonly dated from about 1760 to 1840. It increased demand for raw materials, markets and investment opportunities. It did not begin in the seventeenth century as the original source states.

4. SLAVERY AND COLONIAL LABOUR: DO NOT CONFUSE THE SYSTEMS

Forms of slavery and dependent labour existed in African societies before European arrival and before the transatlantic slave trade. Their forms varied across societies and periods. The transatlantic slave trade developed from the fifteenth century onward and expanded as European empires colonised the Americas and demanded forced labour.

It is therefore inaccurate to say that Europeans first introduced slavery into Africa in order to maintain colonies. The transatlantic slave trade and later formal colonial administration are different historical developments.

Colonial governments and businesses later used coercive labour systems in parts of West Africa, including forced labour, compulsory public works, recruitment pressure and taxation policies that could push people towards wage work or migrant labour. These systems were not identical to the transatlantic slave trade, but they could still be coercive and exploitative. Africans resisted such practices, and colonial labour policies changed over time.

5. MEANING OF INDIRECT RULE

Indirect rule was a form of colonial administration in which British officials governed through selected local rulers, councils or other Native Authority agents. The local authorities carried out specified administrative tasks under colonial supervision. They did not possess full independence from the colonial government.

Sir Frederick Lugard, later Lord Lugard, played a major role in developing and applying this system in Northern Nigeria. He served as the first High Commissioner of the Northern Nigeria Protectorate from 1900 to 1906 and later became Governor-General of the amalgamated Nigeria in 1914. The Northern Nigerian model developed during Lugard’s High Commissionership (1900–1906) and was supported by a sequence of legal proclamations. One historical account identifies the Native Courts Proclamation (1900), Native Revenue Proclamation (1906) and Native Authority (Enforcement) Proclamation (1907). Contemporary Northern Nigeria annual reports also record Native Courts and Native Revenue proclamations in 1906 and describe the 1907–1908 administration as governing through native rulers under British Residents. The later Native Courts, Native Authority and Native Treasury Ordinances of 1914–1916 were used to extend the northern/Lugardian model into Southern Nigeria after amalgamation; they did not mark the beginning of indirect rule in Northern Nigeria. This chronology distinguishes the development of the northern system from its later extension. Indirect rule was not invented from nothing by one person, and it was not unique to Britain; other empires used forms of indirect administration in different territories.

6. WHY THE BRITISH USED INDIRECT RULE

Administrative and fiscal reasons included:
- limited numbers of British officials available to administer large territories;
- the cost of maintaining a large direct colonial administration;
- the use of existing local institutions and authorities where colonial officials considered them useful;
- collection of taxes and other public revenue through Native Authorities and treasuries;
- maintaining local administration and order through intermediaries;
- language and communication barriers between colonial officials and local communities.

British officials described the system as a way to govern through local institutions, but this did not mean that they simply preserved local customs without change. Colonial officials supervised, reorganised and sometimes strengthened or weakened local authorities to serve colonial objectives. Taxation, revenue and political control were central concerns.

7. INDIRECT RULE IN NORTHERN NIGERIA

Northern Nigeria contained different political systems. In areas influenced by the Sokoto Caliphate and other emirate structures, there were established hierarchies of emirs, district heads, local officials, courts and treasuries. British officials recognised and reorganised parts of these systems as Native Authorities. Emirs and other officials could be responsible for local administration and tax collection, but they operated under British supervision and could be affected by colonial appointment and control.

Northern Nigeria was not made up only of emirates. Some communities had decentralised political institutions, councils, village authorities or other arrangements rather than a single emir exercising authority over a large territory. Communities in areas such as parts of the Jos Plateau and Tiv areas had different political histories. British officials sometimes misunderstood these institutions or appointed agents who did not have the support or legitimacy of the people they were expected to govern. Over time, administrative arrangements changed and councils were used in some areas.

The emirate model therefore cannot be treated as the political system of every Northern Nigerian community. Even within emirates, the balance between emirs, councils, district officials and colonial officers varied.

8. ROLES OF TRADITIONAL AUTHORITIES UNDER INDIRECT RULE

Depending on the locality and period, traditional rulers and Native Authorities could:
- collect local taxes and account for revenue through Native Treasuries;
- communicate colonial instructions to local communities;
- administer local affairs through district, village and other local officials;
- operate Native Courts and deal with local disputes under colonial rules;
- regulate local markets, allocate land and help provide or maintain local roads and services;
- support local administration and public order under colonial supervision.

These roles were not identical everywhere. A Native Authority was a colonial administrative institution, not simply the unchanged traditional government of a community. Some traditional leaders retained influence; others were appointed, removed or reorganised by colonial officers. A Native Authority should not be confused with a legislative council. It is inaccurate to claim that all traditional rulers were members of colonial legislative councils.

9. IMPORTANT CORRECTIONS TO COMMON GENERALISATIONS

- Islam was influential in many emirate areas of Northern Nigeria, but the region also included non-emirate communities and people of different religious traditions. Islam does not teach a single political attitude that explains the success of indirect rule.
- It is not accurate to claim that people obeyed colonial rule because they were Muslim or because Islam taught political submissiveness.
- Literacy levels varied and cannot be used as a general explanation for the success of indirect rule. Political institutions, colonial coercion, local authority, taxation and local responses are more useful historical factors.
- Some emirate systems had centralised rulers, while other communities had councils or more decentralised institutions. Neither description applies to every community.
- The word “success” should be qualified. It often reflects the perspective of colonial administrators. The system could help colonial authorities collect revenue and administer territory, but it also changed local institutions and generated resistance.

10. SUMMARY

The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885 established rules for European claims and trade/navigation; it did not draw all African borders or create every colonial administration. Colonial conquest and administration unfolded over time, and West African territories gained independence on different dates. European expansion had economic, political, strategic, religious and ideological motives. Slavery predated European arrival, while colonial forced-labour systems were later and distinct. In Northern Nigeria, indirect rule used varied local institutions as Native Authorities under British supervision for administrative and fiscal purposes. The region included both emirate and non-emirate political systems, and no religious or literacy stereotype can explain the history.

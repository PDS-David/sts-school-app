# Week 3 corrected supplement: Power and authority

**Class:** SS 1  
**Subject:** Government  
**Term/week:** First Term, Week 3  
**Use:** This original supplement replaces the Week Three teaching row for delivery. It does not reproduce the assigned textbook pages.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, a student should be able to:
1. explain power and distinguish its forms from its sources;
2. distinguish power from legitimate authority and identify Weber's three ideal types of legitimate authority; and
3. distinguish Weber's typology from the additional textbook categories and explain the qualified roles of traditional rulers and the constitutional distinction between the Nigeria Police Force and the Armed Forces.

## 1. Meaning of power

In Government, **power** is the capacity of a person, group or institution to influence decisions and behaviour or to secure compliance. Power may be exercised through lawful authority, control of resources, expertise, persuasion or coercion. It does not always involve physical force.

Power and authority are related but not identical. A person may be able to make others comply without having a recognised right to command them. That person has power, but the legitimacy of the command may be disputed.

## 2. Forms of power

The source lists four forms of power. This is a textbook classification; the forms can overlap and are not a complete list of every way influence operates.

1. **Political power:** the capacity to influence public decisions and the direction of government. In Nigeria, the Constitution allocates legislative, executive and judicial powers to institutions. Elections select certain officeholders and provide a democratic mandate; they do not themselves create or redistribute the constitutional powers of the three organs.
2. **Physical power:** influence associated with physical capacity. In public life, any use of force is constrained by law and constitutional rights. This lesson treats the category only as a general concept and gives no operational instructions.
3. **Military power:** the organised defence capacity of a state. It is not a general or unlimited authority to police the public. Nigeria's Constitution separately establishes the Nigeria Police Force in section 214 and provides for the Armed Forces in section 217, with defined constitutional responsibilities.
4. **Economic power:** influence arising from control of wealth, employment, investment, production, finance or other economic resources. Economic resources can affect political choices, but wealth alone does not create a lawful right to hold public office or command others.

## 3. Sources of power

The source lists six possible sources. They should be understood as possible ways influence or authority may arise, not as universal rules applying in every society.

1. **Constitution:** a constitution establishes public institutions, allocates their powers and sets legal limits. It is a source of lawful institutional authority. An election is different: it is a method for selecting certain officeholders under the constitutional system.
2. **Inheritance:** in a hereditary system, a position may pass through a recognised family or customary succession. This can support traditional authority, but inheritance is not a universal or ordinary source of public office in every society.
3. **Coercion:** a person or institution may obtain compliance through threats or compulsion. Coercion can produce obedience, but obedience caused by fear is not by itself proof that the command is legitimate.
4. **Charisma:** people may follow a leader because they believe the leader has exceptional personal qualities. Charismatic influence depends on followers' belief and may change over time.
5. **Position or office:** a person may exercise powers because the law or an organisation assigns responsibilities to that office. The authority belongs to the office within its lawful limits, not to the officeholder without limit.
6. **Wealth:** control of economic resources may influence political debate, political campaigns or policy choices. Wealth may create influence, but it does not automatically confer lawful authority.

**Expertise is not the same as holding office.** Knowledge and professional skill may give a person **technical authority** in a field. That is different from the legal authority attached to a public office.

**Elections and constitutional power must not be confused.** Elections select representatives or other officeholders and can give them a democratic mandate. The Constitution determines the powers and limits of the offices they occupy.

## 4. Meaning of authority

**Authority** is a recognised or legitimate right to give directions, make decisions or issue commands within a particular relationship or office. It is more than the ability to make someone obey. Authority may be recognised because of law, custom or accepted personal leadership.

The distinction is important:
- **Power** asks whether a person or institution can influence decisions or secure compliance.
- **Authority** asks whether that person or institution is recognised as having a legitimate right to give the direction.
- Coercion may produce compliance without legitimacy. Lawful authority should operate within its legal limits and respect rights.

## 5. Weber's three ideal types of legitimate authority

The sociologist **Max Weber** described three **ideal types** of legitimate domination or authority. An ideal type is an analytical model used to understand social life; real institutions may combine features of more than one type.

1. **Traditional authority:** legitimacy rests on accepted customs and long-established practices. A traditional ruler may be recognised because the community accepts a customary system of succession and leadership.
2. **Charismatic authority:** legitimacy rests on followers' belief in a leader's exceptional personal qualities or mission. It depends on recognition by followers and may be less stable when that belief changes.
3. **Legal-rational authority:** legitimacy rests on impersonal rules and the lawful powers attached to an office. Officials are expected to act within established procedures and legal limits.

The precise term is **legal-rational authority**, not merely “legal authority”. Weber's three ideal types are not the same as every category in the textbook list below.

## 6. Additional textbook categories of authority

The source also gives seven categories. These are a separate teaching taxonomy, not Weber's three ideal types.

- **Political authority:** lawful authority exercised by political officeholders within the powers assigned to their offices.
- **Military authority:** command within the armed forces under the Constitution and applicable law. It does not create an unlimited domestic policing power.
- **Traditional authority:** authority recognised through customary institutions and practices.
- **Delegated authority:** a power that a lawful superior body or statute assigns to another official or institution, within stated limits.
- **Technical authority:** influence or recognition based on expertise and professional knowledge. Expertise does not automatically give someone a public office.
- **Judicial authority:** the authority of courts and judges to interpret and apply the law and decide cases.
- **Administrative authority:** powers assigned by law or lawful rules to public administrators to manage public programmes and services.

Do not mix these seven textbook categories with Weber's three ideal types. For example, “delegated” and “technical” are not additional Weberian ideal types.

## 7. Traditional rulers in Nigeria

Oba, Emir and Obi are examples of traditional titles used in different Nigerian communities. They do not describe one uniform national office. The history, customary role and formal responsibilities of traditional rulers differ by community, state and period.

The 2009 African Peer Review Mechanism report on Nigeria is useful historical context: it describes traditional rulers' powers as diverse and notes that the 1999 Constitution did not allocate them a uniform formal constitutional role at that time. This report is not a current survey of every state law. Any claim about a traditional ruler's present formal duties must be checked against the applicable state's current law and relevant custom. Do not assume that every Oba, Emir or Obi has the same powers.

## 8. Nigeria Police Force and Armed Forces: a constitutional distinction

The Constitution treats these institutions separately:

- **Nigeria Police Force:** section 214 provides for a national police force. Its role is public policing and internal law enforcement under the Constitution and applicable law.
- **Armed Forces:** section 217 provides for the Armed Forces of the Federation and sets out their constitutional purposes, including defence of Nigeria and protection of its territorial integrity. Section 217(2)(c) provides for aid to civil authorities to restore order when called upon by the President, subject to the conditions stated there.

The Armed Forces do not have an unlimited, general policing mandate. The Police Force and Armed Forces have distinct legal responsibilities. This is a civic and constitutional distinction only; it does not describe operational methods.

## 9. Military coups: historical events, not a general source of political power

Military coups have occurred in particular countries and periods, including in parts of Africa. Historical studies describe uneven patterns and different periods; they do not support the claim that political power across Africa normally or universally comes from military overthrow.

A coup is an unconstitutional seizure of political authority, not a lawful or ordinary route to public office. In constitutional government, public authority is exercised through lawful institutions and procedures. This lesson treats coups only as a historical and constitutional topic and does not discuss how they are carried out.

## 10. Quick recap

- Power is the capacity to influence decisions or behaviour; authority is a recognised right to command.
- The four textbook forms are political, physical, military and economic power.
- The six possible sources are constitution, inheritance, coercion, charisma, position/office and wealth.
- Elections select certain officeholders; the Constitution allocates and limits institutional powers.
- Weber's three ideal types are traditional, charismatic and legal-rational authority.
- Political, military, traditional, delegated, technical, judicial and administrative authority are a separate textbook taxonomy.
- Traditional rulers' roles differ by community, state, period and applicable law.
- Nigeria's Police Force and Armed Forces are constitutionally distinct.
- Coups are historical events in particular countries and periods, not a normal or universal source of political power.

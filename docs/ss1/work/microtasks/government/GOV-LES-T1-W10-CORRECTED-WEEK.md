## Lesson body
TYPES AND CHARACTERISTICS OF GOVERNMENT: UNITARY AND FEDERAL SYSTEMS

Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- explain the meaning of unitary and federal systems of government;
- compare how constitutional authority is distributed in the two systems;
- describe common features, possible reasons for adoption, advantages and disadvantages without treating them as guaranteed outcomes;
- distinguish unitary countries with devolution or regional autonomy from federations;
- identify Nigeria, the United States, India and Russia as constitutionally federal examples while recognising differences in practice;
- explain Nigeria's Federal Character Principle and the role of the Federal Character Commission.

1. MEANING OF A UNITARY SYSTEM

A unitary system is a form of government in which ultimate constitutional authority is concentrated in the national or central government. The central government has the final constitutional authority over the country as a whole. It may create local authorities or devolve some responsibilities to regional or local institutions, but those arrangements do not make the country a federation with constitutionally co-equal constituent states.

A unitary country can still have local councils, regional assemblies or devolved administrations. The important question is whether those units possess constitutionally protected powers as constituent units of a federation, or exercise powers under arrangements controlled by the central constitutional system.

Examples: The United Kingdom, France and Italy are generally classified as unitary states. Their arrangements are not identical:
- The United Kingdom has devolved institutions in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. The UK Parliament retains ultimate legal authority, although political conventions affect how it uses that authority.
- France is an indivisible republic under its Constitution and has decentralised local authorities. It has a written Constitution, constitutional review and a formal amendment procedure.
- Italy is one and indivisible but recognises local autonomies and regions with constitutionally defined powers. It also has a written Constitution and a formal amendment procedure.

Therefore, a unitary system does not always mean that every decision is made in the capital, and it does not always have a flexible constitution or parliamentary supremacy.

2. COMMON FEATURES OF A UNITARY SYSTEM

Common features may include:
1. Ultimate constitutional authority rests with the central government.
2. There is no federal constitutional division of sovereignty between the centre and constituent states.
3. The central legislature can make laws for the country as a whole, subject to the constitution.
4. Local or regional bodies may exercise powers delegated or devolved within the unitary constitutional arrangement.
5. National policy can be coordinated from the centre.

These are broad characteristics, not a checklist that every unitary country follows in exactly the same way. A written constitution can be rigid or flexible. Parliamentary supremacy is a feature of the United Kingdom's constitutional tradition, not a universal feature of all unitary states.

3. POSSIBLE REASONS FOR ADOPTING A UNITARY SYSTEM

A country may prefer a unitary arrangement for reasons such as:
- easier coordination of national policy through one central constitutional authority;
- reducing the cost or duplication of some government structures;
- promoting consistent national standards where common rules are appropriate;
- making responsibility for national decisions clearer;
- coordinating resources and emergency responses through central institutions.

A small territory or a shared national identity may influence a country's choice, but neither small size nor cultural homogeneity is a constitutional requirement for a unitary system. Efficiency and accountability depend on how institutions are designed and managed; they are not automatic results of centralisation.

4. POSSIBLE ADVANTAGES OF A UNITARY SYSTEM

A unitary system may:
- reduce the cost of maintaining parallel government institutions in some circumstances;
- allow faster national decisions when central coordination is effective;
- promote consistent national policies and standards;
- make responsibility for national decisions easier to identify;
- coordinate national resources and emergency responses.

These are possible advantages. A central government can also become overloaded, make decisions slowly or overlook local needs. The outcome depends on administrative capacity, accountability and the quality of public institutions.

5. POSSIBLE DISADVANTAGES OF A UNITARY SYSTEM

A unitary system may:
- concentrate too much power at the centre and create a risk of authoritarian rule if checks are weak;
- overload central institutions with decisions that could be handled locally;
- reduce local initiative when local bodies have too little authority;
- make government less responsive to local conditions when decision-makers are distant;
- fail to represent minority or regional interests adequately if the centre is not inclusive.

A unitary system does not automatically produce dictatorship or unemployment. These are possible risks or claims that require evidence in a particular country and period.

6. MEANING OF A FEDERAL SYSTEM

A federal system is a form of government in which the Constitution distributes governmental powers between a national or federal government and constituent units, such as states or provinces. Each level has powers protected by the Constitution and can act directly on people within its area of responsibility.

The federal and constituent governments do not simply receive all their authority as revocable instructions from the centre. Their powers come from the constitutional arrangement. Courts and other constitutional institutions help resolve disputes about which level may act in a particular matter.

Federalism is different from ordinary administrative decentralisation. A central government may delegate tasks to local offices in a unitary state; that delegation alone does not make the country a federation.

7. FEDERALISM IN DIFFERENT COUNTRIES

Nigeria
Section 2(2) of the 1999 Constitution describes Nigeria as a Federation consisting of States and the Federal Capital Territory. Section 4 and the Second Schedule divide legislative powers between the National Assembly and State Houses of Assembly through the Exclusive and Concurrent Legislative Lists and State residual powers. The Fourth Schedule also describes local-government functions; local governments are important, but they are not constituent states of the Federation.

United States
The United States has a federal Constitution that gives specified powers to the federal government and reserves other powers to the states or the people under the Tenth Amendment. The Supremacy Clause in Article VI governs conflicts between valid federal law and state law.

India
Article 1 of the Constitution describes India as a “Union of States”. The Supreme Court of India describes its constitutional system as federal, with legislative powers allocated between the Union and the States through the Union, State and Concurrent Lists. The Union has significant powers, so India's federal arrangement is more centralised than some other federations.

Russia
Article 1 of the Russian Constitution describes the Russian Federation as a federative state, and Article 5 sets out its federal structure. Constitutional classification does not by itself prove that regional governments exercise strong practical autonomy. Comparative research describes contemporary centre–regional relations as highly centralised, with regional governors strongly subordinated to the federal centre.

These examples show that federations share a constitutional distribution of powers but can differ greatly in their degree of centralisation and in how their institutions operate.

8. COMMON FEATURES OF A FEDERAL SYSTEM

Common features may include:
1. A written and supreme Constitution that allocates powers between levels of government.
2. Constituent units with constitutionally protected responsibilities.
3. A federal government and state/provincial governments, each with defined functions.
4. Courts or other constitutional mechanisms for resolving disputes over powers.
5. Intergovernmental cooperation and coordination in matters of shared responsibility.

The precise lists of powers, financial arrangements and degree of state autonomy differ between federations.

9. POSSIBLE REASONS FOR ADOPTING A FEDERAL SYSTEM

A country may adopt federalism to:
- accommodate regional, cultural, linguistic or other forms of diversity;
- bring some decisions closer to the people affected by them;
- share constitutional power and reduce excessive concentration at one level;
- allow constituent units to address local priorities within the Constitution;
- pool resources for common national purposes while retaining regional self-government;
- create constitutional safeguards for participation and representation.

A large territory or diverse population may influence the choice, but neither is a universal requirement. Federalism is a constitutional choice, not a formula that automatically solves diversity or development challenges.

10. POSSIBLE MERITS OF A FEDERAL SYSTEM

Federalism may:
- distribute power and reduce the risk of excessive concentration at one level;
- allow state governments to respond to local needs;
- encourage policy experimentation and learning between states;
- provide constitutional space for regional participation and minority safeguards;
- bring some public decisions closer to residents;
- combine national coordination with regional self-government.

These benefits depend on fair institutions, resources, accountability and respect for constitutional limits. Federalism does not automatically prevent dictatorship or guarantee minority protection.

11. POSSIBLE DEMERITS OF A FEDERAL SYSTEM

Federalism may:
- create disagreements about which level has authority over a matter;
- slow decisions when governments must negotiate or coordinate;
- cost more where institutions or services are duplicated unnecessarily;
- produce uneven service delivery where states have different capacities or resources;
- encourage unhealthy competition or secessionist pressures in poorly managed systems.

Boundary disputes and disagreements about revenue or responsibilities may also occur. These are possible challenges, not inevitable features of every federation.

12. NIGERIA: EDUCATION AND HEALTH RESPONSIBILITIES

It is inaccurate to say that all education and health responsibilities in Nigeria are simply one undifferentiated “concurrent power”. The Constitution distributes particular responsibilities in more detailed ways.

- Under Second Schedule, Part II, Item L, the National Assembly has legislative power concerning university, technological and designated professional education. State Houses of Assembly also have powers concerning specified educational institutions and technical, vocational, post-primary, primary and other forms of education.
- Under the Fourth Schedule, local government councils participate in providing and maintaining primary, adult and vocational education and in providing and maintaining health services.
- Health is not listed as one general, blanket item on the Concurrent Legislative List. State powers over matters not on the Exclusive List are important, while federal laws and institutions may govern specific national matters. Chapter II also directs government policy towards adequate medical and health facilities.

The correct lesson is that responsibilities are distributed across levels under specific constitutional provisions and laws. Do not memorise “education and health are concurrent” as a complete description of Nigeria's constitutional allocation.

13. FEDERAL CHARACTER IN NIGERIA

The Federal Character Principle is a constitutional principle intended to ensure that the composition and conduct of government reflect Nigeria's diversity, promote national unity and loyalty, and prevent a few states, ethnic groups or other sectional groups from dominating federal government or its agencies.

Section 14(3) of the Constitution applies this principle to the Government of the Federation and its agencies. Section 14(4) requires State Governments, local government councils and their agencies to recognise the diversity of the people within their areas and promote a sense of belonging and loyalty.

The Federal Character Commission (FCC) is a constitutional body under section 153 and the Third Schedule. Its legal framework includes the Federal Character Commission (Establishment, etc.) Act, 1996. The Commission's functions include developing equitable formulas for the distribution of public-service posts, monitoring and promoting compliance, and addressing imbalances in public institutions and the distribution of socio-economic amenities and infrastructure.

Federal character is not simply a demand that every group must receive exactly the same number of appointments, nor is it a guarantee that an individual will receive a particular job. It is a constitutional framework for fair representation and inclusion. The Commission's official guidance also states that federal character does not replace merit; fair representation and competence must be considered together.

14. POSSIBLE CHALLENGES IN APPLYING FEDERAL CHARACTER

Federal character can raise difficult policy questions:
- Representation and merit: institutions must pursue fair representation while maintaining competence, transparent criteria and equal opportunity.
- Population and balance: states and groups differ in population and circumstances, so a fair formula must be explained rather than assumed to mean identical numbers in every situation.
- Political influence: appointments may be criticised as patronage if criteria and decisions are not transparent.
- Implementation: reliable data, clear guidelines, monitoring and consistent enforcement are needed across many public institutions.

These are governance challenges to manage, not proof that the principle is inherently unfair or that any particular group is favoured. The principle aims to prevent domination and strengthen belonging; it should not be used to stereotype individuals or assume that one person speaks for an entire state or ethnic group.

15. SUMMARY

A unitary system concentrates ultimate constitutional authority at the centre, although it may allow devolution and local administration. A federal system constitutionally distributes powers between a national government and constituent units. Neither system guarantees efficiency, unity, minority protection or development. Country examples must be understood in their constitutional and practical contexts. In Nigeria, Federal Character is a constitutional principle for inclusive representation and national unity.

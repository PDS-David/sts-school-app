## Unitary government — lines 756–795

The source defines unitary government through concentration of ultimate constitutional authority at the centre. That is broadly sound, but unitary states may still delegate or devolve administrative and legislative functions to local/regional institutions. The distinction is that such powers do not necessarily have the same constitutionally entrenched status as the powers of constituent units in a federation.

### Country examples — line 757

The UK, France and Italy are generally classified as unitary states, but their internal arrangements differ:
- **United Kingdom:** parliamentary sovereignty remains a central constitutional principle, while Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland exercise devolved powers. The UK Parliament states that it retains ultimate legal authority and can legislate on devolved matters, although it generally does not do so.
- **France:** has a written Constitution with a formal amendment procedure under Article 89 and a constitutional review system. Its unitary structure coexists with decentralised local/regional authorities.
- **Italy:** has a written Constitution with a formal amendment procedure under Article 138 and constitutionally recognized regional autonomy. It is not a UK-style parliamentary-sovereignty system.

Thus, “unitary” does not mean that all administration occurs in the capital or that every unitary constitution is flexible.

### Flexibility and parliamentary supremacy — lines 764–765

The source says a unitary constitution is flexible and parliament is supreme. These statements fit the UK constitutional tradition, which is uncodified and characterized by parliamentary sovereignty, but they do not describe every unitary state. France and Italy have written constitutions, formal amendment procedures and constitutional review. The corrected lesson must distinguish the form of the state from the form and amendment rules of its constitution.

The source's claims that unitary systems are best for small or homogeneous states, and its lists of advantages/disadvantages, should be presented as common arguments or possible trade-offs rather than universal rules.

## Federal government — lines 800–839

The source describes a federal system as constitutionally sharing governmental powers between a central government and constituent units. This is broadly correct. Distinguish federalism from administrative decentralization: constituent-unit powers are protected by the constitutional allocation of powers, although the precise division and degree of centralization vary by country.

### Country examples — line 801

The four examples are constitutionally federal:
- **Nigeria:** section 2(2) of the 1999 Constitution states that Nigeria is a federation consisting of states and the Federal Capital Territory.
- **United States:** the federal Constitution and its amendments distribute authority between the federal government and the states.
- **India:** Article 1 describes India as a “Union of States”; the Supreme Court of India identifies the Constitution's federal system of governance between the Union and the States.
- **Russia:** Article 1 of the Constitution describes the Russian Federation as a federative state; Article 5 sets out its federal structure.

The formal classification does not imply identical state autonomy or practice. India has a strong Union government; Russia is constitutionally federal but highly centralized in practice. The corrected lesson should separate constitutional design from actual operation.

### Features, reasons, merits and demerits

The source lists constitutional division of powers, local initiative, minority protection, administrative proximity and resource pooling as reasons or advantages. It also lists cost, slower decisions, rivalry, boundary disputes and uneven development as disadvantages. These are arguments and possible effects, not guaranteed outcomes:
- Federalism does not automatically prevent dictatorship.
- It does not guarantee minority protection; constitutional safeguards and institutions matter.
- Duplication of functions may create costs but does not guarantee additional employment.
- The suitability of federal or unitary arrangements cannot be determined by size or ethnic diversity alone.

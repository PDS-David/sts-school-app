## Legitimacy — lines 264–281

The source defines legitimacy through both conformity with rules/law and popular acceptance/support. These are related but distinct:
- Legal or constitutional validity concerns whether authority is established under the applicable legal framework.
- Sociological legitimacy concerns whether people accept an authority as rightful.
- Performance and public support may influence acceptance but do not replace legal requirements.
- Foreign recognition/diplomacy is an external dimension and should not be presented as a direct measure of domestic public acceptance.

The source lists leadership, national symbols, popular participation, foreign diplomacy, good government and popular support as factors. These are retained as possible influences, not an exhaustive or universal test. The assertion that an election winner is automatically legitimate must be qualified by constitutional rules, electoral integrity and the broader acceptance of the political system.

## Sovereignty — lines 286–314

### Bodin

The source gives Jean Bodin's dates as 1530–1596 and says he introduced modern sovereignty theory. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives 1529/30–1596 and identifies *The Six Books of the Commonwealth* (1576) as his major work. Bodin's famous formulation describes sovereignty as the “absolute and perpetual power” of a commonwealth. The source's 1530 is a conventional rounded date; the corrected lesson should give the more precise scholarly range and the 1576 work.

Bodin's “absolute” sovereignty is a historical doctrinal position. The Stanford entry explains that Bodin's sovereign was not bound by civil/positive laws promulgated by the sovereign, but remained bound by natural and divine law. It is therefore inaccurate to turn his formulation into an unqualified claim that every modern state is above all external legal obligations.

### Types of sovereignty

The source lists:
- **Legal sovereignty:** power to make and enforce laws.
- **Political sovereignty:** ultimate political power associated with the electorate.
- **Internal sovereignty:** authority within the state's territory.
- **External sovereignty:** independence in external relations.
- **De facto sovereignty:** described by the source as acquired through force.
- **De jure sovereignty:** described by the source as acquired according to domestic law.

The de facto/de jure distinction needs correction. Jorge E. Núñez's 2024 study distinguishes factual/de facto sovereignty as actual control over a territory from normative/de jure sovereignty as legal status or entitlement; actual control is not necessary for every de jure claim. Thus, de facto is not defined by the method of acquisition, and de jure is not simply a lawful method of taking power. The internal dimension concerns the state's authority within its territory and should not be narrowed to nationals alone.

### Characteristics and international law

The source lists indivisibility, permanence, absoluteness, absence of foreign control, comprehensiveness and inalienability. These are classical attributes in sovereignty theory; they should not be presented as uncontested empirical facts about every contemporary state.

The line 306 statement that a state “receives order from none” expresses a strong classical doctrine. The UN Charter, Article 2(1)–(2), recognizes the sovereign equality of members and requires members to fulfil in good faith their Charter obligations. This does not erase sovereignty; it means sovereign status is compatible with international obligations. The corrected lesson must distinguish sovereign equality from an exemption from international law.

## Democracy — lines 315–346

- The source defines democracy through popular consent and distinguishes direct/classical from representative/indirect democracy. Both are taught.
- The attribution at line 318 is accurate: the formulation “government of the people, by the people, for the people” is from Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, delivered on 19 November 1863. The National Archives confirms the date and speech.
- The source's claim that democracy is “the best form of government” is a normative judgement. Oxford Bibliographies describes democracy as widely understood as the best form, but notes that agreement ends there and that disagreement continues about its justification and institutional realization. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats the moral desirability of democracy as a subject of normative debate. The supplement must attribute the view and present arguments and criticisms neutrally.
- The features list includes elections, rights, majority rule, equality, opposition parties, participation, judicial independence, separation of powers and press freedom. Use inclusive “one person, one vote” wording and explain that majority rule operates within constitutional rights and protections for minorities.
- The demerits section lists slow decision-making, cost and risks of electoral malpractice, and claims about literacy. These are criticisms or context-dependent risks, not universal facts about people or all democracies. Avoid stigmatizing populations by literacy and avoid sensational detail.

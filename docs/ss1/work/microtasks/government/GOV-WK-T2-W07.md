## Independence Constitution of 1960 — children 325–345

The source correctly identifies the constitutional monarchy, Governor-General, parliamentary federalism, bicameral federal legislature and Privy Council appeal. Corrections/qualifications:
- The monarch's constitutional title was Queen of Nigeria, not simply “Queen of England”; the Governor-General represented the monarch as head of state, while the Prime Minister headed the government.
- The federal legislature was bicameral. Northern and Western regional legislatures had Houses of Chiefs and Houses of Assembly, but the Eastern Region had a unicameral legislature; the source's claim that all regional legislatures were bicameral is incorrect.
- Senators were selected through the regional legislatures. They were not directly elected by the general electorate and should not be described as simply appointed by regional governments.
- The 1960 Constitution retained appeals to Her Majesty in Council (the Privy Council) in specified cases; the Supreme Court was not yet the final court of appeal.
- Judicial appointments involved the Judicial Service Commission and constitutional procedures; the source's phrase “made up of judges” is incomplete.
- The Constitution required public audit reporting to Parliament and marked the transition to Nigerian control of public offices, though Nigerianization of the civil service was gradual.
- The Governor's powers concerning a Premier who had lost the confidence of the regional House should be explained through the Constitution and parliamentary conventions, not presented as an unrestricted personal power.

## Republican Constitution of 1963 — children 347–367

The source correctly identifies the Republic, the replacement of the monarch by a ceremonial President, the continued parliamentary system, increased legislative membership and the end of new appeals to the Privy Council. Important corrections:
- The 1963 Constitution itself was supreme law. Section 1 stated that it had force of law throughout Nigeria and prevailed over inconsistent laws. The source's claim of “parliamentary supremacy against the supremacy of constitution” is false.
- The President was elected by a joint sitting of the two Houses of Parliament, not directly by the electorate. The President was ceremonial; the Prime Minister remained head of government. The source's criticism of indirect election is an opinion, not a factual description of the President's powers.
- The Mid-Western Region was created in August 1963 following a referendum. Nigeria had four regions when it became a Republic on 1 October 1963. The source omits this significant development.
- The Supreme Court became the final appellate court when the Republic took effect on 1 October 1963; appeals to the Privy Council ceased, subject to transitional arrangements for pending appeals.
- Fundamental rights were entrenched but subject to constitutional limitations; “fully guaranteed” is too absolute.
- Parliamentary government linked the executive and legislature; that is a defining Westminster feature, not necessarily a constitutional defect. The Prime Minister was accountable to Parliament, whose members were elected by the people.
- Cross-carpeting occurred as a political practice in the First Republic. The source should not say the Constitution explicitly permitted defections for personal rewards; identify the absence or presence of anti-defection provisions from the actual text.

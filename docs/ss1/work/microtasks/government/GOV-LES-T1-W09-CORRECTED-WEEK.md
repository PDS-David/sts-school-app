## Lesson body
POLITICAL SOCIALIZATION AND ITS AGENTS

Learning objectives
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- define political socialization and explain why it continues throughout life;
- distinguish political socialization from political culture;
- identify and explain seven agents of political socialization;
- describe the possible influence of religious groups without assuming that all members share one political view;
- explain how the Nigerian Constitution protects religious freedom, association, participation and non-discrimination.

1. MEANING OF POLITICAL SOCIALIZATION

Political socialization is the lifelong process through which people learn, interpret, develop and sometimes change their political values, beliefs, attitudes and ways of participating in public life. It begins in childhood and continues through adolescence and adulthood. A person may learn through formal lessons, ordinary conversations, observation, personal experience and participation in community activities.

Political socialization is not the same as memorising the names of political leaders or parties. It includes learning how public institutions work, how people discuss public issues, what rights and responsibilities citizens have, how decisions are made and how different people can take part.

The process is not automatic or identical for everyone. People who grow up in the same family or community may form different opinions. New information, education, work, friendships and public events may reinforce or change earlier views. A person can question an idea learned earlier and reach a different conclusion.

2. POLITICAL SOCIALIZATION AND POLITICAL CULTURE

Political culture refers to the shared patterns of political beliefs, values, attitudes and expectations found in a society or community. People within one society do not all think alike; political culture describes broad patterns, not a belief held by every person.

Political socialization is the process through which people learn and respond to those political patterns. In simple terms:
- political culture describes patterns of political values and attitudes;
- political socialization describes how people acquire, interpret and sometimes revise them.

For example, a community may value peaceful elections and public discussion. A young person may learn about those expectations at home, in school, through the media and by observing an election. The shared expectation is part of political culture; the learning process is political socialization.

3. FORMAL AND INFORMAL LEARNING

Formal political learning takes place in organised settings. Examples include Civic Education and Government lessons, school debates, student councils and structured public-information programmes.

Informal political learning happens through everyday experience. Examples include family discussions, conversations with friends, news reports, community meetings, religious gatherings, public campaigns and observing how institutions respond to people.

Both forms matter. A classroom can explain constitutional rights, while a real community discussion may show how people express different views. Neither form should be treated as the only source of political knowledge.

4. SEVEN AGENTS OF POLITICAL SOCIALIZATION

An agent is a person, group, institution or channel through which people may learn about political life. The seven agents in this lesson are family, school, peer groups, political parties, mass media, religious groups and pressure groups. Their influence differs from person to person and can be positive, negative or mixed.

A. FAMILY

The family is often an early setting for political learning. Children may hear discussions about public issues, observe how adults treat people with different opinions, and learn expectations about fairness, responsibility and community life.

A family does not determine a child's final political beliefs. Family members may disagree, and a person may change their views after gaining new information and experience.

Example: family members discuss why people should vote peacefully and listen to different candidates' proposals. A young person learns that political choices can be discussed rather than imposed.

B. SCHOOL

Schools provide formal learning about government, public institutions, rights, responsibilities and civic participation. Teachers can encourage students to examine evidence, compare arguments and disagree respectfully.

School activities such as class debates, student councils, clubs, elections for student representatives and community projects can give learners practical experience of participation and accountability.

Example: a class holds a debate on how to improve the school environment. Students listen to different proposals, give reasons and vote on a fair decision.

C. PEER GROUPS

Peer groups are friends, classmates, teammates and other people of a similar age or shared activity. They may exchange information, discuss public events and influence one another's attitudes. Peer influence can reinforce an opinion or introduce a different point of view.

A learner should not feel forced to accept a group's political opinion. Respectful disagreement is part of responsible participation.

Example: classmates discuss a news report about a local public project and compare what each has learned from different sources.

D. POLITICAL PARTIES

Political parties are organised groups that seek political office and aim to influence or form government. They present policies and manifestos, select candidates, campaign and invite people to participate in political activities.

Parties can teach people about political choices and public programmes. Their claims should still be examined critically: a manifesto is a proposal, not proof that a promise has been achieved.

Example: a student compares two publicly available policy proposals and identifies the evidence each party gives for its promises.

E. MASS MEDIA

Mass media includes newspapers, radio, television, magazines and digital news platforms that communicate information to large audiences. They report public events, provide analysis, carry interviews and debates, and offer educational and entertainment programmes.

Media can influence which issues people notice and how they understand them. Reports may differ in accuracy, emphasis or opinion. Learners should check the date, identify the publisher or speaker, compare reliable reports and distinguish verified facts from commentary, advertisements and rumours.

Example: before sharing a claim about an election, a student checks the date and source and looks for confirmation from a reliable independent report or official record.

F. RELIGIOUS GROUPS

Religious groups may be agents of political socialization when members discuss public responsibilities, social justice, community needs, peace or public policy. They may also organise charitable work and encourage members to consider ethical questions.

Their influence is not universal or uniform. People within the same religious community may hold different political opinions. A religious leader or group does not have an automatic right to decide every member's political choice, and participation in a religious community should not be treated as proof of a person's party preference.

Nigeria's constitutional framework makes several important distinctions:
- Section 10 says the Government of the Federation or of a State shall not adopt any religion as a State Religion.
- Section 38 protects freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including the freedom to change religion or belief and to manifest it individually or with others, in public or private, subject to the Constitution.
- Section 40 protects peaceful assembly and association, including forming or belonging to associations for the protection of interests.
- Section 42 protects citizens from specified forms of discrimination, including discrimination based on religion or political opinion.
- Section 14(2)(c) states that the participation of the people in their government shall be ensured in accordance with the Constitution.

These provisions mean that people and groups may participate in public discussion within the law, while the State must not establish a State Religion and individuals retain their constitutional freedoms. Public participation by religious citizens or organisations is not the same thing as the State adopting a religion. No faith community is named or treated as having a single political position in this lesson.

G. PRESSURE GROUPS

Pressure groups are organised associations that seek to influence public policy or decisions on issues of concern to their members or the wider community. They may use petitions, public education, research, peaceful campaigns, meetings and dialogue with public officials.

Pressure groups are different from political parties. A political party generally seeks to win political office and form or influence government; a pressure group generally seeks to influence particular policies or decisions without necessarily seeking to govern.

Examples include a professional association advocating safer workplaces, a trade union discussing workers' conditions or a community organisation requesting better public facilities. The example does not imply that every group uses the same method or represents every person in its field.

5. WHY POLITICAL SOCIALIZATION MATTERS

Political socialization can help people:
- understand public institutions, constitutional rights and civic responsibilities;
- take part more thoughtfully in community and national affairs;
- compare proposals and assess public information instead of relying only on rumours;
- listen to different views and disagree peacefully;
- understand how public values and attitudes may be reinforced or changed.

These are possible educational benefits, not guaranteed outcomes. Learning about politics does not require a person to join a political party or agree with a particular religious, family or peer-group position.

6. WEEK EIGHT REVIEW: THE STATE

A state is a political community with a permanent population, a defined territory, a government and sovereignty. Government is the set of institutions and officeholders through which public authority is exercised; it is not the same thing as the State itself.

Sovereignty means the state's supreme authority within its constitutional order and its independence in external relations, subject to the legal obligations it has accepted. Population, territory and government are also essential features, but sovereignty is commonly identified as the most distinctive characteristic that separates a state from other associations.

7. SUMMARY

Political socialization is lifelong learning about political values, institutions and participation. It takes place formally and informally. Family, school, peer groups, political parties, mass media, religious groups and pressure groups can all contribute, but their influence varies and does not determine what every person must believe. In Nigeria, participation in public life must be understood alongside constitutional protections for religious freedom, association and non-discrimination and the prohibition on adopting a State Religion.

It is lifelong because it begins in childhood and continues as people grow, meet new people, receive information and experience public events. Learning may be formal, such as in school, or informal, such as through family discussions and observation.

**Answer:** Political culture is the broad pattern of political beliefs, values, attitudes and expectations found in a society or community. Political culture describes the pattern; political socialization describes the learning process. For example, valuing peaceful elections is part of political culture, while learning that value through family, school or media is political socialization.

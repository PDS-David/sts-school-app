# Week 1 corrected supplement: Government as an institution, an art of governing and an academic field

**Class:** SS 1  
**Subject:** Government  
**Term/week:** First Term, Week 1  
**Use:** This original supplement replaces the Week 1 teaching row for delivery. It is not a reproduction of the assigned textbooks.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, a student should be able to:
1. explain why a state establishes government and describe Hobbes's account as one political theory, not as an established history of a “primitive society”;
2. explain government as the process or art of governing, identify the Legislature, Executive and Judiciary, and distinguish Nigeria's federal and state legislatures; and
3. explain Government as a school subject and distinguish it from Political Science as a wider academic discipline.

## 1. Government as an institution of the state

A **state** is an organised political community with a population, a defined territory, a government and sovereignty. **Government** is the set of institutions and public officials through which public authority is exercised and collective decisions are made and carried out.

Government is not the same thing as the state. The state is the continuing political community; the government is the group of institutions and office-holders that administer public affairs at a particular time. Governments and office-holders can change while the state continues.

Government is needed to organise common affairs, establish and apply public rules, maintain public peace and security, settle disputes through lawful institutions, and make and carry out public policies. These are important purposes of government; how well a particular government achieves them depends on its conduct and accountability.

## 2. Why government exists: explanations, not a single proven origin story

Political thinkers have offered different explanations for why people need organised political authority. These are theories and interpretations. They should not be presented as one universally proven account.

### Hobbes's social-contract argument

In *Leviathan* (1651), Part I, Chapter XIII, Thomas Hobbes considers what life might be like without a common public power able to establish and enforce shared rules. He argues that people would have little assurance that others would respect agreements or leave them secure. Hobbes describes the condition in which people lack a common authority; he does **not** call it a “primitive society”.

**Hobbes's short phrase** (original 1651 spelling): “solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.” Many modern editions spell *poore* as *poor*. The phrase is Hobbes's description of the natural condition he is analysing, not a factual description of every society that existed before modern states.

In his social-contract account, people authorise a common sovereign authority so that rules can be made and enforced and peace can be maintained. Hobbes argues for a strong sovereign. This is a political argument about authority and security, not proof that every government began through a real agreement signed by all its people. In Part II, Chapter XVII, he explains the establishment of a commonwealth through covenant.

Other social-contract thinkers reached different conclusions. John Locke placed greater emphasis on protecting natural rights and limiting government; Jean-Jacques Rousseau connected legitimate authority with the general will. Their arguments should not be treated as identical to Hobbes's.

### Other explanations

- **Natural or social explanation:** Aristotle, in *Politics*, Book I, Chapter 2 (1253a), argues that human beings are naturally social and political. On this view, political communities grow from people's capacity to live and make decisions together.
- **Evolutionary or historical explanation:** some accounts describe political communities as developing gradually from households and kin groups into larger settlements and organised institutions. This is a broad historical model, not a single path followed by every society.
- **Force or conquest explanation:** some states and ruling systems have been established or expanded through conquest or coercion. Describing this as a historical explanation does not justify it.
- **Divine-origin or divine-right explanation:** some religious and political traditions have understood political authority as deriving from divine authority. This is a belief-based explanation, not a universally accepted empirical account.

These explanations differ. Studying them helps students compare arguments; it does not require accepting any one of them as the only explanation.

## 3. Government as the art or process of governing

Government as an **art of governing** means the practical process of managing the affairs of a state through public institutions and lawful authority. This includes maintaining peace and security, making and enforcing laws, regulating public affairs, and formulating and implementing public policy.

In the constitutional arrangement of Nigeria, public powers are distributed among three main organs. Sections 4, 5 and 6 of the Constitution assign legislative, executive and judicial powers respectively.

| Organ | Main function | Nigerian illustration |
|---|---|---|
| Legislature | Makes laws, debates proposals and authorises public rules. | At federal level, the **National Assembly** consists of the Senate and House of Representatives. Each state has its own **House of Assembly**. |
| Executive | Implements and administers laws and carries out public policy. | The President heads the federal executive; a Governor heads the executive in a state. |
| Judiciary | Interprets and applies the law, determines disputes and protects lawful process. | Courts exercise judicial power at federal and state levels. |

The organs have different but connected responsibilities. A state cannot be governed properly by treating one organ as a substitute for all the others. Their constitutional roles, cooperation and checks help prevent public power from being concentrated in one institution.

**Do not confuse the two legislatures:** the **National Assembly** is the federal legislature (Constitution, section 47). A **State House of Assembly** is the legislature of one state (section 90). A State House of Assembly is not another name for the National Assembly. A High Court is part of the Judiciary; an electoral commission is an independent public institution, not a fourth organ in the standard three-organ model.

## 4. Government as an academic field of study

At school, **Government** is a subject that introduces students to the structures, processes and institutions through which a state is governed. It examines the organs of government and how they take part in running public affairs. The subject also introduces international organisations to which states belong and relations between states.

**Political Science** is the wider academic discipline that systematically studies government and politics. It includes the study of political institutions, political systems, political behaviour, power, public policy, political ideas, comparative politics and international relations. Government as a school subject overlaps with Political Science, but the two labels are not exact synonyms: one is the school subject; the other is a broader field of academic study.

### Why do we study Government? — Week 1 scope

For this week's question, use only the reasons introduced above: studying Government helps us understand the structure and processes of political institutions and the organs involved in running the state; it also introduces international organisations and relations between states. Further reasons for studying Government are treated in **Week 2**. They are not repeated in this Week 1 answer.

## 5. Quick recap

- Government is the set of institutions through which public authority is exercised; it is not identical to the state.
- Hobbes's social-contract argument is one explanation of why a common authority may be needed. His “natural condition” is not called a primitive society.
- Government as an art is the practical work of governing.
- The three main organs are the Legislature, Executive and Judiciary.
- Nigeria's federal legislature is the National Assembly; a state's legislature is its House of Assembly.
- Government is a school subject; Political Science is the wider academic discipline.
- In the Week 1 scope, studying Government helps us understand political institutions, the organs involved in running the state, international organisations and relations between states. Further reasons are in Week 2.

## Historical background — child 39

The source correctly identifies the Berlin Conference's dates (1884–1885) and Bismarck's chairmanship, but overstates what the conference did. The German Federal Foreign Office records that it ran from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885, established rules for European territorial claims and trade/navigation, and did not draw specific borders. The subsequent partition and colonization unfolded over time through claims, treaties, conquest and administrative arrangements.

The source's list of colonial powers in West Africa is incomplete and its use of “West Africa” is broad. The conference itself involved fourteen states, including Spain; colonial control varied by territory and period. Liberia remained formally independent, but the source's four-power list should not be presented as exhaustive.

The source's 1885–1950s colonial period is too short. UN decolonization records show West African independence dates extending beyond the 1950s: Ghana in 1957, Nigeria in 1960, Sierra Leone in 1961, The Gambia in 1965, Guinea-Bissau in 1974 and Cabo Verde in 1975. Colonial rule and the transition to independence differed across territories.

The statement that resources were removed to develop metropolitan Europe is a historical interpretation associated with anti-colonial political economy, including Walter Rodney's work. It should be attributed as an argument and supported with dated evidence, not presented as an unqualified description of every colony and period.

## Reasons for colonialism — children 43–48

The source lists trade, raw materials, markets, slavery, cultural/religious claims and protection of missionaries. Several corrections are required:
- The First Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the **eighteenth century**, commonly dated around 1760–1840, not the seventeenth century.
- Slavery existed in Africa before European arrival, and the transatlantic slave trade predated formal colonial administrations. Do not teach that colonial administration simply introduced slavery; distinguish earlier slave systems and trade from later colonial labour policies.
- The claim that European powers brought “civilization” should be presented as a colonial ideological justification, not an objective fact.
- Economic, strategic, political and missionary motives varied among empires and territories; no single list explains every colonial action.

## Indirect rule — children 50–66

The source's basic definition—governing through local rulers or appointed intermediaries under colonial supervision—is recognizable. Its account of origin is too simple:
- Lord Lugard played a major role in developing and applying indirect rule in Northern Nigeria, but the system was adapted to local political institutions and varied across regions.
- Toyin Falola's 2024 Cambridge chapter explains that British policy was adapted to different Nigerian societies, existing precolonial institutions and local resistance. It also notes that indirect-rule arrangements were not unique to Britain.
- The source's reference to success in India and Uganda should not imply identical systems or a single transfer of policy.
- Staffing, cost, language and existing institutions were relevant administrative factors. The source's climate/disease explanation is overstated, and the claim that the system was introduced merely to preserve customs ignores colonial control, taxation and fiscal administration.
- The features list at children 53–59 identifies local authorities, customary rules, tax collection and appointed warrant chiefs. Colonial administrators also altered and subordinated local institutions; preservation was not neutral or complete.

## Traditional rulers — children 68–73

The source identifies tax collection, communication with colonial officials, local order and Native Authority administration. These roles varied by region and period. It is inaccurate to say all traditional rulers were members of legislative councils. Distinguish Native Authorities from legislative or advisory councils and avoid treating the role of every chief or emir as identical.

## Indirect rule in Northern Nigeria — children 75–80

The source points to existing emirate and administrative structures and tax collection. These are relevant, but its explanations contain unsupported stereotypes:
- Northern Nigeria was not institutionally uniform; emirate structures existed in some areas, while other communities had different political arrangements.
- The claim that Islam taught political submissiveness is an unsupported generalization about a diverse religion and its adherents. Do not use it as a causal explanation.
- The claim that low literacy among Northern Nigerians caused the policy's success is also an overgeneralization and should not be taught as a fact.
- A corrected account should examine colonial administrative choices, existing local institutions, taxation and varied local responses, without attributing one political attitude to a religion or population.

Explains regional adaptation, existing precolonial institutions, local resistance and Lugard's role; notes that indirect-rule systems were not unique to Britain: [chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/understanding-colonial-nigeria/indirect-rule-and-the-native-administration/1C8FF19EC18E5080668BD8599D4239F4).

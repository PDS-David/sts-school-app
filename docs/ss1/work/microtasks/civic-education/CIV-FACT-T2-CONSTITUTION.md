## Findings against the Constitution

### 1. Which Constitution is current — L371 and L405

The current constitutional instrument is the **Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999, as amended**, not the 1979 Constitution. The Constitution itself identifies its citation as the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (section 319) and its original commencement as 29 May 1999 (section 320). It has since been altered by the First through Fifth Alterations, including the 2023 amendments reflected in the consolidated reference above.

The phrase “current Nigerian constitution” at L371 is broadly correct when it means the 1999 Constitution as amended. L405's reference to the 1999 Constitution is also the correct constitutional instrument, but it should not imply that the unamended 1999 text is the complete current text.

### 2. Federal or unitary structure — L415

Constitution section 2(2) expressly states that Nigeria is a **Federation consisting of States and a Federal Capital Territory**. Sections 3 and 4–6 set out the component States and distribute legislative, executive and judicial powers between the Federation and the States. The source's general description of a constitution defining a country's structure and division of powers is supported; for Nigeria, the answer is federal, not unitary.

### 3. Four-year tenure and the 1979 reference — L431

L431 attributes a four-year tenure to the 1979 Constitution and describes the subject as “a government” generally. That attribution is historical, not the operative source for current tenure. The current 1999 Constitution itself provides:
- section 64(1): the Senate and House of Representatives stand dissolved after four years from the first sitting;
- section 105(1): a State House of Assembly stands dissolved after four years from its first sitting;
- section 135(2): the President vacates office after four years, subject to section 135(1);
- section 180(2): a Governor vacates office after four years, subject to section 180(1).

Assessment: four-year terms are also present in the current 1999 Constitution for these specified offices/houses. The source should not present the 1979 Constitution as the current legal authority or treat “government” as one undifferentiated office.

### 4. Constitutional amendment procedure — L441

Constitution section 9(2) requires, for an ordinary constitutional alteration not governed by section 8, support from **not less than two-thirds of all members of each House of the National Assembly** and approval by resolution of the Houses of Assembly of **not less than two-thirds of all States**.

Section 9(3) sets a higher threshold for altering section 8, section 9 or Chapter IV: **not less than four-fifths of all members of each National Assembly House**, plus approval by resolutions of the Houses of Assembly of not less than two-thirds of all States.

Assessment: “2/3 majority of both houses” is incomplete. It omits the State House of Assembly ratification requirement and the four-fifths threshold for the entrenched provisions specified in section 9(3). The Constitution says two-thirds of **all members** of each House, not merely two-thirds of members present.

### 5. Presidential and State executive powers; separation of powers — L449 and L453

- Section 130(2): the President is Head of State, Chief Executive of the Federation and Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces.
- Section 5(1): federal executive powers are vested in the President and may be exercised directly or through the Vice-President, Ministers and federal public officers.
- Section 5(2): State executive powers are vested in the Governor and may be exercised directly or through the Deputy Governor, Commissioners and State public officers. L453's description of State executive powers substantially tracks this provision.
- Sections 4, 5 and 6 vest legislative, executive and judicial powers respectively in the relevant legislative houses, executives and courts. The source's separation-of-powers summary is a high-level description; it should not be taken to mean that the Constitution contains no checks, shared functions or express qualifications.
- Sections 6(1)–(3) vest federal and State judicial powers in the constitutionally established courts. Sections 230 and 233 establish the Supreme Court and its appellate jurisdiction; section 235 provides that no appeal lies to another body or person from a Supreme Court determination. “Highest court of Appeal” is therefore a broad, understandable description, although “final appellate court” is more exact.

### 6. National Assembly composition and constituency counts — L457

- Section 47 establishes a bicameral National Assembly consisting of the Senate and House of Representatives.
- Section 48: the Senate has three Senators from each State and one from the Federal Capital Territory: 36 × 3 + 1 = **109 Senators**.
- Section 49: the House of Representatives has **360 members**, representing constituencies of nearly equal population as far as possible.
- Section 71 requires INEC to divide each State into three Senatorial districts and the Federation into 360 Federal constituencies.

Assessment: the numerical claims are correct. “Senate” is the proper name of the upper chamber, rather than “House of Senate.” The House of Representatives figure and the 360 Federal constituency figure are supported by sections 49 and 71.

### 7. Local-government functions — L461

Section 7(5) requires functions conferred by State law on local government councils to include those set out in the Fourth Schedule. The Fourth Schedule lists functions including local planning recommendations; rates and licences; cemeteries; markets and motor parks; local roads, street lighting and drains; sanitation; birth/death/marriage registration; and participation in primary, adult and vocational education, agriculture and health services, alongside additional functions that may be conferred by State House of Assembly law.

Assessment: the source's statement that a list of local-government functions is set out is supported. The source excerpt is incomplete and should not be treated as the list itself or as a complete statement of the constitutional allocation.

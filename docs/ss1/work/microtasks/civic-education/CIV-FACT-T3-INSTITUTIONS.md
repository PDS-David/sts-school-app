## Applicable constitutional baseline

The constitutional reference is the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999, consolidated through the Fifth Alteration (2023 edition). It is the latest enacted consolidated text located for this audit. The Sixth Alteration / state-policing reform proposals and the National Policing Bill process reported in 2026 are not treated as enacted law here.

- Section 4 vests legislative power in the National Assembly and State Houses of Assembly within their constitutional competence.
- Section 5 vests federal executive power in the President and State executive power in each Governor, subject to the Constitution.
- Section 6 vests federal and State judicial power in the constitutionally established courts.
- Section 153 and the Third Schedule establish/describe specified federal executive bodies, including the Code of Conduct Bureau.
- Section 214(1) establishes the Nigeria Police Force and, under the current constitutional text, provides for one police force for Nigeria.
- Sections 217–218 establish the Armed Forces (Army, Navy, Air Force and any further branches established by Act) and define their defence/territorial-integrity and limited aid-to-civil-authorities functions.
- Third Schedule Part I A and Fifth Schedule govern the Code of Conduct Bureau and Code of Conduct Tribunal.

Reference:
- [Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999 — updated through Fifth Alteration (PLAC 2023 PDF)](https://placng.org/i/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Constitution-of-the-Federal-Republic-of-Nigeria-2023.pdf)
- [State House: National Policing Bill/state-policing reform process, 4 August 2026](https://statehouse.gov.ng/fg-announces-seven-week-roadmap-for-national-policing-bill-invites-public-input/)

## Institution-by-institution findings

### 1. Nigeria Police Force (NPF) — source pp.183–195

**Current legal name:** Nigeria Police Force.

**Primary legal basis and date:**
- Constitution sections 214–216.
- Nigeria Police Act, 2020; assented to by the President on 16 September 2020. The Act repeals the Police Act, Cap. P19 LFN 2004.
- Nigeria Police Regulations (Amendment) 2026 were reported by the Ministry of Police Affairs as signed in November 2025 and gazetted in February 2026 under section 138 of the 2020 Act; those regulations supplement the principal Act and should be checked for any detailed operational teaching.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Police Act section 4 assigns prevention and detection of crime; protection of rights and freedoms; maintenance of public safety, law and order; protection of lives and property; enforcement of laws without prejudice to other agencies' enabling Acts; collaboration/support for persons in distress; public-road movement facilitation; and community partnership. The constitutional force is national.

**Source assessment:** The source's broad statements about maintaining law/order, protecting life/property and detecting crime are supported. A later lesson should include the Act's rights-based limits and avoid implying that police functions displace the specific mandates of other statutory agencies. The current constitutional text still provides for the Nigeria Police Force; proposals for state policing in 2026 are not treated as law unless and until enacted.

Sources:
- [Nigeria Police Act, 2020 — Ministry of Police Affairs PDF](https://policeaffairs.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Police-Act-2020.pdf), especially sections 3–5 and 13.
- [State House: Police Act assented to on 16 September 2020](https://statehouse.gov.ng/president-buharis-police-reforms/).
- [Ministry of Police Affairs: Nigeria Police Regulations (Amendment) 2026, 16 April 2026](https://policeaffairs.gov.ng/nigeria-strengthens-police-reforms-with-new-regulations-to-drive-accountability-and-professionalism/).

### 2. Armed Forces of the Federation — source pp.184 and 196

**Current legal name:** Armed Forces of the Federation, comprising the Army, Navy, Air Force and any other branch established by an Act of the National Assembly.

**Primary legal basis and date:** Constitution sections 217–219 (1999 Constitution as amended; 2023 consolidated text). The Armed Forces Act and service-specific laws provide further detail.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 217(2) specifies defence against external aggression; maintenance of territorial integrity and border security on land, sea and air; suppressing insurrection and acting in aid of civil authorities to restore order when called upon by the President, subject to conditions prescribed by an Act of the National Assembly; and other functions prescribed by law. Section 218 addresses presidential command and operational use.

**Source assessment:** The Army/Navy/Air Force branch labels are correct. The source's claim that the Army generally “ensures adherence to law and order on land” is overbroad if read as ordinary domestic policing. The constitutional aid-to-civil-authorities function is conditional; the Armed Forces are not a substitute for the Nigeria Police Force in ordinary civilian law enforcement.

Source: [Constitution, sections 217–219](https://placng.org/i/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Constitution-of-the-Federal-Republic-of-Nigeria-2023.pdf).

### 3. Organs of government — source p.186–197

**Correct category:** The legislature, executive and judiciary are constitutional organs/branches of government, not one law-enforcement agency.

**Primary legal basis:** Constitution sections 4, 5 and 6.

**Functions/jurisdiction:**
- Legislature: makes laws within the legislative competence allocated by the Constitution.
- Executive: executes and maintains the Constitution and laws within the relevant federal or State executive sphere.
- Judiciary: exercises judicial power through the constitutionally established courts.

**Source assessment:** The source block at p197 only says `Organs of government (refer to 2 nd term e-note)`. That e-note is not included in this T3 source member. This record does not invent its content. If the source is later taught, identify the three branches and their separate constitutional functions; do not describe “organs” as a single agency.

Source: [Constitution, sections 4–6](https://placng.org/i/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Constitution-of-the-Federal-Republic-of-Nigeria-2023.pdf).

### 4. Nigerian Prisons Service — current name: Nigerian Correctional Service (NCoS) — source p.187 and p.198

**Current legal name:** Nigerian Correctional Service. “Nigerian Prisons Service” is the legacy name in the older source.

**Primary legal basis and date:** Nigerian Correctional Service Act, 2019, Act No. 9; commencement 31 July 2019. Section 45 repeals the Prisons Act, Cap. P29 LFN 2004.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 1 establishes the Nigerian Correctional Service to provide both Custodial Service and Non-Custodial Service. Section 2 objectives include human-rights-compliant correctional practice, non-custodial measures, reformation, rehabilitation and reintegration, and mechanisms addressing the number of persons awaiting trial. Section 4 gives the Controller-General superintendence, including inmates' safety/humane custody and rehabilitation/reintegration.

**Source assessment:** The source's custody/security description is incomplete. The current Service is not merely a prison-guard body ensuring that sentences are served and preventing escapes; it has custodial and non-custodial functions, rehabilitation/reintegration objectives and responsibilities relating to awaiting-trial persons. Do not use the old “Nigerian Prisons Service” name as the current institution.

Source: [Nigerian Correctional Service Act, 2019 — official NCoS PDF](https://www.corrections.gov.ng/storage/report/NCoS_act_2019.pdf), sections 1–4 and 45.

### 5. Nigeria Customs Service (NCS) — source p.188 and p.199

**Current statutory name:** Nigeria Customs Service (not “Nigerian Customs Service”).

**Primary legal basis and date:** Nigeria Customs Service Act, 2023, Act No. 35; commencement 20 April 2023; published in Federal Republic of Nigeria Official Gazette No. 105, Vol. 110, 9 June 2023. The Act repeals the Customs and Excise Management Act and other listed customs/excise legislation.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 4 assigns administration/enforcement of customs and excise laws; collection/accounting for customs duties, excise duties, charges, fees and special assessments assigned by government; administration of related trade/fiscal policies; trade facilitation; prevention of smuggling and customs fraud; border enforcement/regulatory activity; trade statistics; and licensing of customs agents. Section 6 gives Customs officers police-like powers for the purpose of enforcing the Customs Act.

**Source assessment:** “Collects taxes on goods brought into the country” is too imprecise. Use customs/excise duties, charges, fees and assigned assessments, not a blanket claim that Customs collects every tax on imported goods. Customs officers' police powers are tied to enforcement of customs law, not a general nationwide police mandate.

Source: [Nigeria Customs Service Act, 2023 — official Customs PDF / Official Gazette](https://customs.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/NCS-ACT-2023-1.pdf), sections 1–6.

### 6. Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (EFCC) — source p.189–200

**Current statutory name:** Economic and Financial Crimes Commission.

**Primary legal basis and date:** Economic and Financial Crimes Commission (Establishment, etc.) Act, 2004, Act No. 1; commencement 4 June 2004.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 6 includes enforcement/administration of the Act; investigation of financial crimes (including advance-fee fraud and money laundering); coordination/enforcement of economic and financial crimes laws; preventive/regulatory measures; investigation of reported cases; tracing/seizure/forfeiture measures; and collaboration with other bodies. Section 7 and related provisions supply further powers.

**Source assessment:** The source's description of EFCC as a financial/economic-crime enforcement body is broadly correct. It should not be collapsed into ICPC or presented as a general agency responsible for every form of public order.

Source: [EFCC (Establishment, etc.) Act, 2004 — PLAC Laws of Nigeria PDF](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/laws/E1.pdf), sections 1 and 6.

### 7. Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission (ICPC) — source p.189–200

**Current statutory name:** Independent Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Commission.

**Primary legal basis and date:** Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Act, 2003, Act No. 6; commencement 18 May 2003. Section 55 repeals the earlier Act No. 5 of 2000.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 10 requires the Commission, where reasonable grounds exist, to receive and investigate reports of offences under the Act or other laws prohibiting corruption; examine practices/systems/procedures of public bodies that may facilitate fraud/corruption; advise and assist public bodies; educate the public; and foster public support. It may make recommendations to the Attorney-General for prosecution in appropriate cases.

**Source assessment:** ICPC and EFCC are separate commissions with different statutory focuses. ICPC's remit is corrupt practices and related offences; it should not be described as interchangeable with EFCC's broader economic/financial-crime mandate. The source's combined description is too compressed.

Source: [Corrupt Practices and Other Related Offences Act, 2003 — PLAC Laws of Nigeria](https://lawsofnigeria.placng.org/print.php?sn=87), especially sections 1, 3 and 10 and the repeal provision in section 55.

### 8. Federal Road Safety Commission / Federal Road Safety Corps — source p.190 and p.201

**Legal distinction:** The Federal Road Safety Commission is established under section 1 of the Federal Road Safety Commission (Establishment) Act, 2007. Section 10 establishes the **Federal Road Safety Corps** for the Commission. The Commission and the operational Corps are distinct legal bodies/roles; “FRSC” is commonly used for the institution, but a lesson should distinguish them where it describes operational duties.

**Primary legal basis and date:** Federal Road Safety Commission (Establishment) Act, 2007, Act No. 22; dated 25 May 2007.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 10(2)–(3) gives the Corps highway-safety functions, accident prevention/minimisation, clearing highway obstructions, public/driver education, design and production of drivers' licences and vehicle number plates, and determination of requirements for licence applicants. Section 10(4)–(7) provides enforcement powers for traffic offences, and section 15 states that Corps operations cover all public highways.

**Source assessment:** The source's use of “Federal Road Safety Commission (FRSC)” is the statutory Commission name, but the day-to-day highway functions it describes are principally functions of the Corps established under section 10. The Act does assign driver-licence design/production and applicant requirements to the Corps; do not simplify this into an unsupported claim that the Commission alone performs every licensing step.

Source: [Federal Road Safety Commission (Establishment) Act, 2007 — official FRSC PDF](https://frsc.gov.ng/FRSCACT2007.pdf), sections 1, 10 and 15.

### 9. Code of Conduct Bureau (CCB) and Code of Conduct Tribunal (CCT) — source p.191 and p.202

**Current legal names:** Code of Conduct Bureau and, separately, Code of Conduct Tribunal.

**Primary legal basis and dates:**
- Constitution sections 153, 172, Third Schedule Part I A and Fifth Schedule (1999 Constitution as amended; 2023 consolidated text).
- Code of Conduct Bureau and Tribunal Act, 1989, Act No. 1; commencement 1 January 1991.

**Mandate/jurisdiction:**
- The Bureau receives public officers' asset declarations, examines them, retains custody and receives complaints about breaches/non-compliance with the Code of Conduct or related law. Under Third Schedule Part I A paragraph 3(e), it investigates complaints and, where appropriate, refers matters to the Tribunal.
- The Tribunal is a separate adjudicative body established under Fifth Schedule Part I paragraph 15. It determines whether a public officer breached the Code and may impose the constitutional sanctions in paragraph 18, subject to appeal to the Court of Appeal.

**Source assessment:** The source is broadly correct that the Bureau handles asset declarations and complaints and may refer matters to the Tribunal. It must distinguish the Bureau from the Tribunal: the Bureau is not the adjudicating Tribunal and the Tribunal is not the Bureau. Their jurisdiction concerns the Code of Conduct and public officers, not general law-and-order complaints from the public.

Sources:
- [Constitution, Third Schedule Part I A and Fifth Schedule](https://placng.org/i/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Constitution-of-the-Federal-Republic-of-Nigeria-2023.pdf).
- [Code of Conduct Bureau and Tribunal Act, 1989 — PLAC Laws of Nigeria PDF](https://www.lawsofnigeria.placng.org/laws/C15.pdf), sections 1–3 and 20–25.

### 10. Public Complaints Commission (PCC) — source p.185 and p.203

**Current legal name:** Public Complaints Commission.

**Primary legal basis and date:** Public Complaints Commission Act, 1975, Act No. 31; commencement 16 October 1975, with amendment noted as 1979 No. 21. The Act is also preserved by Constitution section 315(5)(b).

**Mandate/jurisdiction:** Section 5(2) authorises a Commissioner to investigate, on their own initiative or on complaint, administrative action by Federal or State ministries/departments, local government departments, statutory corporations/public institutions, companies incorporated under the Companies and Allied Matters Act, and officers/servants of those bodies. Section 6 imposes restrictions, including matters pending before a court, matters outside the Commissioner's terms, certain matters concerning the Armed Forces/Police under their own Acts, and cases where available remedies have not been exhausted. Section 7 allows recommendations after investigation and referral/reporting where crime or disciplinary conduct is found.

**Source assessment:** The source's description of PCC investigating administrative complaints is broadly supported, but its remit is not unlimited. The Commission is an ombudsman-type administrative-complaints body, not a general police or prosecution agency. It makes recommendations/reports to appropriate authorities and is subject to statutory restrictions; it does not replace the courts or the police.

Source: [Public Complaints Commission Act — PLAC Laws of Nigeria](https://www.placng.org/lawsofnigeria/print.php?sn=464), sections 1 and 5–7.

## Cross-agency conclusions for later lesson work

1. The source's agency list is not one undifferentiated “law and order” service. The agencies have distinct legal purposes, jurisdictions and powers.
2. Police have the general public-safety/law-and-order remit; Armed Forces are principally for defence/territorial integrity and only aid civil authorities in the constitutionally prescribed circumstances.
3. The Correctional Service is the current institution replacing the Nigerian Prisons Service; it has custodial and non-custodial responsibilities.
4. Customs revenue means customs/excise duties and assigned charges/fees, not every tax; its police-like powers are for customs-law enforcement.
5. EFCC and ICPC are distinct: economic/financial crimes versus corrupt practices/related offences.
6. The Federal Road Safety Commission and Federal Road Safety Corps are distinct legal roles; highway operations are primarily assigned to the Corps.
7. The Code of Conduct Bureau and Tribunal are distinct and have a public-officer/code-of-conduct jurisdiction.
8. The Public Complaints Commission investigates administrative action within statutory scope and restrictions; it is not a general criminal enforcement agency.
9. “Organs of government” means the legislature, executive and judiciary, not a single agency.
10. Any student-facing statement about powers, arrest, prosecution, expulsion, agency boundaries or current names must cite the applicable Act/constitutional provision and its effective date.

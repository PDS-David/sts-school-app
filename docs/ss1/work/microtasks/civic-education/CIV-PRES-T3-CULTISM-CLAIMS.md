## Source claims preserved for review

### Membership, conduct and characteristics — p44–53

- p44 defines cultism as membership/operation of cults and claims secret activities, unacceptable behaviour, prevalence in tertiary institutions and some secondary schools, secret meeting locations and night operation.
- p46 claims members wear expensive clothes, are bold/daring and enforce their will on people.
- p47 claims secret initiation, rituals and oaths of secrecy.
- p48 claims members assist one another and promote members' interests without regard to reason, justice, fairness, legitimacy or appropriateness.
- p49 claims members use signs, symbols and passwords recognised only by members.
- p50 characterises members as apparently kind/quiet/generous but deceitful/hypocritical and potentially violent/deadly.
- p51 contains a concealed-weapons claim; the source wording is retained only at its locator and is not expanded or operationalised.
- p52 claims excessive alcohol and hard-drug use.
- p53 uses the term “womanizers”.

These are broad generalisations about a diverse, unnamed population. They are not verified characteristics of every member or any identifiable student. The list contains moralising and gendered stereotypes; future teaching must avoid presenting them as diagnostic indicators.

### Historical/traditional associations — p81–87

The source refers to Ogboni, Ekine, Ekpe and Okonko and gives an account of the Pyrates Confraternity, University of Ibadan and 1952, its stated aims, expulsions/splintering and subsequent growth of campus cults. The exact text is indexed at `CIV-WK-T3-W02-03.md`; historical verification is handled separately by `CIV-FACT-T3-CULTISM-HISTORY`. The traditional associations are not treated as equivalent to modern campus secret cults by this preservation record.

### Consequences, criminality and mental-health claims — p97–104

- p99 claims cultism promotes violence and describes attacks between rival groups.
- p100 uses the phrase “potential murderers” and claims killings of rivals or people considered opponents.
- p101 says members disregard school regulations and enact their own laws.
- p102 says activities disrupt school activities.
- p103 links cultism to murder, immorality, violence, crime, drunkenness and drug abuse.
- p104 says members develop “serious mental ill-health especially madness”.

These are the source's claims, not verified prevalence, causation or diagnosis. “Madness” is stigmatising and medically imprecise; it must not be used as a clinical label or repeated as an established mental-health consequence. Do not infer criminality, violence or mental illness from rumour, appearance, association or group name.

### Government and society claims — p106–117

- p106–110 describes a matriculation oath, expulsion of a student found to be in a cult, and prosecution/jail terms for persons arrested and found guilty. No statute, school policy, date, jurisdiction or due-process detail is cited.
- p112–117 describes social disapproval/avoidance, parental restrictions, employer refusal to employ cult members, and at p116 says accusations of crime/theft occur “whether they are guilty of it or not”.

The expulsion/prosecution statements need current legal and institutional verification. The social claims are generalisations; p116 explicitly acknowledges accusations without regard to guilt and must not be used to legitimise profiling or collective blame.

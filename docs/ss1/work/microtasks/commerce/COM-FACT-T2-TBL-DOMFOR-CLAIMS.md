### Row 2 — currencies

**Generally true:** countries usually issue their own national currency, so foreign trade usually involves a different currency on each side.

**Exceptions (both established):**
1. *Countries that share a currency.* Bulgaria became the 21st euro-area member on 1 January 2026 (ECB press release, 1 Jan 2026; Eurostat glossary "Euro area", undated, lists the same 21). Trade between, for example, two euro-area countries is trade between countries (foreign trade) with no currency difference. In West Africa, BCEAO's own page (undated) states that eight States share the currency it issues: Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Guinea-Bissau, Mali, Niger, Senegal and Togo. Benin and Niger border Nigeria.
2. *Invoicing in a third currency.* ECB special feature "Global trade invoicing patterns" (Brüggen, Georgiadis, Mehl, 11 June 2025; data to 2023): about 40% of global exports are invoiced in US dollars, a share much larger than the share of exports going to the United States; the euro's share is above 40%; the two together exceed 80% of global invoicing. The paper defines a vehicle currency as one that belongs to neither the exporter nor the importer but to a third country. It does not settle how Nigeria's own trade is invoiced, which was not asked.

**Why "not endorsed as written":** read literally, the cell says every foreign sale uses different currencies. Exception 1 contradicts that for currency-union partners; exception 2 shows a single agreed currency (often neither party's own) is common. The national currencies still usually differ, so the cell is not false in general. It is incomplete as a universal.

**Endorsed claim limits for a supplement** (a boundary on what may be claimed, not teaching text):
- may say: countries usually have different national currencies, so foreign trade often needs one currency converted into another;
- may say: some countries share a currency (the euro; the West African CFA franc), and a foreign-trade contract may be priced in one agreed currency, which may be either party's or a third country's;
- may name the euro, the West African CFA franc and the US dollar with the dated sources above;
- if a count of euro-area countries is used, date it ("21 from 1 January 2026") and recheck before publishing; the ECB list was last confirmed here for that date only;
- must not say that all foreign trade involves different currencies, or that currencies differ in every foreign transaction.

### Row 3 — weights and measures

**Not true as a general statement of foreign trade today.**
1. *International position.* BIPM (SI Brochure, 9th edition, current version 4.01, June 2026): the SI has been the preferred system of units worldwide for science, technology, industry and trade since its establishment in 1960 (paraphrase of the brochure's preface). BIPM news item of 28 August 2024 describes the SI as the only globally agreed system of units.
2. *United States — the main exception to check.* 15 U.S.C. §205a(3) records Congress's finding that world trade is increasingly geared towards the metric system; §205b designates the metric system as the *preferred* system for US trade and commerce. "Preferred" is not "exclusive": the statute does not make metric the only lawful system. How much customary-unit use remains in US trade was not measured here and is not endorsed either way.
3. *Nigeria.* Weights and Measures Act, Cap. W3, Laws of the Federation of Nigeria 2004 (1974 No. 32, commenced 1 June 1975), text read from the PLAC copy of Cap. W3; FMITI's Weights and Measures Department page names this Act as its governing law:
   - s.1(1): the metre is the unit of length and the kilogramme the unit of mass for any measurement made in Nigeria.
   - s.2(2): Nigeria's primary standards are to be verified against the standards of the International Bureau of Weights and Measures.
   - s.6(1)(a): no person may use for trade a unit of measurement not in the First Schedule (metric units).
   - s.52(1): from 1 January 1979 it is unlawful to possess for trade, sell or import weights, measures, instruments or goods in imperial or other than exclusively metric units.
   - s.51(2): "use for trade" does not include goods for despatch to a destination outside Nigeria (apart from retail tolls, duties and carriage), so the Act does not by itself say what units Nigeria's exports are measured in.
   - s.6(8): for aviation and mineral oil the section is subject to generally accepted international convention and usage.

**Why "not to be taught as written":** the cell says different weights and measures are used in foreign trade as a general feature. Today most trade uses the metric/SI system, and Nigeria itself has been metric by law since 1979. A student could learn that a Nigerian importer and a foreign exporter normally measure in different systems, which is not supported. Historically, countries did use different systems (Nigeria's Department records the 1917 replacement of traditional measures by the imperial system), but that is a history point, not the present cell.

**Endorsed claim limits for a supplement:**
- may say: historically countries used different systems of weights and measures, which made trade harder;
- may say: today the metric system (SI) is the internationally agreed system and is the preferred system of world trade; some countries, such as the United States, have not made it the only system in law;
- may say: Nigeria's law makes the metre and kilogramme its units and, since 1 January 1979, requires exclusively metric units for trade within Nigeria (Cap. W3 ss.1, 6, 52), citing the Act;
- must not say that foreign trade generally uses different weights and measures, or that exports from Nigeria are weighed in non-metric units;
- must not extend s.52 to exports (see s.51(2)).

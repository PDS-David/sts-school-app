## Findings
1. **Number of elements:** “over one hundred” is true but imprecise. IUPAC recognises 118 named elements in the current periodic table. A later correction may state the exact current count with an IUPAC table citation.
2. **Element names:** the source spellings “Bramine”, “Berylium” and “Hydragyrium” are typographical errors for Bromine, Beryllium and Mercury. These corrections must be made only in a separately labelled authored layer.
3. **Lithium symbol:** lithium's official chemical symbol is `Li`, not `L`. Element symbols are case-sensitive; the source's `L` is not a valid symbol for lithium.
4. **Metalloids and element classification:** the metal/non-metal boundary is not absolute; metalloids have intermediate or mixed properties. Classification examples can vary by textbook convention. Graphite is a well-known electrically conducting allotrope of carbon, which is a non-metal.
5. **Mercury:** mercury is a metal and is liquid at 20°C. The source's exception to the general “metals are solids” statement is correct.
6. **Metals/non-metals:** “they are solids (except mercury)” is too broad for non-metals: many non-metals are gases at room temperature and bromine is a liquid. Graphite's conductivity is a valid exception to a blanket “non-metals do not conduct” statement. Properties should be expressed as tendencies with exceptions, not universal rules.
7. **Valency versus oxidation state:** IUPAC's Gold Book defines valence in terms of combining capacity; oxidation state is a separate electron-counting concept with a signed value. A negative sign belongs to an oxidation-state or ionic-charge description, not to a simple valency table. Do not silently treat the source's negative valency entries as standard signed valencies.
8. **Sulfur and carbon:** sulfur and carbon can exhibit multiple oxidation states in different compounds. The source's “−2, −4 or −6” for sulfur and “−2 or −4” for carbon require the relevant concept and examples to be distinguished; they should not be presented as a universal valency list.
9. **Argon:** argon is a noble gas with zero conventional valency in the introductory context. This is a school-level simplification; it should not be used to imply that noble-gas chemistry is impossible under all conditions.
10. **Radical table notation:** the source's `0H-`, `N03-`, `N02-`, `C032-`, `S042-` and `HC03-` use the digit zero where the letter O is intended. The likely intended formulas are OH⁻, NO₃⁻, NO₂⁻, CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻ and HCO₃⁻. The original formatted-run preservation is separate; any corrected formula must be explicitly reviewed before publication.
11. **“Radical” terminology:** the source uses “radical” for polyatomic ions. Modern chemistry distinguishes radicals (species with unpaired electrons) from polyatomic ions; the school usage is historical/legacy terminology and needs careful clarification rather than silent replacement.
12. **Reading assignment:** the reference spelling “Collegs” is a typographical error. The exact edition and page range must be matched to the assigned book copy.

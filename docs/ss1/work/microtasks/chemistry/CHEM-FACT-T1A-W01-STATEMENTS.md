## Findings

### 1. Scientific method and the word “Pattern”
**Source locator:** lines 129–149; the seven labels at 133–145 are Observation, Pattern, Problem, Hypothesis, Experiments, Theory and Law.

**Finding:** These are recognisable ideas used in scientific inquiry, but the source presents them as a fixed sequence. There is no single universal step-by-step method followed by every scientific investigation. Scientific inquiry is iterative and context-dependent: observations can lead to questions, hypotheses and predictions; investigations generate evidence; interpretation may revise the original question or hypothesis; and findings are scrutinised and communicated. The word “pattern” can describe a relationship noticed in observations or data, but it is not a universally required named stage placed in a fixed position between observation and problem. The source's list should therefore be treated as a simplified classroom model, not a universal law of how science works.

**Evidence:** National Science Teaching Association, *Nature of Science* position statement (the official statement says no single universal step-by-step method captures the complexity of science); National Research Council, *Taking Science to School* (2007), Chapter 5, pp. 132–133 (questioning and hypothesis formulation are iterative phases, and investigations may begin in different ways); University of Calgary, *The Scientific Method* (states there is no single route and that scientific progress is not neat and linear).

### 2. Hypothesis, theory and scientific law
**Source locator:** line 149.

**Finding:** The source calls a hypothesis a “prediction or guesses” and says a tested hypothesis becomes a theory, then a sufficiently tested theory becomes a law. This is scientifically inaccurate:
- A hypothesis is a tentative, testable explanation or proposed answer to a question. A prediction is an expected result derived from a hypothesis; the two terms are related but not interchangeable.
- A scientific theory is a well-supported explanatory framework, not simply a hypothesis that has passed one test.
- A scientific law describes a regularity or relationship in nature; a theory explains phenomena and may explain why a law-like relationship occurs. A theory does **not** turn into a law after enough testing. They are different kinds of scientific knowledge.
- Scientific explanations remain open to scrutiny and revision when strong new evidence requires it; “proven correct without any exception” is not a suitable description of how scientific theories are established.

**Evidence:** National Science Teaching Association, *Nature of Science* (theories do not become laws); National Academies, *Next Generation Science Standards: For States, By States*, Appendix H / understandings about the nature of science (laws describe relationships; theories are explanations and do not become laws or facts); University of Calgary, *The Scientific Method* (hypotheses are tentative explanations; laws summarise observations while theories explain them).

### 3. Three branches and their descriptions
**Source locator:** lines 109–125.

**Finding:** The source names physical, organic and inorganic chemistry as “three main branches”. This is a simplified traditional grouping, not an exhaustive modern classification. Other authoritative educational and professional bodies also identify analytical chemistry and biochemistry as major areas; the American Chemical Society's Areas of Chemistry page lists analytical, biological/biochemistry, inorganic, organic and physical chemistry, as well as chemical engineering.

Specific content findings:
- **Physical chemistry:** “physical aspect ... like quantum theory, heat and electricity” is too narrow and vague. Physical chemistry studies how matter and chemical systems behave and how chemical reactions occur, including energy, thermodynamics, kinetics and molecular/atomic behaviour.
- **Organic chemistry:** the short phrase “carbon and its compounds” is a useful first approximation, but must not imply that every carbon-containing compound is classified as organic. Some carbon compounds, such as carbon oxides and carbonates, are conventionally treated in inorganic chemistry.
- **Inorganic chemistry:** “elements, their properties and uses” is incomplete because inorganic chemistry also studies inorganic compounds and their structures, properties and reactions.
- The source's three names may be retained as its introductory grouping, but the package should explain that chemistry has additional areas and overlapping specialisations rather than presenting exactly three as the complete modern taxonomy.

**Evidence:** American Chemical Society, *Areas of Chemistry*; Lehigh University Department of Chemistry, *Traditional Divisions* (identifies five traditional divisions); IUPAC, *Compendium of Chemical Terminology* (Gold Book, Version 5.0.0, 2025), as the terminology reference. The IUPAC Gold Book does not prescribe a single exhaustive list of “branches”.

### 4. Definition of chemistry
**Source locator:** lines 103–105.

**Finding:** The source's definition—study of the nature, structure, composition, properties and uses of matter and changes in its structure and composition—is broadly serviceable for an introductory SS1 lesson. It is not a complete technical definition, and the wider teaching should make clear that chemical transformations also involve energy changes. No standalone correction task is required solely for this sentence; the follow-up teaching should clarify its scope without silently replacing the original wording.

## Findings register
| Source locator | Source statement / issue | Finding and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| 1129 and 1197 | Introductory list says “fractional precipitation”; body heading is “PRECIPITATION” | Precipitation is a separation process in which a dissolved substance is converted into an insoluble solid. Fractional precipitation can describe selective precipitation under controlled conditions, but it is not interchangeable with fractional crystallisation. The intended method name needs review against the exact surrounding paragraph and source syllabus. |
| 1171-1173 | Crystallisation suits salts that “decompose easily on heating”; term “seedling” | Crystallisation can be preferred over strong evaporation where a substance is heat-sensitive, but the procedure must be matched to the substance and its solubility curve. In crystallisation, the relevant term is usually “seeding” (adding a small crystal to encourage nucleation); “seedling” is not the standard chemistry term in this context. |
| 1199 | Iron(II) sulphate and ethanol precipitation example; formula extracted as “FeS04” | The intended formula is FeSO₄ (subscript and letter O, not digit zero; see the separate formula-preservation record). Iron(II) sulfate is soluble in water and is much less soluble in ethanol; adding ethanol to an aqueous solution can reduce solubility and cause precipitation. The source formula is not corrected here. |
| 1205 | Iodine and ammonium chloride listed as sublimation examples | Iodine is a standard sublimation example. Ammonium chloride is commonly used in school demonstrations, but its vapour-phase behaviour involves dissociation into ammonia and hydrogen chloride and recombination on cooling; it should not be described as a simple molecular sublimation without qualification. Any demonstration requires a separate safety review. |
| 1221 | “(less than 100oC” with an unclosed bracket | This is an incomplete parenthesis and a degree-symbol/notation issue. A boiling point is pressure-dependent; a temperature value should be tied to a stated pressure and substance. Do not generalise “less than 100°C” to all liquids. |
| 1215 | “vapourising” | This is an accepted British English spelling of “vaporising”; it is not necessarily a scientific error. Preserve local spelling unless the school style guide requires otherwise. |
| 1285-1287 | Criteria for purity | Purity tests should be stated with the specific substance and method. A fixed melting or boiling point can be evidence of purity for a suitable pure substance at a stated pressure, but mixtures may show ranges and impurities can shift or broaden transitions. The exact source criteria require line-by-line review before correction. |

## Safety boundary
The source descriptions do not provide a full risk assessment, emergency response, disposal procedure or supervised practical protocol. This record adds none. Potassium permanganate is an oxidising agent and must be kept away from incompatible combustible materials; the PubChem safety summary identifies it as a strong oxidiser. Do not use the source as an unsupervised practical instruction.

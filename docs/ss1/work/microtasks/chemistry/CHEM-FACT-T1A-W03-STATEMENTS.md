## Findings
1. **Matter definition:** the source says “has weight”. OpenStax defines matter as anything that occupies space and has mass, and explicitly distinguishes mass from weight: weight is gravitational force and changes with gravitational field; mass does not. For precise teaching, “mass” is the correct property, not “weight”.
2. **States of matter:** a solid has a definite shape and volume; a liquid has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container; a gas has neither a definite shape nor a definite volume and expands to fill its container. “Fixed mass” is not an intrinsic state property: the mass depends on how much sample is present.
3. **Gas-particle speed:** particles do not all move at one “fixed speed”. Molecular speeds are distributed and depend on temperature and molecular mass. The source's wording at lines 479–485 is not an accurate general statement.
4. **Gas compressibility:** gases are compressible because particles are much farther apart than in liquids and solids; this supports the general idea but not a claim that gas particles move at a fixed speed.
5. **Physical changes:** phase changes and dissolving common salt in water are normally physical changes because no new chemical species are produced by the process as described. “Liquefaction of gases to solids” is not the correct phase-change name: gas directly becoming solid is deposition/desublimation; liquefaction refers to gas becoming liquid.
6. **Chemical changes:** reaction of metals with acids and limestone (mainly calcium carbonate) with acids are chemical reactions. Electrochemical cells involve redox processes. These examples are chemically plausible but should be described with the actual reactants and products in a later lesson.
7. **Energy in physical changes:** the source's “very little energy changes take place” is false as a general rule. Melting, vaporisation and other physical changes can involve substantial latent heat. Energy transfer does not by itself distinguish physical from chemical change.
8. **Mass:** mass is conserved in a closed system during physical and chemical changes. It is not a feature that separates physical change from chemical change; an open system may appear to gain or lose mass if matter enters or leaves.
9. **Weekend assignment:** “carbon (iv) oxide” is an older/Stock-style naming form for carbon dioxide; no issue is inferred solely from the Roman numeral. The question “reaction of water with sodium chloride” is misleading if it intends ordinary dissolving: sodium chloride dissolves in water to form a solution; this is not ordinarily a chemical reaction. A question about dissolution should be phrased accordingly.
10. **Headings and reference:** “TYPES OF CHANGE” (line 427) versus “TYPES OF CHANGES” (line 497) is a source consistency issue, not a scientific discrepancy. “Ababio.Pg 8 -9” should be matched to the actual assigned edition before publication.

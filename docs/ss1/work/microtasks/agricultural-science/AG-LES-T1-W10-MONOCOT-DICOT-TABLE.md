## Lesson body

Week 10 Supplement: Seven Differences Between Monocotyledonous and Dicotyledonous Plants

## 1. Meaning of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants

Flowering plants are called angiosperms. A young plant embryo inside a seed has one or more seed leaves called **cotyledons**.

- **Monocotyledonous plants (monocots)** have one cotyledon in the embryo.
- **Dicotyledonous plants (dicots)** traditionally refers to flowering plants with two cotyledons. Many familiar plants in this school-level group are more precisely called **eudicots** in modern classification.

The number of cotyledons is one important difference. Monocots and dicots also commonly differ in their leaves, roots, stems, flowers, pollen and patterns of growth.

## 2. Seven differences between monocots and dicots

| Feature | Monocotyledonous plants (monocots) | Dicotyledonous plants (dicots, mainly eudicots) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cotyledons (seed leaves) | The embryo has **one cotyledon**. Example: maize. | The embryo has **two cotyledons**. Example: bean. |
| 2. Leaf venation | Leaves usually have **parallel venation**: the main veins run alongside one another along the leaf. | Leaves usually have **reticulate or net-like venation**: smaller veins branch and form a network. |
| 3. Root system | The mature plant usually has a **fibrous or adventitious root system**, with many roots of similar size and no dominant taproot. | The plant usually develops a **taproot system**, with one main root and smaller lateral roots. |
| 4. Stem vascular bundles | Vascular bundles containing xylem and phloem are usually **scattered** through the stem. | Vascular bundles are usually **arranged in a ring** near the outer part of the stem. |
| 5. Arrangement of flower parts | Flower parts are usually in **threes or multiples of three**. | Flower parts are usually in **fours or fives, or multiples of four or five**. |
| 6. Pollen grains | Pollen grains usually have **one main furrow or aperture**; this is called monosulcate pollen. | Pollen grains of most eudicots usually have **three furrows or apertures**; these are described as tricolpate, trisulcate or triporate, depending on their form. |
| 7. Secondary growth | Conventional secondary growth from a vascular cambium is generally absent or limited in most monocots. Some tree-like monocots, such as palms, increase in stem thickness by different thickening processes. | Secondary growth from a vascular cambium is common in many woody eudicots. It produces additional xylem and phloem and increases the thickness of stems and roots. Not every dicot is woody or shows secondary growth. |

## 3. Examples of monocots and dicots

### Monocot examples

- Maize
- Rice
- Wheat
- Sugarcane
- Banana
- Onion

### Dicot examples

- Bean
- Cowpea
- Groundnut
- Tomato
- Mango
- Sunflower

A plant should not be classified only by its common name or one visible feature. Compare several characteristics where possible. For example, maize has one cotyledon, parallel-veined leaves, a fibrous root system and scattered stem vascular bundles. A bean plant has two cotyledons, net-veined leaves and usually a taproot.

## 4. How to use the differences when identifying a plant

1. **Look at the seed or seedling:** one cotyledon suggests a monocot; two suggest a dicot.
2. **Examine the leaves:** parallel veins commonly suggest a monocot; a net-like pattern commonly suggests a dicot.
3. **Observe the roots:** many similar fibrous roots commonly suggest a monocot; one main taproot commonly suggests a dicot.
4. **If a stem cross-section is available:** scattered vascular bundles commonly suggest a monocot; bundles arranged in a ring commonly suggest a dicot.
5. **If flowers or pollen can be examined:** flower-part numbers and pollen apertures provide additional evidence.

For reliable identification, use a combination of features. Do not depend on one feature alone.

## 5. Important scientific note: these are general patterns

The table gives the usual school-level differences, not rules without exceptions. Some monocots have unusual leaf venation or increase in thickness by special growth processes. Some plants traditionally called dicots do not have a typical taproot or do not develop woody secondary growth. A few species also have flower-part numbers that differ from the usual pattern.

Modern plant classification recognises eudicots as a major group of flowering plants; the traditional word “dicot” is still commonly used in introductory lessons. The scientific patterns in the table are useful for learning and comparison, but botanists consider several features together when classifying a plant.

## 6. Quick revision

1. Monocots have one cotyledon; dicots have two.
2. Monocots usually have parallel leaf veins; dicots usually have net-like veins.
3. Monocots usually have fibrous roots; dicots usually have a taproot.
4. Monocot stem vascular bundles are usually scattered; dicot bundles are usually in a ring.
5. Monocot flower parts are usually in threes; dicot flower parts are usually in fours or fives.
6. Monocot pollen usually has one aperture; eudicot pollen usually has three.
7. Secondary growth is usually limited in monocots but common in many woody eudicots.

Remember: use several characteristics together because each feature has exceptions.

## Practice and complete marking guide

### P1 — Seven paired differences (14 marks)

**Question:** State seven differences between monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants in a table.

**Answer:**
1. Cotyledons: monocots one; dicots two.
2. Leaf venation: monocots usually parallel; dicots usually reticulate/net-like.
3. Roots: monocots usually fibrous/adventitious; dicots usually taproot.
4. Stem vascular bundles: monocots usually scattered; dicots usually arranged in a ring.
5. Flower parts: monocots usually in threes/multiples of three; dicots usually in fours or fives/multiples of four or five.
6. Pollen: monocots usually monosulcate (one furrow/aperture); most eudicots usually tricolpate/trisulcate/triporate (three furrows/apertures).
7. Secondary growth: conventional vascular-cambium secondary growth is generally absent or limited in most monocots; it is common in many woody eudicots, though exceptions occur.

### P3 — Identify an unknown plant (4 marks)

**Question:** An unknown flowering plant has parallel-veined leaves, scattered stem vascular bundles and flower parts in threes. Which group is it most likely to belong to? Give two reasons for your answer.

**Answer:** It is most likely a monocot. Parallel leaf venation, scattered stem vascular bundles and flower parts in threes are all characteristics commonly associated with monocots.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Identifies the plant as most likely a monocot.
- 2 marks — Gives two correct supporting features, one mark each.

### P4 — Recognise exceptions (4 marks)

**Question:** A learner says, “All monocots can never increase in stem thickness, and every dicot must have a taproot.” Explain why this statement is too absolute.

**Answer:** These are general patterns, not rules without exceptions. Most monocots have little or no conventional vascular-cambium secondary growth, but some tree-like monocots such as palms increase in thickness through other thickening processes. Dicots usually have taproots, but some have adventitious or modified root systems. Several features should be considered together when identifying a plant.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains that monocot secondary growth is usually limited but some monocots such as palms thicken by other processes.
- 2 marks — Explains that taproots are typical of many dicots but not universal and that several features should be considered.

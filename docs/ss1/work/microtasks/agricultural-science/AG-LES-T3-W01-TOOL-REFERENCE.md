# Third Term, Week One: Simple Farm Tools — Illustrated Reference

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Identify and describe all 30 farm tools in the original Week One list.
2. Match each tool to an appropriate farm operation and explain one suitable use.
3. State practical care and maintenance for each tool and explain why maintenance matters.
4. Select at least seven tools that can be used during post-planting operations and justify each choice.
5. Complete a drawing-book project using labelled illustrations and observations of real tools.

## 1. What is a simple farm tool?

A simple farm tool is a hand-operated implement used by a farmer to carry out a particular operation. It helps a person perform work such as cutting, digging, loosening soil, planting, carrying materials, watering, pruning or repairing equipment. Most simple tools are operated by human effort; they do not need an engine.

Tools are designed for different jobs. A cutting tool should not be used as a digging tool, and a screwdriver should not be used as a chisel. Choosing the right tool makes work more effective and helps prevent damage and injury.

## 3. The five selected tools described in the assigned source

The original Week One note gives fuller descriptions for five selected tools. This supplement retains those five as the core source examples and clarifies the distinctions needed for correct identification:

- **Cutlass:** the source describes a short metal blade, often with a curved end, attached to a wooden or rubber handle. It is used for land clearing, planting, weeding and cutting small trunks or pegs.
- **Spade and shovel:** the source describes long-handled tools with metal blades. A spade has a relatively flat blade for cutting into and turning soil; a shovel has a dished or scoop-shaped blade for lifting and moving loose material. The source associates them with digging, spreading manure and turning or mixing soil.
- **Garden fork:** the source describes four strong, pointed metal prongs on a long handle. It is used for digging, loosening and breaking soil.
- **Secateurs:** the source describes a scissor-like tool with two short blades joined by a pivot, with handles and a spring. It is used for preparing suitable stem cuttings and pruning small branches of woody plants.
- **Emasculator:** the source describes a strong pincer-like veterinary instrument. The anatomically correct term is spermatic cord, not “spermatid cord”. An emasculator is used by trained veterinary personnel during castration; it crushes and cuts the spermatic cord in one action to help limit bleeding. This lesson does not teach or authorise a castration procedure.

## 4. Tools for post-planting operations

Post-planting operations are activities carried out after seeds or seedlings have been planted. They may include watering, weeding, supplying missing plants, thinning, mulching, manuring or applying fertiliser, earthing up, supporting plants, and pruning where the crop requires it. The correct operation and timing depend on the crop and local recommendations.

| Tool from the list | Post-planting operation | Why it is suitable |
|---|---|---|
| Hoe (No. 2) | Shallow weeding and earthing up | Its blade loosens surface soil and removes weeds between crop plants or rows. Work carefully to avoid cutting crop roots. |
| Cutlass (No. 1) | Slashing tall weeds between rows | It can cut larger weeds that are difficult to remove with a small hand tool. Keep the blade well away from crop stems and people. |
| Hand fork (No. 6) | Hand weeding and shallow loosening | Its short tines work in small spaces around established plants. Avoid deep digging close to roots. |
| Rake (No. 27) | Collecting weeds and spreading light mulch | The teeth gather removed weeds and plant debris; the back of the head can help level loose surface material. |
| Watering can (No. 23) | Watering seedlings and newly transplanted crops | A rose attachment can spread water gently and reduce disturbance of the soil around young plants. |
| Head pan (No. 10) | Carrying compost, mulch or harvested weeds | It carries small loads from a store or compost area to the plot. |
| Wheel barrow (No. 18) | Moving manure, compost, mulch and farm inputs | Its tray and wheel carry heavier loads than a person should carry by hand. |
| Shovel (No. 4) | Moving compost or manure | Its scoop lifts loose material into a head pan, wheelbarrow or application container. |
| Secateurs (No. 26) | Pruning suitable shoots | They make controlled cuts on small branches where pruning is recommended for that crop. |

Seven examples for the source evaluation are: hoe, cutlass, hand fork, rake, watering can, head pan and wheel barrow. The shovel and secateurs are two additional examples. A tool is selected because of the operation being done; not every tool is used on every crop or at every growth stage.

## 5. General maintenance of farm tools

The assigned source gives six general maintenance practices. They are retained here:

1. Keep tools with wooden parts in a termite-free store. Termites can damage wooden handles and make a tool unsafe.
2. Oil or grease metal parts. A thin protective film helps reduce rust on suitable metal surfaces.
3. Paint metal parts before long storage when appropriate. Do not paint cutting edges, working surfaces, joints or surfaces that must fit together.
4. Sharpen blunt edges regularly and safely. A blunt cutting edge can require more force and may slip. Sharpening must be supervised and done with the correct equipment.
5. Clean or wash tools after each use. Remove soil, sap, manure and plant residue. Dry metal parts before storage.
6. Keep nozzles free from blockage. This is especially important for watering cans and other tools that distribute water.

Additional good practice:
- Inspect handles, joints, wheels, nuts and bolts before use. Do not use a loose, cracked or badly bent tool.
- Use the tool only for the operation for which it was designed.
- Store sharp or pointed tools with their edges covered and out of children's reach.
- Keep tools in a dry, organised store; do not leave tools on paths or where people may step on them.
- Lubricate moving joints, such as the pivot of secateurs or shears, where the manufacturer recommends it.
- Clean manure-handling equipment separately from equipment used to handle harvested food.

## 6. Safe handling

- Wear suitable footwear and clothing; use gloves and eye protection when the operation requires them.
- Carry cutlasses, axes, sickles, knives and other sharp tools with the cutting edge controlled and protected. Never run with a sharp tool.
- Keep a safe distance between workers when cutting, digging or swinging a tool.
- Pass a sharp tool handle-first, with its cutting edge directed away from the receiver.
- Do not use a tool with a loose head, cracked handle, damaged blade or faulty wheel.
- Do not use a screwdriver as a chisel, a spanner as a hammer, or a shovel as a lever.
- Use a crowbar, axe, mattock or other heavy tool only with teacher supervision and enough clear space.
- Emasculators are veterinary instruments. Students must not use them on animals; examination is by sight or demonstration by a qualified veterinary professional only.

## 8. Check your understanding

### Question 1 (6 marks)
Name the tool that is most suitable for each operation: (a) carrying compost across the farm; (b) watering newly transplanted seedlings gently; (c) loosening soil around a seedling in a small nursery bed; (d) cutting a thick branch too large for secateurs; (e) tightening a nut on a wheelbarrow; (f) collecting leaves and removed weeds from a seedbed.

**Answer:** (a) Wheel barrow; (b) Watering can; (c) Hand fork; (d) Pruning saw; (e) Spanner; (f) Rake.

**Marking:** 1 mark for each correct tool.

### Question 2 (4 marks)
State two differences between a spade and a shovel.

**Answer:** A spade has a relatively flat blade and is used mainly for cutting into and turning soil. A shovel has a dished or scoop-shaped blade and is used mainly for lifting and moving loose materials such as soil, compost or manure.

**Marking:** 1 mark for identifying the spade's flatter blade; 1 mark for its soil-cutting/turning use; 1 mark for identifying the shovel's scoop-shaped blade; 1 mark for its lifting/carrying use.

### Question 3 (4 marks)
Explain the difference between a garden fork and a hand fork.

**Answer:** A garden fork is larger, has a long handle and strong tines, and is used for digging or loosening a larger area of soil. A hand fork is smaller, has a short handle and shorter tines, and is used for shallow work around seedlings or in small beds.

**Marking:** 2 marks for the size/handle/tine distinction; 2 marks for the difference in suitable operation.

### Question 4 (6 marks)
State six general maintenance practices for farm tools.

**Answer:** Store wooden-handled tools away from termites; oil or grease suitable metal parts; paint suitable metal parts before long storage but not working edges or joints; sharpen blunt edges safely; clean or wash tools after use and dry them; keep watering-can nozzles clear.

**Marking:** 1 mark for each correct practice, up to 6.

### Question 5 (7 marks)
List seven tools from the source list that can be used in post-planting operations and state one suitable operation for each.

**Answer:** Hoe—shallow weeding/earthing up; cutlass—slashing tall weeds between rows; hand fork—shallow loosening/hand weeding; rake—collecting weeds or spreading light mulch; watering can—watering seedlings; head pan—carrying compost or mulch; wheel barrow—transporting manure or other inputs.

**Marking:** 1 mark for each correct tool-operation pair, up to 7.

### Question 6 (4 marks)
Classify each tool by its main function: secateurs, spanner, shovel and watering can.

**Answer:** Secateurs—cutting/pruning; spanner—fastening/repair; shovel—lifting/carrying loose materials; watering can—watering/irrigation.

**Marking:** 1 mark for each correct classification.

### Question 7 (4 marks)
Give four safety rules for using or storing farm tools.

**Answer:** Any four: carry sharp tools with the edge controlled and protected; pass sharp tools handle-first; keep a safe distance from other workers; wear suitable footwear and protective equipment; do not use damaged tools; store sharp tools safely; use heavy tools under supervision; do not allow students to operate an emasculator.

**Marking:** 1 mark for each valid and clearly stated rule, up to 4.

### Question 8 — Drawing-book project (10 marks)
Draw and label any five of the 30 tools. For each, write one correct use and one maintenance practice.

**Rubric:** 1 mark per clear, recognisable drawing (5 marks); 1 mark for correctly labelling major parts across the five drawings; 1 mark per correct use for two selected tools (2 marks); 1 mark per appropriate maintenance practice for two selected tools (2 marks).

**Total for Questions 1–7: 35 marks.** The practical project is assessed separately for 10 marks. A teacher may assess the full 30-tool drawing-book assignment over several lessons using the same rubric.

## Option-by-option agronomic assessment

### A. Reduce soil temperature — supported
The source says mulching “regulates” soil temperature. A peer-reviewed 2021 review of yam production in West Africa explains that yam mound mulching with dry grass/leaves increases soil moisture and reduces soil temperature. Nigerian field studies of white yam also report lower soil temperature under mulch. “Reduce soil temperature” is therefore a defensible expression of the source's temperature-regulation benefit in the yam context.
- Neina, D. (2021), “Ecological and Edaphic Drivers of Yam Production in West Africa,” *Applied and Environmental Soil Science*, Article 5019481, section 2.2, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/5019481
- Olasantan, F.O. (1999), “Effect of time of mulching on soil temperature and moisture regime and emergence, growth and yield of white yam in western Nigeria,” *Soil and Tillage Research* 50(3–4), 215–221, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-1987(98)00192-5

### B. Conserve soil moisture — supported
This is stated verbatim in source block 200. The Neina review (2021) independently records increased soil moisture in yam mounds under mulch. Nigerian yam field trials also report improved soil moisture under mulching.
- Neina (2021), section 2.2, DOI above.
- Akanbi, O.S. & Ojeniyi, S.O. (2008), “Effect of Siam Weed Mulch on Soil Properties and Performance of yam in Southwest Nigeria,” *Nigerian Journal of Soil Science* 17, 120–125, DOI: https://doi.org/10.4314/njss.v17i1.37508

### C. Prevent pests attack — conditionally supported; wording is too broad
It is not a universal effect of every mulch, but there is direct yam-specific evidence for particular materials and pests:
- IITA's *Yam Regeneration Guidelines* state that dry grass mulch placed over exposed tubers protects them from rodent attack and sun scorching (p. 4).
- Tobih's field experiment in Delta State evaluated ten plant materials as organic mulch against yam tuber beetles. *Cymbopogon citratus* and *Ocimum viride* consistently reduced beetle feeding/damage; the study reports that they acted as repellant/antifeedant botanicals, but none caused beetle mortality. The other tested mulch materials were not uniformly effective.
- Tobih, F.O., “Evaluation of Some Plant Materials as Organic Mulch for the Control of Yam Tuber Beetles (*Heteroligus* sp.) in Delta State, Nigeria,” *Agricultural Journal*, pp. 166–171, DOI: https://doi.org/10.36478/aj.2011.166.171

Thus C can be a true, qualified benefit, but “prevent pests attack” overstates it. It should identify the particular protection and the mulch material, rather than imply that ordinary grass/leaf mulch prevents all pests.

### D. Prevent yam rot — not established as a general mulching benefit
The reviewed yam-specific guidance does not identify ordinary mulching as a method for preventing fungal tuber rot:
- IITA's *Yam Regeneration Guidelines* identify post-harvest tuber rots as fungal diseases (including *Penicillium*, *Fusarium oxysporum* and *Botryodiplodia theobromae*) and describe disease-management measures separately. The mulch recommendation in the same guide is for protection of exposed tubers from rodents and sun scorching, not rot prevention.
- The IITA guide also directs growers to remove rotten/infected tubers and use appropriate disease-control measures; it does not list mulch as a rot treatment.
- As a general horticultural caution (not yam-specific proof), UC IPM notes that some mulch materials can favor root diseases, and excess or unsuitable mulch can create plant-health problems. This reinforces that mulch should not be presented as a rot-prevention measure without crop-specific evidence.
- Dumet, D. & Ogunsola, D., *Yam Regeneration Guidelines*, International Institute for Tropical Agriculture (IITA), Ibadan, Nigeria, pp. 3–4: https://www.iita.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/Yam_Regeneration_guidelines.pdf
- UC Statewide IPM Program, “Mulches Vary in Their Benefits and Drawbacks”: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/mulches-vary-in-their-benefits-and-drawbacks/

## Finding and recommended disposition
- A and B are established yam-mulching benefits.
- C can be valid for specific protective uses: dry grass over exposed tubers can protect against rodents, and selected botanical mulches can reduce yam-beetle damage. It is not a universal effect of all mulch.
- D is not an established general benefit. Yam rot is treated in IITA guidance as a fungal disease requiring separate management; mulch is not identified as its preventive treatment.
- **Likely intended exception: D.** However, the printed C is so broad that a learner could reasonably reject it as an unqualified claim. Do not assign an answer key to the unchanged wording.

### Precise correction
Retain the item and intended key D only after replacing option C with a crop- and material-specific statement, for example:

> C. protect exposed yam tubers from rodent attack when dry-grass mulch is used

The revised options then test one clear exception: **D. prevent yam rot**. If the assessment cannot be edited, withdraw this MCQ from scored assessment rather than force a key. This is an evidence-based recommendation, not a teacher/owner decision or an applied correction; the source wording and any answer key remain unchanged.

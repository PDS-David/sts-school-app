## Lesson body

Week 7 Supplement: Socio-economic Factors Affecting Land Use

## 1. Meaning of land use

Land use means the way people use an area of land. Examples include crop farming, livestock grazing, housing, markets, roads, schools, factories, forestry and wildlife conservation.

Socio-economic factors are conditions connected with people, society and the economy that influence how land is obtained, valued, shared and used. They are different from physical factors such as soil type, slope, rainfall and temperature.

Five important socio-economic factors that affect land use are explained below.

## 2. Five socio-economic factors affecting land use

### A. Population growth and urbanisation

Population growth means an increase in the number of people living in an area. As population increases, people need more food, houses, schools, hospitals, markets, roads and other services.

Urbanisation is the growth of towns and cities as more people live and work in urban areas. When a town expands, nearby farmland may be converted to residential estates, shops, roads, schools or industrial sites. At the same time, a larger population creates greater demand for food and other agricultural products.

**Example:** If a town expands towards a farming community, some farms may be converted into houses and shops. Farmers may have less land close to the town, even though the town's demand for food is increasing.

### B. Land tenure, ownership and customary practices

Land tenure refers to the rules and arrangements that determine who may occupy, control, inherit, rent or use land. In Nigeria, land may be held or used through state/public, private/family or communal arrangements, alongside statutory and customary rights.

Family customs, inheritance practices and community rules can influence who receives access to land, how land is shared and whether a farmer can continue using a plot. Secure and clear rights can encourage people to invest in improvements such as soil conservation, irrigation or farm buildings. Unclear or disputed rights can discourage long-term investment.

**Example:** A farmer who is confident that the family or community will allow continued use of a plot may be more willing to improve it. If access is uncertain, the farmer may avoid spending money on improvements that take several years to produce benefits.

### C. Income, prices, market demand and access to markets

People often choose how to use land partly by considering the income they expect to earn. Farmers may choose crops or livestock according to market demand, selling prices, production costs and the distance to buyers.

Where a crop has a reliable market and can be transported at a reasonable cost, farmers may be encouraged to use more land for it. Where input costs are high or markets are difficult to reach, some forms of farming may become less attractive. Land may instead be used for another crop, grazing, housing or a different economic activity.

**Example:** If buyers offer a reliable price for tomatoes and a road makes it easier to transport them, a farmer may decide to use part of the farm for tomato production. If transport costs become too high, the farmer may choose a crop that is easier to store or sell locally.

### D. Infrastructure and public services

Infrastructure includes facilities such as roads, bridges, electricity, water supply, drainage and communication networks. Public services include schools, health centres, markets and other facilities used by the community.

These facilities need land to be built. A new road, school, hospital, market or industrial facility may therefore change the way nearby land is used. Better roads can also make farmland easier to reach and products easier to transport. Infrastructure can support agriculture, but it can also take land away from farming when land is allocated to buildings or transport routes.

**Example:** When a road is built through a farming area, a strip of farmland may be used for the road. However, the remaining farms may become easier to reach and their produce may reach markets more quickly.

### E. Government laws, policies and land-use planning

Government decisions influence how land is allocated and what activities may take place on it. Laws and policies may provide land for agriculture, housing, roads, schools, industries, public projects or conservation. Land-use planning helps authorities and communities decide which activities are suitable for particular areas and how competing needs should be balanced.

In Nigeria, the Land Use Act of 1978 places land in each state under the Governor, to be held in trust for the people. It provides for state control and management of urban land and local-government control and management of other land, subject to the Act. The law also provides for allocation of rights of occupancy for different purposes. This means that government land decisions can influence whether land is used for farming, housing, commerce or other purposes.

**Example:** A government may allocate an area for a school, road or industrial project. The land may then change from farming to public or commercial use. A land-use plan may also protect an area for farming or conservation.

## 3. How the factors work together

Socio-economic factors often act together rather than separately.

For example, a town's population may increase. More people need houses and services. A new road may make nearby farmland more valuable. Families may decide to sell or lease some land, while government may approve new housing or commercial development. As a result, the pattern of land use changes.

The same road may also help farmers transport produce to market. Therefore, a socio-economic factor can create both opportunities and challenges for agriculture.

## 4. Socio-economic factors and physical factors

Do not confuse socio-economic factors with physical factors.

- **Socio-economic factors** arise from people, society, economic activities and institutions. Examples include population growth, land ownership, market prices, roads, public services and government policy.
- **Physical factors** arise from the natural characteristics of the land and environment. Examples include soil type, slope or topography, rainfall, temperature and water availability.

Both groups influence land use, but they are not the same.

## 5. Quick revision

1. Population growth and urbanisation increase demand for food, housing and public services and can change the amount of land available for farming.
2. Land tenure, ownership and customary practices influence who can use land and whether people feel secure enough to improve it.
3. Income, prices, market demand and market access influence which crops or other activities people choose.
4. Infrastructure and public services need land, but they can also improve access to farms and markets.
5. Government laws, policies and land-use plans influence how land is allocated and what it may be used for.

Remember: land use is influenced by both human and economic decisions and the physical characteristics of the land.

## Practice and complete marking guide

### P1 — Five socio-economic factors (10 marks)

**Question:** State five socio-economic factors that affect land use and briefly explain how each can influence land use.

**Answer:** 1. Population growth and urbanisation increase demand for housing, services and food and may convert farmland to built-up areas. 2. Land tenure, ownership and customary practices determine who can access or inherit land and whether use is secure. 3. Income, prices, market demand and market access influence which crops or activities are profitable. 4. Infrastructure and public services such as roads, schools and markets require land but can also improve access to farms and buyers. 5. Government laws, policies and land-use plans influence how land is allocated for farming, housing, commerce, industry or conservation.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Names population growth/urbanisation and explains its effect on land demand or conversion.
- 2 marks — Names land tenure/ownership/customary practices and explains its effect on access or security.
- 2 marks — Names income/prices/market demand/access and explains its effect on economic land-use choices.
- 2 marks — Names infrastructure/public services and explains how they use or improve access to land.
- 2 marks — Names government laws/policies/planning and explains how they influence allocation or permitted uses.

### P2 — Physical and socio-economic factors (4 marks)

**Question:** Give two examples of physical factors and two examples of socio-economic factors affecting land use.

**Answer:** Physical factors: soil type and slope/topography (rainfall or temperature are also acceptable). Socio-economic factors: population growth and land tenure (income/market access, infrastructure or government policy are also acceptable).

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Gives two correct physical factors, one mark each.
- 2 marks — Gives two correct socio-economic factors, one mark each.

### P3 — Apply the factors to a situation (6 marks)

**Question:** A town is expanding towards a farming community. A new road is built, more houses and shops are proposed, and buyers begin paying higher prices for vegetables. Identify three socio-economic factors in this situation and explain how each may affect land use.

**Answer:** Population growth/urbanisation increases demand for houses and services and may convert some farmland. Infrastructure development (the new road) takes land for transport but may also improve access to farms and markets. Market demand and prices may encourage farmers to use more land for vegetables because the expected income is higher.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Identifies population growth/urbanisation and explains the pressure for housing or services.
- 2 marks — Identifies infrastructure development and explains its land requirement or improved access.
- 2 marks — Identifies market demand/prices and explains how they influence crop or land-use choices.

### P4 — Land tenure and government policy (6 marks)

**Question:** Explain two ways in which land tenure and government policy can influence land use.

**Answer:** Land tenure determines who can occupy, inherit, rent or use land and how secure that access is. Secure rights may encourage long-term farm improvements, while uncertain or disputed access can discourage investment. Government laws and policies influence who allocates land and which purposes may be approved, such as agriculture, housing, roads, commerce or conservation.

**Marking:**
- 3 marks — Explains how ownership, inheritance, customary or occupancy rights determine access and security of land use.
- 3 marks — Explains how government laws, allocation decisions or land-use plans influence permitted or planned uses.

## 1. The liver, bile and digestive enzymes

The liver is an important accessory organ of digestion. It produces **bile**, a digestive secretion that helps the body digest and absorb fats. The liver does not supply a digestive enzyme called a liver enzyme to the intestinal lumen in the way the pancreas supplies pancreatic enzymes.

### What is bile?

Bile is a fluid made by the liver. In animals that have a gallbladder, the gallbladder stores and concentrates bile between meals and releases it into the duodenum when needed. In some animal species, bile flows from the liver to the intestine without being stored in a gallbladder.

Bile contains bile salts (bile acids), water and other substances. Bile salts help to **emulsify fats**. Emulsification breaks large fat globules into many smaller droplets. This increases the surface area on which the enzyme lipase can act. Bile therefore assists fat digestion, but bile itself is **not a digestive enzyme**.

Bile also helps the absorption of fats and fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K.

### Bile compared with enzymes

| Feature | Bile | Digestive enzymes |
|---|---|---|
| Main source | Made by the liver | Made by digestive glands such as the salivary glands, stomach, pancreas and small-intestinal lining |
| Is it an enzyme? | No | Yes; enzymes speed up specific chemical reactions |
| Main role | Bile salts emulsify fats and help fat absorption | Break large food molecules into smaller molecules that can be absorbed |
| Example | Bile salts help disperse fat droplets | Lipase breaks down fats; amylase acts on starch; proteases act on proteins |

**Important distinction:** the liver produces bile, while the pancreas produces enzymes such as pancreatic lipase, amylase and proteases. The liver performs many metabolic functions and contains enzymes inside its cells, but these are not the digestive enzymes referred to in the Week Four assessment.

### Simple sequence of fat digestion

1. Food containing fat leaves the stomach and enters the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine.
2. Bile enters the duodenum and bile salts help disperse large fat globules into smaller droplets.
3. Pancreatic lipase acts on the fat at the increased surface area.
4. The products of fat digestion are absorbed through the intestinal lining, with bile salts also helping the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

## 2. Similarities between monogastric and polygastric digestion

A **monogastric** animal has one main stomach compartment. Examples include pigs. A **polygastric** animal, such as cattle, has a specialised multi-compartment stomach with rumen, reticulum, omasum and abomasum.

Their stomach arrangements and use of fibrous feed differ, but both groups share important digestive processes.

### Similarities

1. **Ingestion through the mouth:** both take in feed through the mouth. Teeth and jaw movements help reduce feed particle size.
2. **Saliva and swallowing:** both produce saliva, which moistens feed and helps swallowing. Saliva also has other digestive and protective functions.
3. **Movement through the oesophagus:** swallowed feed moves through the oesophagus by muscular contractions called peristalsis.
4. **Stomach processing:** both have stomach regions that store, mix and process feed. The ruminant abomasum is the glandular or true stomach; the pig has a single main stomach.
5. **Chemical digestion:** both use digestive secretions and enzymes to break food components into smaller substances.
6. **Small-intestinal digestion and absorption:** both use pancreatic and intestinal enzymes in the small intestine, and most absorbable nutrients pass through the intestinal wall into the blood or lymph.
7. **Large-intestinal functions:** both have a large intestine that absorbs water and electrolytes and helps form and move faeces.
8. **Accessory organs:** both use the liver and pancreas in digestion. The liver supplies bile; the pancreas supplies digestive enzymes and other secretions.

### Why they are not identical

- Ruminants depend heavily on microbial fermentation in the rumen and other forestomach compartments to use fibrous plant materials.
- Monogastrics do not have a rumen. However, some monogastric herbivores, such as horses and rabbits, have important microbial fermentation in the caecum and large intestine.
- The pig's single stomach and the cow's four-compartment stomach are not interchangeable. The type of feed and the animal's digestive adaptations influence how feed is processed.

Do not write that bacteria help only polygastric animals. Microorganisms are present in the digestive tracts of many animals; their location and contribution to digestion differ among species.

### Comparison table

| Feature | Monogastric example: pig | Polygastric example: cattle | Similarity or difference? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mouth, teeth and saliva | Present | Present | Similar |
| Oesophagus | Present | Present | Similar |

## 3. Blood composition: why water is a major constituent

Blood is a fluid connective tissue that moves through blood vessels. It transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products, helps regulate body conditions and protects the animal against disease and blood loss.

Blood has two broad components:

- **Plasma:** the liquid part of blood. It contains mostly water, together with proteins, dissolved salts (electrolytes), nutrients, hormones, gases and waste products.
- **Formed elements:** the cellular components suspended in plasma. These are red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets (called thrombocytes in veterinary contexts).

### Main components and functions

| Component | Main function |
|---|---|
| Plasma | Carries dissolved substances, distributes heat, helps maintain fluid balance and transports hormones and waste products |
| Red blood cells (erythrocytes) | Carry oxygen using haemoglobin; they also help transport some carbon dioxide |
| White blood cells (leukocytes) | Defend the body against infection and foreign substances |
| Platelets or thrombocytes | Help start blood clotting and reduce blood loss after injury |

### Why water is a major constituent

Water is the largest component of plasma. In standard human physiology references, plasma is about 90–92% water; veterinary references also describe plasma as predominantly water. The exact proportions of plasma and formed elements vary with species, age, hydration, health and physiological condition, so one fixed percentage should not be applied to every farm animal.

Water in plasma acts as a solvent and transport medium. It carries dissolved nutrients, salts, hormones and waste products around the body. It also helps distribute heat and maintain blood volume.

A simple school-level description is:

**Blood = plasma + formed elements.**

Plasma contains mostly water and dissolved substances. The formed elements are red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.

Do not say that the whole of blood is 90% water. The 90–92% figure refers to the water content of plasma in commonly cited human physiology references, not to a universal percentage for whole blood in every animal.

## 4. Sphincters in the digestive tract

A **sphincter** is a ring or circular band of muscle around an opening or passage. When it contracts, it narrows or closes the passage. When it relaxes, the passage opens and material can move through it.

Sphincters help control the direction and timing of movement through the digestive tract. They can also help prevent material from flowing backwards.

### Examples of digestive sphincters

| Sphincter | Location | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Upper oesophageal sphincter | Between the pharynx and oesophagus | Opens during swallowing to allow food to enter the oesophagus |
| Lower oesophageal (cardiac/gastro-oesophageal) sphincter | Between the oesophagus and stomach | Allows swallowed food to enter the stomach and helps prevent stomach contents from moving backwards into the oesophagus |
| Pyloric sphincter | Between the stomach and duodenum | Regulates the passage of stomach contents into the small intestine |
| Ileocecal sphincter or valve | Between the ileum and caecum | Regulates movement from the small intestine into the large intestine and helps limit backflow |
| Anal sphincters | Around the anal canal | Control the release of faeces |

The sphincter of Oddi is another example. It controls the entry of bile and pancreatic secretions into the duodenum. The exact anatomical arrangement can vary among species.

### How a sphincter works: the pyloric example

1. Food is mixed with gastric juice in the stomach to form a semi-liquid material called chyme.
2. The pyloric sphincter regulates the release of small amounts of chyme from the stomach into the duodenum.
3. It helps control the rate at which stomach contents enter the small intestine.
4. Its muscle tone also helps limit backward movement of intestinal contents into the stomach.

A sphincter is therefore not a separate digestive organ that produces an enzyme. It is a muscular control structure that regulates passage through a particular opening.

## 5. Quick revision

- The liver produces bile; bile is not a digestive enzyme.
- Bile salts emulsify fats and help the absorption of fats and fat-soluble vitamins.
- Pancreatic lipase is an enzyme that digests fats.
- Monogastric and polygastric animals both ingest, move, digest and absorb feed, but their stomach structures and microbial fermentation differ.
- Blood consists of plasma and formed elements. Plasma contains mostly water and dissolved substances.
- Red blood cells transport oxygen, white blood cells defend the body, and platelets help blood clot.
- A sphincter is a circular muscle that controls the opening and closing of a passage.
- Examples include the lower oesophageal, pyloric, ileocecal and anal sphincters.

### Question 1 (6 marks)
Distinguish between bile and a digestive enzyme. State the role of bile in fat digestion and give one example of a digestive enzyme.

**Answer:** Bile is a fluid made by the liver; it is not an enzyme. Bile salts emulsify fats into small droplets, increasing the surface area for lipase to act and helping fat absorption. Lipase is an example of a digestive enzyme.

**Answer:** The two broad components are plasma and formed elements. Plasma transports dissolved substances and helps maintain fluid balance. Red blood cells carry oxygen using haemoglobin. White blood cells defend the body against infection. Platelets help blood clot and reduce blood loss.

### Question 4 (6 marks)
Explain why water is described as a major constituent of blood.

**Answer:** Blood plasma contains mostly water. Water acts as a solvent and transport medium for dissolved nutrients, salts, hormones and waste products. It also helps distribute heat and maintain blood volume. The water percentage commonly quoted for plasma must not be confused with the percentage of water in whole blood.

**Marking:**

- 2 marks — Explains transport/solvent function.
- 1 mark — Gives another function such as heat distribution or maintaining blood volume.
- 1 mark — Distinguishes plasma water percentage from whole-blood percentage or notes species variation.

### Question 5 (6 marks)
Define a sphincter and explain the functions of the lower oesophageal, pyloric and anal sphincters.

**Answer:** A sphincter is a circular band of muscle that controls an opening or passage by contracting and relaxing. The lower oesophageal sphincter controls passage from the oesophagus into the stomach and helps prevent reflux. The pyloric sphincter controls passage from the stomach into the duodenum. The anal sphincters control the release of faeces.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Correct definition of a sphincter.
- 1 mark — Function of the lower oesophageal sphincter.
- 1 mark — Function of the pyloric sphincter.
- 2 marks — Function of the anal sphincters.

### Question 6 (6 marks)
A student says, “The liver produces an enzyme called bile, and only polygastric animals use bacteria to digest food.” Identify and correct the two errors.

**Answer:** First, bile is not an enzyme. The liver produces bile, whose bile salts emulsify fats and help fat absorption; pancreatic lipase is an enzyme that digests fats. Second, microbial activity is not exclusive to polygastric animals. Ruminants depend heavily on microbial fermentation in the rumen, but some monogastric animals, including horses and rabbits, also have important microbial fermentation in the hindgut.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Corrects the bile/enzyme error and identifies bile's function.
- 2 marks — States that ruminants have major rumen microbial fermentation.
- 2 marks — Gives an accurate monogastric hindgut-fermentation example and explains the distinction.

## Lesson body

Week 4–5 Supplement: Staking and Training Yam Vines

## 1. How yam plants grow

Yam is a climbing plant. After a planted yam sett sprouts, it produces shoots that develop into vines. The vines naturally twine around supports they meet. In a yam field, a farmer may provide a support so that the vines can climb instead of spreading across the ground.

Two related practices are used:

- **Staking** means placing a suitable upright support near a yam mound for the vine to climb.
- **Vine training** means guiding the growing yam vine onto and around the support so that it climbs in the intended direction.

Staking provides the support; training helps the vine use that support.

## 2. Materials used for staking

Farmers may use locally available, strong supports such as:

- split bamboo;
- suitable branches or poles;
- existing small trees that can safely and legally serve as live supports;
- strong stalks of crops such as maize or guinea corn in an intercrop; and
- fences or other suitable structures in home gardens.

The support should be strong enough to carry the vine and leaves during the growing season. It should be placed securely so that it does not fall easily.

A stake does not always have to serve only one yam plant. Where suitable, a farmer may guide vines from more than one nearby mound to a shared stake. This can reduce the number of stakes needed, but the plants must still have enough room and light.

## 3. How to stake yam

1. **Choose the support:** Select a suitable stake or other support that is strong enough for the expected vine growth.
2. **Place it near the mound:** IITA's yam-production research describes placing stakes near yam mounds at planting or when the planted sett begins to sprout. An IITA variety-trial protocol gives another timing example: staking is normally done about one month after planting, when about 90% of the sprouts have emerged. This is a trial protocol, not a fixed date for every farm.
3. **Set it firmly:** Push the lower end into the soil so the stake is stable. Take care not to damage the planted sett or developing tuber.
4. **Allow the vine to reach the support:** As the shoot grows, guide it towards the stake.
5. **Check the support:** Inspect it during the growing period and replace or secure a stake that becomes loose or falls.

## 4. How to train yam vines

1. Observe the young yam shoot as it emerges and begins to climb.
2. Gently direct the growing vine towards the stake or support.
3. Guide the vine to twine around the support as it grows. Do not pull, twist or break the shoot.
4. Check the field regularly and guide new growth that has fallen away from the support. In IITA's variety-trial protocol, vines (especially lateral branches) are guided at least twice a week during active growth; ordinary farm frequency depends on the variety, growth rate and available labour.
5. Keep the vines reasonably spread out so that leaves can receive light and the plants do not become unnecessarily tangled.

Training is not the same as pruning. Training guides the vine onto a support; it does not mean cutting off healthy vines.

## 5. Why farmers stake and train yam vines

### A. Better exposure to sunlight

In some growing environments, supporting the vines helps arrange the leaves so that they receive more sunlight. Sunlight is needed for photosynthesis, the process by which green leaves make food for plant growth.

### B. Improved air movement around the leaves

A supported canopy can allow better air movement around leaves. In humid environments this may help reduce conditions that favour some foliar diseases. Staking does not prevent every disease, so farmers must still observe their crops and follow local extension advice.

### C. Better organisation of the crop

Stakes keep climbing vines off the ground and help organise the plant canopy. This can make it easier to inspect the crop and carry out other field activities. It can also help farmers arrange yam with suitable intercrops.

### D. Support for vigorous climbing varieties

Some yam varieties produce long, vigorous vines and benefit from strong supports. The height and type of stake should suit the variety and local growing conditions.

## 6. How mulching relates to staking and training

Mulching is a different yam cultivation practice. It means covering the soil surface around the mound with suitable material such as dry grass, leaves or crop residues. The original Second Term source teaches mulching in earlier blocks 197–203, including moisture conservation, temperature regulation, weed suppression, erosion control and the addition of humus as organic material decomposes. Mulching covers the soil; staking supports the vine; training guides the vine onto the support. Do not confuse these three practices.

## 7. Is staking necessary in every yam field?

No. Staking requirements differ with the yam species or variety, sunlight, rainfall, local farming system, availability and cost of materials, and the farmer's production objective.

- In humid forest environments, cloud cover may reduce available sunlight. Staking can help raise and spread the leaves so that they receive more light.
- In sunnier savanna environments, staking may be less elaborate. Yam vines may be guided onto the stalks of maize or guinea corn grown in the same field, and some farmers grow suitable varieties without artificial stakes.
- Some prostrate yam varieties grow along the ground and may be cultivated without staking.

Therefore, staking should be chosen according to local conditions. It is not correct to say that every yam plant must be staked or that staking always increases yield in every location.

## 8. Quick revision

- Staking is providing an upright support for a yam vine.
- Training is guiding the growing vine to climb on that support.
- Stakes may be made from split bamboo, suitable branches, existing safe supports or cereal stalks.
- Stakes may be placed at planting or when the yam sett sprouts.
- Vines are guided onto the stake as they grow.
- Staking can improve leaf exposure to light, air movement and crop organisation.
- Staking is more important in some environments than others; local conditions and variety matter.

Remember: **staking provides the support; training guides the vine.**

## Practice and complete marking guide

### P1 — Define staking and training (4 marks)

**Question:** Define staking and training of yam vines.

**Answer:** Staking is placing a suitable upright support near a yam mound for the vine to climb. Training is guiding the growing yam vine onto and around the support so that it climbs in the intended direction.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Defines staking as providing an upright support near the yam mound.
- 2 marks — Defines training as guiding the growing vine onto and around the support.

### P2 — Steps in staking and training (6 marks)

**Question:** Describe six important steps or considerations in staking and training yam vines.

**Answer:** Select a strong suitable stake such as split bamboo or a suitable branch; place it near the mound at planting or when the sett sprouts (some IITA trial protocols stake about one month after planting when most sprouts have emerged); set it firmly without damaging the sett or developing tuber; guide the young vine towards the stake; gently train the vine to twine around it as it grows; inspect regularly and guide new growth or secure a loose stake.

**Marking:**
- 1 mark — Selects a strong, suitable support such as bamboo or a suitable branch.
- 2 marks — Places the stake near the mound at planting or early sprouting, with timing adapted to the local method.
- 1 mark — Sets the stake firmly without damaging the sett or tuber.
- 1 mark — Guides the growing vine onto and around the support.
- 1 mark — Checks the support and guides new growth as needed.

### P3 — Benefits (6 marks)

**Question:** Explain three benefits of staking and training yam vines.

**Answer:** They help expose leaves to sunlight for photosynthesis; can improve air movement around foliage and reduce conditions favourable to some foliar diseases in humid environments; and organise the vines so that crop inspection and other field activities are easier. They may also help arrange suitable intercrops.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains improved exposure of leaves to sunlight and photosynthesis.
- 2 marks — Explains improved air movement or reduced conditions favouring some foliar diseases.
- 2 marks — Explains better crop organisation, easier field operations or intercrop arrangement.

### P4 — Agroecological variation (4 marks)

**Question:** Explain why staking may be more important in a humid forest environment than in a sunnier savanna environment.

**Answer:** Cloud cover in humid forest environments can reduce available sunlight, so supporting and spreading yam vines may help leaves receive more light and improve air movement. In sunnier savanna environments, sunlight is more abundant, so staking may be less elaborate or unnecessary for some varieties; vines may also use maize or guinea-corn stalks as supports.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains that cloud cover in humid forest areas can reduce sunlight and that staking helps raise/spread leaves.
- 2 marks — Explains that savanna sunlight may make elaborate staking less necessary, or identifies cereal stalks as possible supports.

### P5 — Distinguish mulching, staking and training (4 marks)

**Question:** A farmer covers the yam mound with dry grass, places a bamboo pole beside it and guides the young vine onto the pole. Name the three practices and state the role of each.

**Answer:** Covering the mound with dry grass is mulching; it covers the soil and can conserve moisture, moderate temperature and suppress weeds. Placing the bamboo pole is staking; it provides an upright support. Guiding the vine onto the pole is training; it helps the vine climb on the support.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Identifies mulching and gives a correct soil-covering function.
- 2 marks — Identifies staking as providing support and training as guiding the vine to climb.

## Findings and safe dispositions

### 1. Iron “dextian” injection in piglets (block 1064)

**Finding:** “Dextian” is not the standard generic name; the veterinary product name is **iron dextran**. Iron-deficiency anaemia is a recognised problem in suckling piglets because piglets are born with limited iron reserves and sow's milk contains little iron. Veterinary references describe iron supplementation in early life as common in intensive pig production.

The source's initial timing (within the first few days after birth) is broadly consistent with veterinary references. A repeat treatment later in the suckling period is not a universal fixed schedule: whether a second supplement is needed depends on the initial preparation and amount, piglet growth and management, and veterinary/product guidance. Kansas State University notes that the need for a second injection depends on the initial iron supplementation; some research finds improved blood iron status from a second lactation-period supplement, but this does not establish a single mandatory repeat schedule for all herds.

Excess iron can cause serious toxicosis, including weakness, respiratory distress, neurological signs and death. Risk is higher in piglets with inadequate antioxidant status, including vitamin E and/or selenium deficiency. The source's schedule must therefore not be converted into a universal treatment direction.

**Recommended wording:** “Piglets can develop iron-deficiency anaemia because they are born with small iron reserves and sow's milk contains little iron. Veterinary programmes may provide an iron supplement early in life. The product and whether a further supplement is required must be determined by a veterinarian and the product label.”

**Disposition:** Correct the generic name in any separately approved delivery correction from “dextian” to “dextran”; do not alter the source file. Retain the general educational point about early iron deficiency/supplementation. Remove the fixed repeat schedule from any learner-facing advice unless it is tied to an approved, product-specific veterinary protocol. Do not supply a dose or injection instructions.

### 2. External parasites and pesticide/insecticide (block 1080)

**Finding:** Pigs can be affected by ectoparasites such as hog lice (*Haematopinus suis*) and sarcoptic mange mites (*Sarcoptes scabiei* var. *suis*). Veterinary sources describe species-appropriate ectoparasiticides/acaricides as part of control, alongside treatment of the herd and its environment, quarantine/biosecurity and management. “Pesticide” is a broad agricultural term and is not sufficiently precise for animal treatment. “Insecticide” may apply to lice, but mange mites are arachnids; “acaricide” or “ectoparasiticide” is more accurate for mites.

Products and approvals vary by country and formulation. A compound used in cattle or another species is not automatically safe for pigs. Product labels, meat-withdrawal requirements and veterinary advice are essential.

**Recommended wording:** “External parasites of pigs include lice and mange mites. Their control requires a veterinary-approved product specifically labelled for pigs, together with appropriate herd and housing management.”

**Disposition:** Retain the general idea that approved ectoparasiticides can control external parasites, but replace generic “pesticide/insecticide” wording with “pig-approved ectoparasiticide”; identify acaricides for mites where needed. Do not name a product, concentration, application method or treatment interval.

### 3. Internal parasites and control (block 1082)

**Finding:** Internal pig parasites include gastrointestinal helminths (worms) and other parasite groups. Merck Veterinary Manual identifies anthelmintics as the drug class used against gastrointestinal helminths and stresses that parasite-control programmes depend on the production system and parasites present. Sanitation, housing/flooring, pasture management, nutrition and targeted treatment all matter. A generic external-use insecticide is not a suitable general treatment for internal worms. Some veterinary compounds may have both endo- and ectoparasite indications, but this is product- and parasite-specific; it does not justify describing all pesticides/insecticides as internal dewormers.

**Recommended wording:** “Internal worms in pigs are controlled with appropriate anthelmintic medicines and good hygiene/management. The choice of treatment depends on the parasite identified and the production system and should be guided by a veterinarian.”

**Disposition:** Distinguish ectoparasiticides from anthelmintics. The exact parasite names in block 1082 have not been independently rechecked because the week inventory does not reproduce them verbatim; do not add or silently replace a species list on the strength of this general review.

### 4. African swine fever, tick transmission and “10% caustic soda” (blocks 1085–1087)

**Disease and transmission:** African swine fever is a highly contagious viral disease of domestic and wild pigs. The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) lists transmission through direct contact with infected pigs, ingestion of contaminated material (including feed/food waste), contaminated fomites such as clothing, footwear and vehicles, and biological vectors—soft ticks of the genus *Ornithodoros*—where present. The tick statement is therefore conditionally correct, but “ticks” without qualification is misleading: not all ticks are competent ASF vectors, and tick transmission is not the only or universal route. The disease should not be described as exclusively tick-borne.

**Caustic-soda claim:** Sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) is among the chemicals shown to inactivate ASF virus under validated conditions, but a generic “10% caustic soda” instruction is not supported as a universal farm procedure by the official guidance reviewed. FAO contingency guidance lists a different sodium-hydroxide formulation and emphasizes that cleaning/disinfection belongs within an official disease-control plan. Disinfectant performance depends on the chemical, formulation, surface, organic material, temperature and validated contact conditions. A concentration copied from a textbook without these conditions is not an adequate or safe disinfection protocol.

Sodium hydroxide is highly corrosive. The US Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) states that solid sodium hydroxide and its solutions can cause severe burns to skin and eyes; dissolving it in water releases substantial heat. It is unsuitable for an unsupervised school activity or casual farm handling. This note intentionally does not give a substitute concentration, mixing sequence or application instructions.

**Two-month vacancy:** The “two months empty” statement appears to conflate distinct outbreak-control milestones. FAO's older ASF contingency manual describes a minimum destocking interval after depopulation and decontamination, and separately describes a two-month period without further outbreaks before normal trade may resume. These are not interchangeable rules. The appropriate restocking decision depends on the competent veterinary authority, the outbreak and the current national response plan; the older FAO manual is historical guidance, not a substitute for Nigeria's current official directions.

**Recommended wording:** “ASF spreads through infected pigs and contaminated feed, materials or equipment; soft *Ornithodoros* ticks can act as vectors where present. Suspected ASF must be reported to veterinary authorities. Cleaning, disinfection, destocking and restocking are carried out only under the current official veterinary response plan using approved disinfectants and trained personnel.”

**Disposition:** Retain the conditional soft-tick vector fact and broaden transmission to include infected pigs, contaminated feed/materials and fomites. Remove the unqualified “10% caustic soda” and fixed “two-month empty pen” instructions from any learner-facing treatment guidance. Do not give students a caustic-soda recipe or invite them to handle the chemical.

### 5. Swine erysipelas and penicillin (blocks 1089–1090)

**Finding:** Swine erysipelas is caused primarily by the bacterium *Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae*. Merck Veterinary Manual states that the organism is susceptible to beta-lactam antimicrobials and that penicillin is the most commonly recommended treatment; early treatment generally leads to recovery. The core association is therefore correct.

However, diagnosis should not be made from skin lesions alone. Diamond-shaped lesions can resemble lesions from African swine fever, classical swine fever and other conditions. Merck recommends diagnosis using clinical findings together with bacterial culture and/or molecular testing. Treatment is a veterinary decision; product, duration, food-animal withdrawal requirements and antimicrobial stewardship matter.

**Recommended wording:** “Swine erysipelas is a bacterial disease caused by *Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae*. Penicillin is a commonly recommended veterinary treatment, but diagnosis and treatment must be directed by a veterinarian.”

**Disposition:** Retain the disease–penicillin association with veterinary oversight and diagnostic qualification. Do not supply a dose, interval, duration or injection method.

### 6. Hypoglycaemia and glucose injection (blocks 1092–1093)

**Finding:** Hypoglycaemia means abnormally low blood glucose. It can occur in newborn piglets, particularly when they receive insufficient colostrum/milk, have low energy reserves, become chilled, or are affected by enteric infection or other illness. Glucose supplementation is a recognised part of treatment in clinically affected piglets, but injection is a clinical procedure, not a general husbandry instruction.

A 2023 veterinary study compared different glucose-administration routes in piglets and notes that hypoglycaemia commonly occurs in the first days of life when milk/colostrum intake is inadequate or other risk factors are present. The study's comparison of routes demonstrates that administration method affects glucose response; it does not justify teaching a single route or dose as universally appropriate. The underlying cause—such as inadequate milk intake, chilling or illness—also requires attention.

**Recommended wording:** “Newborn piglets may develop hypoglycaemia if they do not obtain enough energy from colostrum or milk, or if they become chilled or ill. Severe cases require prompt veterinary assessment; glucose may be used as part of treatment under veterinary direction.”

**Disposition:** Retain the general association between hypoglycaemia and glucose treatment, but remove the standalone “glucose injection” as an instruction. Do not give a route, concentration, dose or injection procedure.

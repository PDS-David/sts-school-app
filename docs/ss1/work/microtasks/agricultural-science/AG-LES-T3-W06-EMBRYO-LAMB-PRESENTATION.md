# Third Term, Week Six Supplement: Sheep Embryo Development and Normal Lamb Presentation

This supplement addresses the Week Six theory questions on embryo development and the normal position of a lamb before birth. It is for educational identification only. It is not a lambing procedure, veterinary diagnosis or guide to assisting delivery.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Describe the main stages of sheep embryo development from fertilisation through early implantation and foetal growth.
2. Explain the basic roles of the foetal membranes and placenta.
3. Distinguish fetal presentation, position and posture.
4. Describe the common normal presentation and posture of a lamb at birth.
5. Recognise that abnormal presentation or delayed progress requires an experienced livestock professional or veterinarian.

## 1. From fertilisation to an embryo

**Fertilisation** occurs when a sperm cell joins with an ovum (egg cell) to form a single cell called a **zygote**. In sheep, fertilisation normally takes place in the oviduct (fallopian tube).

The zygote develops through a sequence of stages:

1. **Zygote:** the first cell formed after fertilisation. It contains genetic material from both parents.
2. **Cleavage:** the zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis to form smaller cells called blastomeres. The total conceptus does not initially grow much in size as these early divisions occur.
3. **Morula:** the developing embryo becomes a compact ball of cells. In sheep, it enters the uterus at approximately the morula stage.
4. **Blastocyst:** a fluid-filled cavity develops. Cells begin to organise into an inner cell mass, which forms the embryo proper, and an outer cell layer called the trophectoderm, which contributes to extra-embryonic tissues.
5. **Hatching and elongation:** the blastocyst sheds the protective zona pellucida. In sheep and other ruminants, the conceptus then elongates along the uterine surface.
6. **Attachment and implantation:** the trophoblast attaches to the uterine lining (endometrium). In sheep, implantation is a superficial attachment process; it is not the deep invasion seen in some other mammals.
7. **Embryonic development:** cells differentiate into the three primary germ layers—ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm. These layers give rise to the tissues and organs of the developing animal.
8. **Foetal growth:** after the main body plan and organ systems have begun to form, the developing young is described as a foetus. The foetus grows and its organs mature during the remainder of gestation.

### Simple development sequence

**Fertilisation → zygote → cleavage → morula → blastocyst → elongation and attachment to the uterus → embryo develops tissues and organs → foetus grows until birth.**

The sequence is more important for this lesson than memorising exact day numbers. Research reports approximate days for particular sheep breeds and study conditions; these timings should not be treated as identical for every ewe or pregnancy.

## 2. Foetal membranes and the placenta

The developing lamb is supported by extra-embryonic membranes and the placenta. These structures help protect the conceptus and support exchange between the ewe and the developing lamb.

| Structure | Main role |
|---|---|
| Amnion | Forms a membrane around the embryo/foetus and encloses amniotic fluid, which cushions and protects the developing young. |
| Chorion | The outer foetal membrane; contributes to the foetal part of the placenta and participates in exchange with maternal tissues. |
| Allantois | Helps form the chorioallantoic placenta and is involved in the handling of foetal waste during development. |
| Yolk sac | Supports very early development and contributes to early blood-cell and germ-cell development; it is not the main nutrient store for a sheep embryo as it is in a bird egg. |
| Placenta | Provides the interface for transfer of oxygen and nutrients from the ewe to the foetus and movement of carbon dioxide and waste products away from the foetus. |

In sheep, the placenta is **cotyledonary**. Specialised areas on the foetal membranes (cotyledons) connect with corresponding maternal structures in the uterus (caruncles). Together, these form placentomes, which are important sites of exchange.

The placenta is not simply a storage bag. It is an active exchange organ that supports the developing foetus while the foetus remains inside the uterus.

## 3. What happens during foetal development?

After the embryo has established contact with the uterus:

- cells continue to divide and specialise;
- the nervous system, skeleton, muscles and internal organs develop;
- the heart and blood vessels support circulation;
- the limbs, head and body become recognisable;
- the foetus grows as nutrients and oxygen are supplied through the placenta;
- organs continue to mature in preparation for life outside the uterus.

The developing lamb is enclosed by foetal membranes and surrounded by fluid. The uterus, placenta and membranes provide protection and support, but the foetus still depends on the ewe for oxygen and nutrients until birth.

## 4. Understanding lamb presentation, position and posture

These three words describe different aspects of how a foetus is arranged in the uterus and birth canal. They should not be used as if they mean the same thing.

### A. Presentation

**Presentation** describes how the long axis of the foetus lies in relation to the long axis of the ewe's birth canal.

- **Anterior (cranial) longitudinal presentation:** the lamb's head and forequarters are directed towards the birth canal. This is the common normal presentation in lambing.
- **Posterior (caudal) longitudinal presentation:** the lamb's hindquarters are directed towards the birth canal.
- **Transverse presentation:** the lamb lies across the uterus/birth canal rather than lengthwise.

### B. Position

**Position** describes how the lamb's back (dorsum) is oriented in relation to the ewe's pelvis.

- **Dorsosacral position:** the lamb's back is directed towards the ewe's sacrum (the upper, back part of the pelvic passage). This is the usual position in a normal anterior presentation.
- Other positions may be described by the direction of the lamb's back towards the ewe's side or pelvic bones. These require veterinary interpretation.

### C. Posture

**Posture** describes the arrangement of the lamb's head, neck and limbs in relation to its own body.

- **Normal anterior posture:** the head and neck are extended, and both forelimbs are extended forwards alongside the head.
- A flexed or retained limb, a head turned back, or limbs arranged abnormally is an abnormal posture, even if the lamb is still in anterior presentation.

## 5. Common normal presentation of a lamb

The most common normal presentation at lambing is:

- **Presentation:** anterior longitudinal — the head and forequarters are directed towards the birth canal.
- **Position:** dorsosacral — the lamb's back is oriented towards the ewe's sacrum.
- **Posture:** the head and neck are extended, with both forelimbs extended forwards and the nose positioned between or just ahead of the forelimbs.

This is often described as an **anterior dorsosacral presentation with normal posture**. Most lambs are delivered in this arrangement.

A posterior longitudinal presentation with the hindlimbs extended can also occur, but it is not the common normal presentation used for the basic classroom description. A transverse presentation, a head turned back or a retained limb is not the normal anterior posture.

### Remember the three terms

| Term | Question it answers | Normal lamb example |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation | Which end of the lamb is directed towards the birth canal? | Head/forequarters first (anterior) |
| Position | Which way is the lamb's back facing relative to the ewe's pelvis? | Back towards the ewe's sacrum (dorsosacral) |
| Posture | How are the head, neck and limbs arranged? | Head/neck and both forelimbs extended |

## 6. Recognising when professional help is needed

An abnormal presentation, position or posture can obstruct birth and endanger both ewe and lamb. Difficult birth is called **dystocia**.

Examples of abnormal findings include a head turned back, one or both forelimbs retained, hindquarters or tail appearing without the expected normal posture, or more than one lamb entering the birth canal together.

These are recognition terms only. Students must not attempt an internal examination, reposition a lamb, pull a lamb, use obstetrical instruments or administer medicines. If a real ewe appears to be having a difficult birth, the safe response is to alert the teacher, farm supervisor, experienced trained stockperson or veterinarian immediately. The professional decides whether and how assistance is required.

## 7. Quick revision

- Fertilisation forms a zygote.
- Cleavage produces blastomeres; a compact morula develops, followed by a blastocyst.
- The sheep blastocyst hatches and elongates before attaching to the uterine lining.
- Embryonic tissues differentiate; the embryo develops organs and becomes a growing foetus.
- Foetal membranes and placenta protect and support the developing lamb and enable exchange with the ewe.
- Presentation describes the long axis of the foetus relative to the birth canal.
- Position describes the orientation of the foetal back relative to the maternal pelvis.
- Posture describes the arrangement of the foetal head, neck and limbs.
- The common normal lamb presentation is anterior longitudinal, dorsosacral, with the head/neck and both forelimbs extended.
- Abnormal presentation or delayed progress is a veterinary/livestock-professional matter, not a student practical exercise.

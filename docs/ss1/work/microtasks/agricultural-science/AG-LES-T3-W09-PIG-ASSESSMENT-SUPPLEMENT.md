# Third Term, Week Nine Supplement: Pig Housing Terms and Intensive-Rearing Limitations

This short supplement supplies two missing pieces needed by the Week Nine assessments: the term for a pig enclosure and a second supported disadvantage of intensive pig rearing. It is additional teaching and does not replace the original pig-management lesson.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this supplement, you should be able to:

1. Explain the terms pigsty, sty and pig pen.
2. State two disadvantages of intensive pig rearing and explain each.
3. Explain why disease prevention and control require particular attention in intensive pig units.
4. Use the correct livestock term fatteners.

## 1. What is a sty or pigsty?

A **sty** or **pigsty** is an enclosure or place where pigs are kept. A **pig pen** is an individual enclosed space or section within a pig house or pig unit. The terms are related and may overlap in ordinary use.

The Week Nine source explains that pigs are housed in pens. The weekend assessment uses the word sty. Both words can be correct:
- **Pigsty:** a place or enclosure for keeping pigs.
- **Pig pen:** a particular enclosed area where one pig or a group of pigs is housed.

A pig unit may contain several pens for different groups, such as pregnant sows, farrowing sows and piglets, weaners or fatteners. The design should provide suitable space, drainage, ventilation, clean water, feeding facilities and safe flooring.

## 2. Intensive pig rearing

In an **intensive system**, pigs are kept in a managed housing unit and the farmer supplies most of their feed, water and daily care. The system can allow close observation, controlled feeding and efficient use of land, but it requires substantial resources and careful management.

### Two disadvantages of intensive pig rearing

**1. High initial capital investment**

An intensive pig unit may require substantial spending on buildings, pens, floors, drainage, water supply, feeding equipment, ventilation, waste handling and other facilities. The farmer must provide these facilities before the pigs can be managed in the system.

**2. Greater disease-control demands under confinement**

When many pigs are housed close together, an infectious disease can spread through direct contact, contaminated surfaces, equipment, feed/water areas or other routes. Poor ventilation, overcrowding and weak hygiene can increase the risk. Intensive systems therefore require careful disease prevention, observation, sanitation, isolation of sick animals and veterinary guidance.

This does **not** mean every intensive pig farm will have more disease than every extensive farm. The level of risk depends on stocking density, housing design, biosecurity, animal health, ventilation and the quality of daily management. The supported disadvantage is the greater importance and cost of effective disease prevention and control under confinement.

### Summary table

| System | General description | Possible advantages | Possible disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extensive | Pigs are kept with more space and may forage for some of their feed | Lower investment in buildings; pigs can express more natural foraging behaviour | Requires more land; feeding and observation may be less controlled |
| Semi-intensive | Combines housing with some access to an outdoor area | Combines some control with access to space | Needs both suitable housing and a managed outdoor area |
| Intensive | Pigs are housed and managed in a controlled unit; the farmer supplies feed and water | Controlled feeding, close observation and efficient use of land | High initial capital investment; greater disease-control demands if biosecurity and management are inadequate |

## 3. Vocabulary: fatteners

A **fattener** is a pig being reared during the finishing stage to gain weight for meat production. The plural is **fatteners**.

The Week Nine source uses the correct spelling fatteners later in the feeding and management sections, but an earlier term is printed as “Fatheners”. The standard livestock spelling is fatteners. The original source wording remains unchanged; this supplement uses the correct spelling for learners.

## 4. Disease prevention in an intensive pig unit

Disease prevention is important in every pig-rearing system. In intensive housing, the close proximity of animals makes good management especially important.

General principles include:
- keep pens, floors, troughs and water systems clean;
- provide suitable ventilation and avoid overcrowding;
- observe pigs daily and seek veterinary advice when animals appear unwell;
- separate sick animals under veterinary or farm-supervisor direction;
- control the movement of people, animals, equipment and vehicles into the pig unit;
- follow veterinary vaccination, parasite-control and testing programmes;
- dispose of manure, bedding and dead animals in accordance with veterinary and environmental rules.

This is general husbandry education, not a disease-treatment guide. The original source's pesticide, disinfectant, injection and drug statements are not validated instructions and are not repeated here. Chemical use, disease diagnosis and treatment must be directed by an appropriately qualified professional.

### Question 1 (4 marks)
What is a sty or pigsty? Explain how a pig pen is related to it.

**Answer:** A sty or pigsty is an enclosure or place where pigs are kept. A pig pen is an individual enclosed space or section within a pig house or pig unit.

### Question 2 (4 marks)
State two disadvantages of intensive pig rearing.

**Answer:** High initial capital investment in buildings and equipment; greater disease-control demands under confinement, especially if overcrowding, poor ventilation or weak biosecurity occur.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — High initial capital investment, with a relevant explanation.
- 2 marks — Greater disease-control demands under confinement, with a relevant explanation.

### Question 3 (6 marks)
Explain why disease prevention and control are important in an intensive pig unit.

**Answer:** Pigs may be housed close together, so infectious disease can spread through contact, contaminated surfaces, equipment or feed/water areas. Poor ventilation and overcrowding may increase risk. Good hygiene, ventilation, observation, isolation of sick animals and veterinary guidance help reduce spread.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains how close housing can increase contact and transmission opportunities.
- 2 marks — Identifies relevant risk factors such as poor ventilation, overcrowding or contaminated equipment.
- 2 marks — Gives suitable prevention measures such as hygiene, observation, isolation and veterinary guidance.

### Question 4 (6 marks)
Compare intensive and extensive pig-rearing systems. Give one advantage and one disadvantage of each.

**Answer:** Intensive rearing keeps pigs in a managed housing unit with supplied feed and water; an advantage is controlled feeding and close observation, while disadvantages include high initial investment and greater disease-control demands. Extensive rearing allows pigs more space and may allow foraging; an advantage is lower investment in buildings, while disadvantages include greater land requirements and less control over feeding and observation.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Correctly describes the two systems.
- 2 marks — Gives one valid advantage of each system.
- 2 marks — Gives one valid disadvantage of each system.

### Question 5 (4 marks)
Define the term fattener in pig production and state its main purpose.

**Answer:** A fattener is a pig being reared during the finishing stage to gain weight for meat production. Its main purpose is to reach a suitable market weight for pork production.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Correct definition of fattener.
- 2 marks — States its meat-production/finishing purpose.

### Question 6 (6 marks)
A farmer has built an intensive pig unit but does not clean the pens regularly and keeps too many pigs in a small space. State two disease-control risks and two improvements the farmer should make.

**Answer:** Overcrowding increases close contact and opportunities for disease transmission. Dirty pens and contaminated troughs/equipment can expose pigs to infectious agents. The farmer should reduce stocking density to an appropriate level and improve cleaning, ventilation and hygiene. Sick animals should be observed and referred to a veterinarian.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Identifies two relevant risks, one mark each.
- 2 marks — Recommends two relevant improvements, one mark each.
- 2 marks — Explains why the improvements reduce disease risk.

## 1. Cattle tattooing (blocks 1203 and 1253)

**Finding:** Tattooing is a recognised permanent method of cattle identification. It consists of marking letters and/or numbers in the animal's skin—most commonly inside the ear—using tattoo ink so that the mark remains as a permanent identification record after healing. It is used for registration, ownership/transfer identification and herd-performance/parentage records. Mississippi State University Extension describes ear tattooing as a reliable permanent identification method and notes that tattoos can be difficult to read from a distance, so they may be paired with another identification method such as an ear tag.

**Recommended school definition:** “Tattooing is a permanent method of identifying cattle by marking letters or numbers, usually inside the ear, with tattoo ink. The mark remains after the skin heals and helps with registration and record keeping.”

**Assessment decision:** The theory question asking learners to define tattooing is valid, but the Week Ten teaching body only names tattooing as an identification method and does not define it. A short assessment supplement is justified to teach the definition before it is assessed. No step-by-step application procedure is needed in the supplement.

## 2. “Rational grazing” versus “rotational grazing” (block 1188; theory 1253)

**Finding:** “Rational grazing” is a recognised livestock/pasture-management term; it is not a spelling error that should automatically be changed to “rotational grazing.” The Federal University of Agriculture, Abeokuta (FUNAAB), in its *Beef Animal Production* lecture notes, explicitly distinguishes the two:
- **Rotational grazing** involves moving livestock between paddocks/pastures and allowing pasture to recover between grazing periods.
- **Rational grazing** is described as an elaboration of rotational grazing associated with André Voisin. Its rest periods and paddock use are adjusted according to pasture growth and season; some paddocks may be rested or conserved for hay/silage while others are grazed.

University of Maine Extension describes rotational grazing as moving animals among paddocks and allowing previously grazed plants to recover. University of Georgia/Clemson Extension lists “rational grazing” among terms used within management-intensive grazing. Terminology varies across sources and regions, but the FUNAAB definition is especially relevant to a Nigerian cattle-production curriculum and demonstrates that the source's “rational” wording is legitimate.

**Assessment decision:** Do not silently replace “rational” with “rotational.” They are related, and rational grazing can be taught as a planned form/elaboration of rotational grazing, but the terms are not always used identically. The assessment's request to define rotational grazing is not directly matched by the lesson's one-word mention of rational grazing. A short supplement should define both and state their relationship.

**Recommended school wording:**
- “Rotational grazing is a system in which livestock are moved from one paddock or pasture to another so that grazed pasture can rest and regrow before animals return.”
- “Rational grazing is a planned form of rotational grazing in which the rest period and paddock use are adjusted to pasture growth and the season.”

**Disposition:** Keep “rational grazing” in the source transcription. Add both definitions to the dependent assessment supplement so the theory question is explicitly taught and keyed. Do not mark the source word as a typo.

## 3. “Pig houses” in the cattle mange section (block 1210)

**Finding:** The reference to maintaining hygiene and sanitation in “pig houses” appears in the Week Ten cattle-mange section. This is a cross-topic copy error: the subject of the surrounding text is cattle mange, not pig housing. The relevant setting is cattle housing, sheds or pens. Merck Veterinary Manual explains that cattle mange mites can spread by direct contact and contaminated fomites; some mites can survive away from the host and be transmitted through contaminated environments/housing. Hygiene and sanitation of cattle housing are therefore relevant to cattle mange control.

**Recommended delivery wording:** Replace “pig houses” with “cattle housing” (or “cattle sheds and pens”) in the cattle-mange passage. “Cattle housing” is the most neutral term and avoids implying that every cattle operation uses a house or shed.

**Disposition:** A delivery-only correction is justified. Do not change the original source bytes or its source transcription.

## Sources consulted

1. **Mississippi State University Extension Service**, “Tattooing Cattle,” Publication P3880. Describes ear tattooing as permanent cattle identification used for registration, ownership and performance records, and notes its limitation of being hard to read from a distance. https://cto.extension.msstate.edu/publications/tattooing-cattle
2. **Federal University of Agriculture, Abeokuta (FUNAAB)**, APH509 *Beef Animal Production*, lecture notes, section “Grazing Methods, Uses and Problems.” Explicitly describes rotational grazing and then defines rational grazing as an elaboration of rotational grazing associated with André Voisin, with rest periods adjusted according to season and pasture growth. https://funaab.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Beef%20Animal%20Production.pdf
3. **University of Maine Cooperative Extension**, “Rotational Grazing.” Defines rotational grazing as moving animals among paddocks and allowing grazed pasture to recover; explains the importance of grazing duration and recovery periods. https://extension.umaine.edu/livestock/pasture-course/lesson-6/rotational-grazing/
4. **University of Georgia Cooperative Extension / Clemson University**, Dennis Hancock and John Andrae, “What is Management-intensive Grazing (MiG) and what can it do for my farm?” Describes rotational grazing and lists rational grazing among related terms used in management-intensive grazing. https://sustainagga.caes.uga.edu/content/dam/caes-subsite/sustainable-agriculture/documents/ManagementIntensiveGrazing.pdf
5. **Merck Veterinary Manual**, “Mange in Cattle,” full review March 2023, last updated September 2024. Describes cattle mange mites, direct-contact transmission, and transmission via contaminated environments/fomites/housing for certain mites. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/integumentary-system/mange/mange-in-cattle

|---|---|---|

It is absent or negligible in several livestock species, including ruminants, dogs, cats and horses; some omnivores (e.g. pigs) have more activity. | “Where present, salivary amylase begins starch digestion by hydrolysing starch into shorter carbohydrates. | Rennin / “Renin” (405) | The source uses two spellings. **Rennin** is the older name for **chymosin**, a milk-clotting enzyme important in the stomach of very young suckling animals; secretion is age- and species-dependent. **Renin** is a different enzyme of the renin–angiotensin system, not the milk-digesting enzyme. | Use “chymosin (formerly called rennin), present in young suckling animals of species that secrete it”; do not generalize it to every non-ruminant. |

Trypsin is the active protease; it cleaves proteins/polypeptides into smaller peptides and also activates other pancreatic zymogens. |
| Bile (411) | The statement that bile emulsifies fats is correct. ### Specific trypsinogen correction
Block 410 currently says: “Trypsinogen – Converts protein and peptones to polypeptides”. This reverses the enzyme/precursor relationship and the direction of protein breakdown. A correct school-level statement is:

> **Trypsinogen** is an inactive enzyme precursor (zymogen) produced by the pancreas. In the small intestine it is activated by enteropeptidase to form **trypsin**, which breaks proteins and polypeptides into smaller peptides.

### Bile and the “liver enzyme” issue
- Bile is produced by the liver; in species with a gallbladder it is stored/concentrated there, while some species (e.g. horses) lack a gallbladder and bile flows continuously into the intestine.
- Bile is a digestive secretion, not an enzyme. Its bile salts emulsify fat; pancreatic lipase performs the chemical hydrolysis.
- Thus an assessment asking for a “liver enzyme” should not accept bile as an enzyme. The source's bile description at block 411 is broadly right when it says bile helps emulsify fat, but it must be distinguished from enzymes.

### Adjacent terminology error at block 411
The source says the liquid food “called chyle” passes from the duodenum to the ileum. At this stage the correct term is **chyme** (partially digested food mixed with gastric and intestinal secretions). **Chyle** is the milky, lipid-rich lymph formed after absorbed fats enter intestinal lacteals. Replace “chyle” with “chyme” in a future delivery correction.

## Monogastric/polygastric comparison table — block 431

| Table claim | Assessment | Accurate, species-qualified description |
|---|---|---|
| Monogastrics cannot ruminate; polygastrics can | Generally correct for the intended comparison of simple-stomached animals with true ruminants such as cattle, sheep and goats. | True ruminants regurgitate and rechew cud; simple-stomached animals do not ruminate. |
| Monogastrics mainly eat “basal and concentrated food”; polygastrics mainly eat grasses/cellulose | Overgeneralized. Diet depends on species and production system. Ruminants often use forage but may receive concentrates; monogastric herbivores such as horses and rabbits rely heavily on forage. | Describe typical diets by named species/production system, not as a universal stomach-type rule. |
| Monogastrics have one compartment; polygastrics have four | Broadly correct for simple-stomached animals versus common domestic ruminants, but “four stomachs” would be misleading. | A monogastric has a simple stomach; a typical ruminant has **one compound stomach with four compartments**: rumen, reticulum, omasum and abomasum. |
| Monogastrics cannot digest cellulose/fibre properly; polygastrics digest it very well | Too broad. Ruminants digest fibre extensively through foregut fermentation. But monogastric hindgut fermenters (horses and rabbits) also use microbes to ferment fibre in the caecum/colon, with species-specific efficiency. | “Ruminants ferment fibre in the foregut; some monogastrics (e.g. horses and rabbits) ferment fibre in the hindgut. Other simple-stomached species have more limited fibre fermentation.” |
| Monogastric digestion is not aided by bacteria; polygastric digestion is aided by bacteria | **Incorrect.** Gut microbes aid digestion in monogastrics too, especially hindgut fermenters. The major difference is the site and scale of fermentation. | “Ruminants rely heavily on rumen microbes for foregut fermentation; some monogastrics rely on hindgut microbes for fermentation, but microbial protein recovery differs by species.” |
| Monogastrics cannot synthesize their own protein; polygastrics can | **Incorrect as written.** All animals synthesize body proteins from amino acids. Rumen microbes synthesize microbial protein, which ruminants digest after it passes to the abomasum/intestine. Hindgut fermenters also have microbial protein production; rabbits recover some via cecotrophy, while horses generally do not recover it efficiently. | “Rumen microbes synthesize microbial protein that ruminants can digest and absorb. Hindgut fermenters also produce microbial protein, but its availability to the host depends on species and behaviours such as cecotrophy.” |

### Comparative physiology evidence
- FAO's *Basic Ruminant Nutrition* describes the dense rumen microbial population, bacterial fermentation of plant cell-wall carbohydrates, microbial protein synthesis, and subsequent digestion of microbes in the intestine. https://www.fao.org/4/T0423E/T0423E03.htm
- University of Minnesota College of Veterinary Medicine, Erin Malone DVM PhD, *Vet Med: Applied GI Physiology — Supplemental Notes*, section “Fermentation”: ruminants/camelids are foregut fermenters; horses are hindgut fermenters; rabbits are caecal fermenters and recover microbial nutrients through coprophagy/cecotrophy. https://open.lib.umn.edu/vetphysioapplied/open/download?type=pdf
- Merck Veterinary Manual, “Nutrition of Rabbits” (reviewed by a University of Georgia College of Veterinary Medicine specialist; updated July 2026): rabbits are nonruminant hindgut fermenters; caecal microbes ferment feed and cecotrophy returns microbial protein and vitamins. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/exotic-and-laboratory-animals/rabbits/nutrition-of-rabbits

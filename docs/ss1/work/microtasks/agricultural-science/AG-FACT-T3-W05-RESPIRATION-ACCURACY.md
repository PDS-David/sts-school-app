## 1. Aerobic respiration — blocks 532–538
The source's aerobic equation is visually concatenated and has no visible reaction arrow. Its labels identify glucose, oxygen, carbon dioxide, water and ATP. The balanced overall equation is:

> `C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (captured in ATP)`

The equation is balanced: one glucose reacts with six oxygen molecules to yield six carbon dioxide and six water molecules. The ATP yield should not be fixed to an outdated single number in a general school definition; the overall yield depends on cell type and accounting convention.

Evidence: Khan Academy's cellular-respiration overview gives the balanced glucose/oxygen/carbon dioxide/water equation; Monash University explains that aerobic respiration converts glucose and oxygen into ATP, carbon dioxide and water.
- Khan Academy, “An introduction to cellular respiration”: https://www.khanacademy.org/science/biology/cellular-respiration-and-fermentation/intro-to-cellular-respiration/a/cellular-respiration-overview

## 2. Anaerobic respiration — blocks 539–541

### Source equation and classification
Block 540 gives glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide (with coefficients shown for ethanol and carbon dioxide), and block 541 labels the products as alcohol, carbon dioxide and ATP. This is **alcoholic fermentation**, characteristic of yeast and some microorganisms. It is not the anaerobic pathway used by animal skeletal muscle.

### Correct animal versus yeast distinction
- **Animal muscle when oxygen supply is insufficient:** lactic acid/lactate fermentation. A school-level net equation is:

  > `C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + 2 ATP`

  (glucose → 2 lactate/lactic acid + net 2 ATP)

- **Yeast alcoholic fermentation:** ethanol and carbon dioxide are produced:

  > `C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + 2 ATP`

The source's equation is therefore chemically the yeast/alcoholic pathway, but it is mislabelled if presented as the general anaerobic respiration equation for animals. The lesson should either label it explicitly “alcoholic fermentation in yeast” and add the animal lactate equation, or replace it with the lactate equation if the topic is animal respiration.

Evidence: Monash University explicitly distinguishes lactic acid fermentation in animal muscles from alcoholic fermentation in yeast (sections “Anaerobic respiration in animals” and “Anaerobic respiration in yeasts”). OpenStax Biology 2e likewise identifies lactic acid fermentation as the pathway used by animals and some bacteria, while yeast fermentation produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
- Monash University, “Anaerobic respiration”: https://www.monash.edu/student-academic-success/biology/cellular-respiration/anaerobic-respiration
- OpenStax Biology 2e, “7.5 Metabolism without Oxygen”: https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-5-metabolism-without-oxygen

## 3. Bronchioles and epiglottis — block 546
The source says bronchioles are the passageways by which air passes “through the nose or mouth to the alveoli”. This skips the major airway branches and incorrectly makes bronchioles sound like the whole route.

Correct mammalian airway sequence:
> Nares/nose or mouth → nasal/oral cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → primary bronchi → smaller bronchi → bronchioles → alveolar ducts/sacs → alveoli.

Bronchioles are the fine branches coming off the bronchi; gas exchange occurs in the alveoli. The epiglottis acts as a flap/trap door at the top of the larynx, helping keep food out of the larynx and trachea during swallowing. The source's general protective role is correct, but the more precise anatomical description is “covers the laryngeal inlet during swallowing”.

Evidence:
- Merck Veterinary Manual, “The Respiratory System in Animals” (reviewed by Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine, April 2026): the mammalian respiratory tract proceeds from nares through pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi and bronchioles to alveoli; gas exchange occurs in alveoli. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/respiratory-system/respiratory-system-introduction/the-respiratory-system-in-animals
- Merck Veterinary Manual, “Terms Used to Describe the Respiratory System”: bronchioli are fine branches from bronchi; epiglottis is the trap door at the top of the larynx that keeps food out of larynx/trachea during swallowing. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/multimedia/table/terms-used-to-describe-the-respiratory-system

## 4. Gill rakers, filaments, arch and operculum — block 552
- **Gill arch:** supporting structure for the gill filaments and rakers.
- **Gill filaments:** bear thin lamellae with dense capillary networks; the lamellae are the main gas-exchange surfaces.
- **Gill rakers:** comb-like projections on the gill arches. They filter food particles from the water, help retain food in the mouth and can protect delicate gill filaments from particles/debris. Their number and spacing vary with feeding ecology.
- **Operculum:** bony gill cover in most bony fish; it protects the gill openings and participates in the buccal/opercular pumping that moves water over the gills. Sharks and rays do not have a typical operculum; they have exposed gill slits.

The source is broadly correct that rakers prevent particles from reaching the gills and that the operculum covers the gill chamber. It should be more precise that rakers filter particles/food and the operculum is the protective cover and part of water ventilation. The operculum does not perform the same filtering role as a raker.

Evidence:
- Western Australia Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development, “Fact Sheet: Fish Anatomy”, reviewed 26 August 2021: gill arches, rakers and filaments; bony fish have an operculum; cartilaginous fish have 5–7 gill slits. https://marinewaters.fish.wa.gov.au/resource/fish-anatomy/
- University of Hawai‘i at Mānoa, *Exploring Our Fluid Earth*, “Structure and Function – Fish”, section “Gills and Oxygen Exchange”: gill arches support filaments, rakers filter food from water before it reaches the gills, and the operculum covers/protects gills and participates in buccal pumping. https://www.manoa.hawaii.edu/exploringourfluidearth/biological/fish/structure-and-function-fish

## 5. Epiglottis/gill MCQ — block 597
Question: “Which of following performs same function in gills as epiglottis? A. chamber B. filament C. operculum D. Raker”

**Defensible answer: D. Raker.**
- The epiglottis helps keep food out of the airway.
- Gill rakers filter food/particles from water and keep food particles from proceeding to the gill surfaces.
- The operculum covers and protects the gills and assists water flow; it is not the filtering structure.

This is an analogy, not an exact anatomical equivalence. Retain D as the intended answer, but phrase the explanation as “the closest functional analogue is the gill raker” rather than claiming the structures are identical.

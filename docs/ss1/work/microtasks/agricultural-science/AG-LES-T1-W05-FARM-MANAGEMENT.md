## Lesson body

Week 5 Assessment Supplement: Improved Farm-Management Systems

A farm-management system is an organised way of using land, crops, livestock, labour and other farm resources. The systems below are examples of ways farmers can plan production and manage soil, pasture and farm resources. Their suitability depends on local soil, climate, water, labour and market conditions.

## Five Improved Farm-Management Systems

1. **Crop rotation:** Growing different crops on the same field in a planned sequence over successive seasons. A planned sequence can help maintain soil health, manage nutrients and interrupt some pest and disease cycles.
2. **Mixed farming:** Keeping crop and livestock enterprises on the same farm. Crop residues may be used as animal feed, while animal manure can be returned to crop fields; this can help reuse farm resources and spread risk.
3. **Rotational grazing:** Dividing pasture into paddocks and moving livestock between them so grazed areas can rest and regrow before animals return.
4. **Intercropping:** Growing two or more crops together in the same field during the same period. Crops may be arranged in rows or other patterns to use space and resources and reduce some production risks.
5. **Agroforestry:** Deliberately growing trees or shrubs together with crops and/or livestock on the same land-management unit. Trees can provide products such as fruit, fodder or timber and may help protect soil and water.
6. **Conservation agriculture:** Managing land around three linked principles: minimum soil disturbance, keeping the soil covered with plants or residues, and diversifying crops through rotation or association.

## Bush Fallowing: How It Differs

Bush fallowing is a traditional fallow or shifting-cultivation system. A plot is cultivated for a relatively short period and then left uncultivated for a longer period while natural vegetation regrows and the soil recovers. The farmer may cultivate another plot during the fallow period.

Bush fallowing is not the same as crop rotation:
- Crop rotation plans a sequence of different crops on the same field over successive seasons.
- Bush fallowing alternates a period of cultivation with a period when the plot is left fallow for vegetation to regrow.

Bush fallowing is also different from rotational grazing. Rotational grazing moves livestock between pasture paddocks and allows grazed areas to rest; bush fallowing rests a cultivated land plot through a fallow period. Bush fallowing is a land-use system, not simply another name for crop rotation.

## Important Distinctions

- Mixed farming combines crop and livestock enterprises; intercropping combines two or more crops in the same field.
- Rotational grazing manages livestock and pasture; crop rotation manages the sequence of crops on arable land.
- The systems are not one-size-fits-all. Farmers choose practices that suit local conditions and available resources.

**Question:** Define crop rotation, mixed farming and rotational grazing.

### P2 — Six farm-management systems (12 marks)

**Question:** Describe six improved farm-management systems and give one main feature of each.

**Answer:** 1. Crop rotation — Different crops are grown on the same field in a planned sequence over successive seasons. 2. Mixed farming — Crop and livestock enterprises are kept on the same farm; residues and manure can be reused between them. 3. Rotational grazing — Livestock are moved between pasture paddocks so grazed areas can rest and regrow. 4. Intercropping — Two or more crops are grown together in the same field during the same period. 5. Agroforestry — Trees or shrubs are deliberately integrated with crops and/or livestock. 6. Conservation agriculture — The system combines minimum soil disturbance, permanent soil cover and crop diversification.

### P3 — Bush fallowing and crop rotation (4 marks)

**Question:** State two differences between bush fallowing and crop rotation.

**Answer:** Crop rotation keeps the field in a planned sequence of different crops over successive seasons; bush fallowing cultivates a plot for a relatively short period and then leaves it uncultivated for a longer fallow period. Crop rotation does not require a long period of natural vegetation regrowth; bush fallowing relies on vegetation regrowth during the fallow period to help restore the land.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains that crop rotation is a planned sequence of crops on the same field over successive seasons, unlike the crop-then-fallow cycle of bush fallowing.
- 2 marks — Explains that bush fallowing leaves the plot uncultivated for a fallow period with natural vegetation regrowth, whereas crop rotation does not require this long fallow.

### P4 — Identify the system (5 marks)

**Question:** Identify the farm-management system shown in each example: (a) maize is grown one season and cowpea the next on the same field; (b) crop residues feed livestock and manure is returned to the field; (c) cattle are moved between fenced paddocks; (d) maize and cowpea grow together in the same field; (e) trees are grown among crops.

**Answer:** (a) Crop rotation. (b) Mixed farming. (c) Rotational grazing. (d) Intercropping. (e) Agroforestry.

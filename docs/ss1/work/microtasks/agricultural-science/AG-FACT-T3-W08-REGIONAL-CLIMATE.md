## Findings

### 1. Rainfall, tsetse flies and trypanosomiasis (blocks 867 and 936)

**Finding:** The association is broadly valid but should not be simplified to “rainfall causes tsetse flies.” Tsetse distribution is governed by species-specific ecology and suitable habitat, including vegetation/land cover, humidity, temperature, water and host availability. Some tsetse groups inhabit tropical rain forest; others prefer gallery/riverine forest, swamps, woodland or savannah. In West Africa, FAO describes a north–south climatic gradient: in the humid south, favourable rainfall, humidity, temperature and water support a wider range of tsetse habitats; in transitional areas, flies may spread along river systems during the rainy season. Thus rainfall acts mainly through habitat and microclimate, and its effects differ by species and region.

Tsetse flies transmit African animal trypanosomosis (nagana), a major livestock constraint in Nigeria. The Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development's 2022 national strategy reports 11 tsetse species in Nigeria and describes trypanosomosis as a major obstacle to livestock production. The report identifies risk across Nigeria's agro-ecological zones, so tsetse is not confined to the equatorial rainforest.

**Recommended wording:** “Rainfall can favour tsetse flies indirectly by supporting humid vegetation and suitable habitats. Tsetse distribution also depends on temperature, humidity, vegetation, water and the fly species. Tsetse flies transmit trypanosomosis, which reduces livestock health and productivity.”

**Disposition:** Retain the rainfall–habitat–tsetse association, but describe it as indirect and ecology-dependent. Avoid implying that rain alone creates tsetse flies or that all tsetse species occupy only rainforest.

### 2. Wind and tuberculosis (block 879)

**Finding:** Tuberculosis is transmitted mainly by inhalation of infectious droplets/aerosols expelled from the respiratory tract of an infected animal. Merck Veterinary Manual identifies inhalation of infected droplets from the lungs as the usual route of TB infection in animals; ingestion, including through contaminated milk, water or feed, is another possible route.

Wind or air movement can influence the movement, dilution and persistence of airborne particles, but “wind spreads tuberculosis” is too broad if it implies that wind itself is the cause or that outdoor wind invariably increases transmission. In animal housing, ventilation and air currents affect the concentration and movement of infectious aerosols; close exposure to infected animals and shared air are the relevant mechanisms.

**Recommended wording:** “Tuberculosis can spread when animals inhale infectious droplets or aerosols expelled by an infected animal. Air movement and ventilation can influence how airborne particles move and disperse, but wind itself is not the cause of tuberculosis.”

**Disposition:** Replace the unqualified wind claim with the established airborne-transmission mechanism. Do not describe wind as a pathogen or as a sufficient cause of infection.

### 3. Milk spoilage “above 37 °C” (block 950)

**Finding:** A fixed 37 °C spoilage threshold is not supported. Milk quality deteriorates through bacterial growth and enzyme activity; the rate depends on time, initial microbial contamination, hygiene and temperature. Milk can spoil at temperatures well below 37 °C if held long enough or contaminated. The FAO *Small-Scale Dairy Farming Manual* identifies 15–40 °C as an unfavourable range in which bacteria multiply quickly and enzyme activity is high; it recommends keeping milk cool, around 4 °C. FAO's current milk-preservation guidance explains that natural inhibitors in freshly drawn milk can delay significant bacterial increases for a limited period at ambient temperature, but prompt cooling to 4 °C is the preferred way to preserve quality.

Therefore, 37 °C is within a warm range that encourages rapid microbial growth, but it is not the temperature at which milk suddenly begins to spoil. The source's phrasing may confuse the approximate body temperature of a cow with a safe/unsafe storage boundary.

**Recommended wording:** “Warm conditions speed up bacterial growth and milk spoilage. Milk should be handled hygienically and cooled promptly; about 4 °C is the recommended cold-storage temperature. Spoilage depends on both temperature and the length of time milk is kept warm, not on a single 37 °C threshold.”

**Disposition:** Remove the “above 37 °C” threshold. Retain the general point that high temperatures accelerate spoilage; explain time–temperature–hygiene dependence.

### 4. Climate effects on milk and egg production (blocks 950–953)

**Dairy cattle:** Heat stress reduces feed intake and milk yield and can impair growth and reproductive performance. High humidity makes heat dissipation more difficult; solar radiation and wind/air movement also affect the animal's heat balance. FAO's livestock-climate review describes reduced feed intake, lower milk production and reduced growth in heat-stressed dairy cattle, with breed differences. A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that heat stress reduced dry-matter intake, energy-corrected milk and milk-protein concentration, with effects related to the temperature–humidity index (THI). Do not present one temperature threshold as universal for all breeds or production systems.

**Laying hens:** Heat stress can reduce feed intake, hen-day egg production, egg weight and eggshell weight/thickness. A Nigerian-authored review in *World's Poultry Science Journal* identifies high environmental temperature as a major non-genetic constraint on egg production and shell quality. An Ontario government technical factsheet likewise summarizes reductions in egg production, egg size and shell quality under heat stress. Effects depend on intensity and duration of heat, humidity, bird age, genotype and housing.

**Recommended wording:** “High temperature, especially when combined with high humidity, can cause heat stress in farm animals. In dairy cattle this can reduce feed intake, milk yield and reproductive performance. In laying hens it can reduce feed intake, egg production, egg weight and shell quality. The severity depends on the animal's breed, age, housing, humidity and duration of heat exposure.”

**Disposition:** Retain the general relationship between climate and animal production, but distinguish dairy-cow and laying-hen responses. Avoid one universal temperature cutoff or the claim that every animal/breed responds identically.

### 5. “The greatest climate factor affecting animal production in Nigeria” (MCQ 971)

**Finding:** The source's preceding climate section identifies multiple factors—rainfall, temperature, wind, relative humidity and light—but does not rank them nationally. The evidence reviewed does not establish one universally greatest factor for all Nigerian animal species, regions and production systems.

Different factors dominate different pathways:
- **Rainfall** strongly influences pasture/forage growth, drinking-water availability, seasonal grazing and drought risk. A Nigerian study of rainfall variability and livestock output examines this relationship, but it does not establish rainfall as the single most important climate variable for every livestock outcome.
- **Temperature together with relative humidity** is a major direct physiological constraint in heat-sensitive livestock. A Nigerian study of cattle in southern Nigeria found that ambient temperatures frequently exceeded the cattle comfort range and that humidity was often too high; it assessed cattle performance rather than ranking all national climate factors.
- **Solar radiation, wind and humidity** alter heat exchange and may amplify or alleviate heat stress depending on conditions.
- **Seasonal rainfall and vegetation** also affect vector habitats, disease exposure and feed availability.

**Recommended wording for the lesson/assessment:** “There is no single climate factor that is greatest for every animal-production system in Nigeria. Rainfall is especially important for pasture and water supply, while temperature combined with relative humidity is a major direct cause of heat stress and reduced productivity. The most important factor depends on the species, region and production system.”

If the assessment must have one answer, the question should specify its intended outcome (for example, “the main climatic determinant of pasture and water availability” or “the main direct cause of heat stress in dairy cattle”). Do not mark a nationwide one-word answer as scientifically settled from the current source.

**Disposition:** Mark the existing single-answer question as under-specified. Recommend a revised, outcome-specific question rather than silently assigning “rainfall” or “temperature” as the universal answer.

### 6. Equatorial-rainforest cattle-production rationale (MCQ 974)

**Finding:** Tsetse-transmitted trypanosomosis is a defensible major historical and ecological constraint on conventional cattle production in humid forest/woodland zones. FAO describes humid forest and riverine habitats as suitable for certain tsetse groups, and notes that tsetse infestation can explain low permanent village-cattle densities in otherwise suitable moist subhumid areas. FAO's West African farming-zone account describes the humid forest zone as tsetse-infested and reports relatively few livestock, including mainly trypanotolerant cattle, sheep and goats in the wider humid zone. Nigeria's 2022 national strategy documents tsetse/trypanosomosis as a major national livestock constraint.

The explanation must not be made absolute. Tsetse species occur in several Nigerian agro-ecological zones; the national strategy reports tsetse across all zones. Cattle are not biologically impossible in the rainforest: trypanotolerant breeds, appropriate management, disease surveillance and vector-control measures can support production. The constraint is disease risk and the associated cost/productivity burden, not a simple rule that heavy rainfall makes cattle unable to live there.

**Recommended wording:** “Cattle production has traditionally been limited in parts of the humid equatorial rainforest because suitable humid vegetation habitats support tsetse flies, which transmit trypanosomosis. The disease reduces cattle health and productivity. Cattle keeping is not impossible, but it requires suitable breeds and effective management and disease-control measures.”

**Disposition:** If the source answer is “tsetse flies/trypanosomosis,” retain it as a major historical constraint but qualify it. Reject “high rainfall alone makes cattle production impossible” or “there is no cattle production in the rainforest.”

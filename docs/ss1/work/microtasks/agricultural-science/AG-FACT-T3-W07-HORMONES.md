## Findings by hormone

### 1. Testosterone / androgen

**Source claim:** Male; testes; development of male secondary sexual characteristics and sperm production through spermatogenesis.

**Finding:** Substantially correct, with a necessary qualification. Testosterone is the principal testicular androgen. In domestic animals, testicular interstitial (Leydig) cells produce testosterone under LH stimulation. Androgens are required for development and function of male reproductive organs, accessory glands, male secondary sex characteristics and reproductive behaviour. Spermatogenesis is not attributable to testosterone alone: FSH stimulates the process, while androgens—especially testosterone—support/augment it and are required for normal completion.

**Recommended wording:** “Testosterone (the principal androgen) is produced mainly by Leydig cells in the testes under LH stimulation. It supports male reproductive-organ development and function, secondary sexual characteristics and normal spermatogenesis; FSH also has a direct role in supporting sperm production.”

**Disposition:** Retain the male/testes association and core functions; qualify the sperm-production statement so it does not imply testosterone acts alone. “Androgen” is a hormone class, not an exact synonym for testosterone; use “testosterone (an androgen)” as the row label.

### 2. Oestrogen

**Source claim:** Female; ovary; female secondary sexual characteristics, production of ova/eggs through oogenesis, and mammary-gland development.

**Finding:** The ovary is an important source, particularly ovarian follicles, but it is not an exclusive source in every species or reproductive state; the foetoplacental unit and adrenal cortex can also contribute. Oestrogen supports female reproductive-tract development and function, endometrial growth, oviduct secretory activity, sexual receptivity, mammary duct development and secondary sexual characteristics. The claim that oestrogen produces ova through oogenesis is inaccurate as a direct hormone function. Oogenesis is the development of female gametes in the ovary; it should not be presented as a product or direct action of oestrogen. FSH supports ovarian follicular development and ovarian oestrogen synthesis.

**Recommended wording:** “Oestrogens are produced mainly by ovarian follicles (with additional foetoplacental/adrenal sources in some contexts). They promote female reproductive-tract and endometrial development, oviduct secretions, sexual receptivity, mammary-duct development and female secondary sexual characteristics. Oogenesis occurs in the ovary; it is not accurate to say that oestrogen itself produces ova.”

**Disposition:** Retain the ovarian association as the principal school-level source, but qualify it as non-exclusive. Replace the oogenesis function with a correctly stated reproductive-tract/follicular-cycle function.

### 3. Progesterone

**Source claim:** Female; corpus luteum; uterus development and implantation of the fertilized ovum, inhibition of oestrus/ovulation, mammary-gland development and continuance of pregnancy.

**Finding:** The corpus luteum is the major source in many domestic mammals, including cattle and pigs throughout pregnancy; placental contribution or placental takeover occurs in some other species. The source is therefore broadly correct but should not imply that the corpus luteum is the only source in every animal. Progesterone prepares and maintains the endometrium and uterine environment for implantation and pregnancy, reduces uterine contractility during pregnancy, and supports mammary lobulo-alveolar development. It participates in feedback regulation of gonadotropins and usually suppresses oestrous behaviour/ovulation during the luteal phase and pregnancy. “Uterus development” is imprecise; the relevant function is endometrial preparation/maintenance, not general development of the whole uterus.

**Recommended wording:** “Progesterone is produced mainly by the corpus luteum in many domestic species; placental production also contributes or takes over in some species. It prepares and maintains the uterine lining and environment for implantation and pregnancy, reduces uterine contractions during pregnancy, regulates gonadotropin activity, and supports mammary lobulo-alveolar development.”

**Disposition:** Retain corpus luteum as the principal source with a species qualification. Replace “uterus development” with “prepares and maintains the endometrium/uterine environment”; retain pregnancy maintenance and mammary development with more precise terminology.

### 4. Oxytocin

**Source claim:** Female; pituitary; uterine-wall contraction during parturition, milk let-down, and assistance with sperm transportation in the vagina.

**Finding:** The site needs anatomical precision. Oxytocin is synthesized by hypothalamic neurons (notably the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei), then transported to and stored/released from the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis). “Pituitary” is an acceptable shorthand for the release site only if it is specified as the posterior pituitary; it is not the synthesis site. Oxytocin is not exclusively a female hormone, although uterine contractions at parturition and milk ejection are especially relevant female reproductive functions. Its established domestic-animal actions include stimulation of uterine smooth-muscle contractions during labour/parturition and contraction of mammary myoepithelial cells to cause milk ejection (let-down).

The claim about sperm transportation in the vagina is not established as a general physiological function and should not be taught as an unqualified fact. Research has investigated oxytocin and sperm movement, but the evidence is limited and context-dependent. A review of mammalian sperm migration describes the role of oxytocin in sperm migration as little explored. In an experimental ewe study, exogenous oxytocin did not alter sperm movement through the uterus and into the oviducts. Older studies report pharmacological effects on sperm transport in particular experimental settings; these do not establish normal oxytocin-mediated sperm transport as a standard function.

**Recommended wording:** “Oxytocin is synthesized in the hypothalamus and stored/released by the posterior pituitary. In females it stimulates uterine contractions during parturition and causes milk ejection (let-down). Do not list sperm transportation in the vagina as an established general function.”

**Disposition:** Clarify hypothalamic synthesis/posterior-pituitary release; retain uterine contraction and milk ejection; remove the sperm-transport claim from any future corrected teaching version unless a specific, appropriately qualified species-specific evidence base is supplied.

### 5. Relaxin

**Source claim:** Female; pituitary; relaxation of pelvic ligaments during parturition.

**Finding:** The stated function is broadly consistent with the recognised role of relaxin in preparing the birth canal, particularly its softening/relaxing effect on pelvic ligaments in late pregnancy. The listed secretion site is incorrect as a general statement. Relaxin is not appropriately described as a pituitary hormone. Its main source is species-dependent: the corpus luteum is the principal pregnancy source in the sow; the placenta is the principal source in late equine pregnancy and in several other species. Other reproductive tissues may express relaxin locally. Evidence about the source in cattle and sheep is less uniform, so a single universal source should not be asserted.

**Recommended wording:** “Relaxin is produced by reproductive tissues, with the principal source varying among species (for example, the corpus luteum in the pregnant sow and the placenta in the late-pregnant mare). It helps prepare the birth canal for delivery, including softening/relaxing pelvic ligaments.”

**Disposition:** Correct the source from “pituitary” to a species-qualified reproductive source. Retain the pelvic-ligament function, phrased as preparation for parturition rather than a universal action confined to the moment of birth.

### 6. Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)

**Source claim:** Female; pituitary; growth of the ovarian follicle.

**Finding:** The source site is correct but incomplete in scope. FSH is secreted by the anterior pituitary in both sexes. In females it stimulates ovarian follicular development and supports oestrogen synthesis. In males it acts on Sertoli cells/seminiferous tubules and supports spermatogenesis. Listing it as female-only omits a fundamental male reproductive role.

**Recommended wording:** “FSH is secreted by the anterior pituitary in both sexes. In females it stimulates ovarian follicle development and oestrogen synthesis; in males it acts on Sertoli cells and supports spermatogenesis.”

**Disposition:** Change the sex association from “Female” to “Both sexes” in any future corrected table; retain pituitary with “anterior” specified; add the male function.

### 7. Luteinizing hormone (LH)

**Source claim:** Female; pituitary; secretion of oestrogen and progesterone, follicle rupture and release of ova.

**Finding:** The source site is correct but incomplete in scope. LH is secreted by the anterior pituitary in both sexes. In females it triggers ovulation in spontaneously ovulating species and supports luteinization/corpus-luteum development and subsequent progesterone production. Its interaction with ovarian follicular cells contributes to steroidogenesis; the source's broad “secretion of oestrogen and progesterone” wording is too imprecise to stand alone. In males LH acts on Leydig cells in the testes to stimulate testosterone production. Listing LH as female-only omits this major male role.

**Recommended wording:** “LH is secreted by the anterior pituitary in both sexes. In females it triggers ovulation and supports luteinization/corpus-luteum development; in males it stimulates Leydig cells in the testes to produce testosterone.”

**Disposition:** Change the sex association from “Female” to “Both sexes”; specify anterior pituitary; retain the female ovulatory role but refine the steroid-secretion wording; add Leydig-cell/testosterone production in males.

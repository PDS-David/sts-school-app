# Third Term, Week Ten Supplement: Cattle Tattooing and Grazing Terms

This supplement supplies the verified definitions needed for the Week Ten theory assessment. It explains cattle tattooing and distinguishes rotational grazing, rational grazing and zero-grazing. It does not provide animal-handling or tattoo-application procedures.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Define cattle tattooing and explain its use in animal identification and record keeping.
2. Define zero-grazing, rotational grazing and rational grazing.
3. Explain the relationship between rational grazing and rotational grazing.
4. Select an appropriate grazing or feeding system for a simple cattle-farm situation.

## 1. Cattle tattooing

**Tattooing** is a permanent method of identifying cattle by marking letters or numbers in the skin, commonly inside the ear, using tattoo ink. The mark remains after the skin heals and can help identify an animal for registration, ownership records, breeding records and herd management.

Tattooing is an identification method, not a disease treatment or a form of decoration.

### Why cattle may be tattooed

- to provide a permanent identification mark;
- to support herd records and animal registration;
- to help match an animal with its breeding, health and production records;
- to support ownership and traceability.

### Limitation of tattooing

A tattoo can be difficult to read from a distance or when the ear is dirty or darkly pigmented. Farmers may use another identification method, such as an ear tag, alongside a tattoo. The appropriate identification method depends on the animal, the farm's record system and applicable rules.

This lesson defines tattooing only. It does not explain how to restrain cattle, prepare equipment, apply a tattoo or handle an animal during the procedure. Those activities require trained personnel and appropriate animal-welfare practices.

## 2. Zero-grazing

**Zero-grazing** is a system in which cattle are kept in a stall, shed or yard and are not allowed to graze directly on pasture. Instead, the farmer cuts forage such as grasses or legumes and carries it to the animals. This is also called a cut-and-carry feeding system.

### Features

- cattle remain in a managed housing area;
- forage is harvested and carried to the animals;
- the farmer controls the quantity and type of feed offered;
- manure can be collected for use or disposal;
- the system requires labour to cut, transport and provide forage and water.

### Possible advantages and limitations

Zero-grazing can be useful where land is limited or where animals must be kept away from crops. It can also allow closer control of feeding. However, it requires regular labour, reliable forage supply, suitable housing, water and manure management. If forage is poorly selected or insufficient, animal nutrition can suffer.

## 3. Rotational grazing

**Rotational grazing** is a pasture-management system in which livestock are moved between two or more paddocks. Animals graze one paddock for a planned period, then move to another while the grazed paddock is rested so plants can recover.

### Main idea

**Graze one paddock → move the animals → allow the first paddock to rest and regrow → return when pasture has recovered sufficiently.**

The length of grazing and resting periods depends on pasture species, growth rate, rainfall, season, soil conditions and stocking rate. A fixed number of days is not suitable for every farm.

### Possible benefits

- allows grazed plants time to recover;
- helps the farmer manage how much pasture is eaten;
- can improve the distribution of manure;
- may reduce selective overgrazing when properly planned;
- can help maintain ground cover and reduce soil exposure.

Rotational grazing needs fencing or another way to control animal movement, reliable water access in the paddocks and regular observation of pasture condition.

## 4. Rational grazing

In the Nigerian agricultural teaching reference used for this supplement, **rational grazing** is described as a planned elaboration of rotational grazing. The farmer plans the use and rest of paddocks according to pasture growth and the season.

For example, a farmer may:
- allow a grazed paddock to rest until the pasture has recovered;
- adjust the length of the rest period when pasture growth slows;
- use some paddocks for grazing while resting or conserving others for hay or silage;
- plan grazing to avoid repeatedly grazing the same plants before they recover.

The term is used somewhat differently in different teaching materials and regions. For this SS1 lesson, follow the Nigerian reference's distinction: rotational grazing is the general practice of moving animals between paddocks for rest and regrowth; rational grazing is a more planned form in which the use and rest periods are adjusted to pasture growth and season.

**Important:** “Rational grazing” is a valid term in the cited Nigerian teaching material. It should not automatically be treated as a spelling error for “rotational grazing.”

## 5. Compare the three systems

| System | Where the cattle are kept | How they obtain forage | Main management requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-grazing | In a stall, shed or yard | Farmer cuts and carries forage to them | Labour, forage supply, housing, water and manure management |
| Rotational grazing | In a series of paddocks | Cattle graze a selected paddock, then move to another | Paddock control, water access and pasture recovery |
| Rational grazing | In a planned grazing arrangement, commonly using paddocks | Grazing and resting are adjusted to pasture growth and season | Monitoring pasture growth and planning grazing/rest periods |

### Worked examples

**Example A — A small dairy farm has little land but can obtain fresh forage daily.** Zero-grazing may be suitable because cattle can be housed while forage is cut and carried to them. The farmer must have enough labour and a reliable supply of good-quality forage.

**Example B — A cattle farm has several fenced paddocks.** Rotational grazing can be used by moving cattle from one paddock to another and allowing previously grazed pasture to recover.

**Example C — A cattle farmer notices that grass grows slowly during the dry season.** A rational grazing plan adjusts rest periods and paddock use to the slower pasture growth. Some pasture may be rested or conserved as hay or silage rather than grazed immediately.

## 7. Quick revision

- Tattooing is a permanent animal-identification method using letters or numbers marked in the skin, commonly inside the ear.
- Zero-grazing means cattle are housed and forage is cut and carried to them.
- Rotational grazing means cattle are moved between paddocks so grazed pasture can rest and regrow.
- Rational grazing is described in the cited Nigerian teaching reference as a planned elaboration of rotational grazing, with grazing and rest periods adjusted to pasture growth and season.
- Zero-grazing, rotational grazing and rational grazing are different management arrangements; they should not be used as interchangeable terms.

### Question 6 (8 marks)
A farmer has three fenced paddocks. The grass in the first paddock has been grazed very short and has not recovered. Explain how rotational or rational grazing could help the farmer manage the pasture.

**Answer:** The farmer should move the cattle to another suitable paddock rather than returning them immediately to the overgrazed paddock. The first paddock should be rested to allow the grass to regrow. The farmer should monitor pasture growth and decide when the paddock is ready for grazing again. A rational grazing plan adjusts rest and grazing periods according to pasture growth and the season and may conserve some paddocks for hay or silage.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains moving cattle to another paddock.
- 2 marks — Explains resting the grazed paddock for recovery.
- 2 marks — Explains monitoring pasture regrowth before returning cattle.
- 2 marks — Explains adjustment of the plan to pasture growth/season or conservation of forage.

### Question 7 (6 marks)
A farmer has little grazing land but can cut enough forage daily. Which system may be suitable? Give two advantages and one limitation.

**Answer:** Zero-grazing may be suitable. Advantages include keeping cattle away from growing crops and controlling the quantity/type of forage supplied. A limitation is the labour and reliable forage supply needed to cut and carry feed every day.

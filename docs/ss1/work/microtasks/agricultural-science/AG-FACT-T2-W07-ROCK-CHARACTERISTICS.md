## Igneous rocks — blocks 443–448

| Source claim | Assessment | More accurate wording |
|---|---|---|
| “They have glassy appearance” (443) | **Only some.** Intrusive igneous rocks are usually coarse-grained and crystalline; extrusive rocks are often fine-grained, and some are glassy (e.g. obsidian). | “Some rapidly cooled extrusive igneous rocks have a glassy texture; others are fine-grained or crystalline, while many intrusive rocks are coarse-grained and crystalline.” |
| “They contain crystals … crystalline in nature” (444) | **Generally, but not universally.** Igneous rocks form by cooling/crystallization, but obsidian is volcanic glass and may have no visible crystals. | “Most igneous rocks contain mineral crystals; rapidly quenched volcanic glass such as obsidian may contain few or no crystals.” |
| “They do not occur in layers” (445) | **Incorrect as an absolute.** Stacked lava flows and layered igneous intrusions are documented. They are not normally sedimentary beds, but igneous bodies can be layered. | “Igneous rocks do not usually form sedimentary bedding, but lava flows can stack in layers and some intrusions show igneous layering.” |
| “They do not contain fossils” (446) | **Broadly correct as a general distinction.** Molten rock and high temperatures do not normally preserve organism remains within the igneous rock. Fossils are typically preserved in sedimentary rocks. | “Fossils are not normally preserved within igneous rocks.” |
| “They are hard and impervious” (447) | **Overgeneralized.** Many igneous rocks are hard, but vesicles, cooling joints, fractures and contacts can make volcanic rock permeable. | “Many igneous rocks are hard; their porosity and permeability vary with texture, vesicles and fractures.” |
| “They are resistant to erosion” (448) | **A tendency, not a universal property.** Granite can be highly resistant, but durability depends on mineral composition, weathering, joints and environment. | “Many massive crystalline igneous rocks are relatively resistant to weathering and erosion, but resistance varies.” |

Evidence: USGS explains that intrusive rocks cool slowly and develop coarse grains, while extrusive rocks can be fine-grained or glassy; gas bubbles may form vesicular textures. USGS documents stacked basalt lava flows and igneous layering in layered intrusions. USGS engineering geology also records basalt flows with vesicles, fractures and variable permeability.

## Sedimentary rocks — blocks 461–465

| Source claim | Assessment | More accurate wording |
|---|---|---|
| “They occur in layers or strata” (461) | **Often true, not universal.** Bedding is characteristic of many sedimentary deposits, but not every sedimentary rock displays obvious layers. | “Sedimentary rocks commonly occur in layers or strata (bedding).” |
| “They are not in crystal form … not crystalline” (462) | **Incorrect.** Clastic rocks are made of grains and cement, but chemical sedimentary rocks form by precipitation/crystallization; rock salt, gypsum and some limestone are crystalline. | “Many clastic sedimentary rocks are made of cemented grains; some chemical sedimentary rocks have crystalline textures.” |
| “They contain fossils of plants and animals” (463) | **Often true, not universal.** Fossils are typically found in sedimentary rocks, but many sedimentary rocks contain no fossils. | “Some sedimentary rocks contain plant or animal fossils; fossil preservation depends on depositional and burial conditions.” |
| “They are not resistant to erosion” (464) | **Incorrect as a universal rule.** Poorly cemented mudstone/shale can erode readily, while well-cemented sandstone and some limestone can be resistant. | “Resistance to weathering and erosion varies with composition, cementation, porosity, bedding, fractures and climate.” |
| “They may be coarse, fine, soft or hard” (465) | **Broadly correct.** Sedimentary grain size ranges from clay to boulders, and hardness depends in part on cementation and composition. | “Sedimentary rocks vary from fine- to coarse-grained and from weakly to strongly cemented.” |

Evidence: USGS says sedimentary rocks often show bedding and may range from clay-sized grains to boulders. The National Park Service explains that cementation and crystallization can solidify sedimentary material and identifies chemical sedimentary rocks. An introductory geology text documents crystalline limestone, rock salt and gypsum. The British Geological Survey shows that some sandstone and limestone resist erosion while other rocks are softer and more easily worn.

- National Park Service, “Sedimentary Rocks”: https://home.nps.gov/subjects/geology/sedimentary.htm
- *A Practical Guide to Introductory Geology*, section 5.4, “Chemical Sedimentary Rocks”: https://pressbooks.openeducationalberta.ca/practicalgeology/chapter/5-4-chemical-sedimentary-rocks/
- British Geological Survey, “Relief”: https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/geological-processes/relief/

## Metamorphic rocks — blocks 481–485

| Source claim | Assessment | More accurate wording |
|---|---|---|
| “Some may occur in layer or strata” (481) | **Partly correct, but imprecise.** Some metamorphic rocks are foliated or banded due to mineral alignment; this is not the same process as sedimentary bedding. Some low-grade rocks may retain relict bedding. | “Some metamorphic rocks show foliation or mineral banding; others are non-foliated.” |
| “The rock may be hard or soft” (482) | **Broadly correct.** Hardness and strength vary with mineral composition, grain size, foliation and metamorphic grade. | “Metamorphic rocks vary in hardness and strength according to their minerals and texture.” |
| “They are not in form of crystals” (483) | **Incorrect.** Recrystallization and the growth/rearrangement of minerals are central to metamorphism; schist and gneiss can show visible mineral crystals, and marble is recrystallized calcite. | “Metamorphic rocks commonly contain recrystallized or newly formed mineral grains; crystal size and visibility vary.” |
| “They exist in different colours and texture” (484) | **Broadly correct.** Mineral composition and metamorphic texture produce varied colours and textures. | “They vary in colour, mineral composition and texture.” |
| “They may contain fossils” (485) | **Possible but uncommon.** Fossils are typically found in sedimentary rocks and only occasionally survive in fine-grained, low-grade metamorphic rocks; greater heat/pressure commonly destroys or distorts them. | “Rarely, fossils may survive in fine-grained, low-grade metamorphic rocks; they are generally uncommon in metamorphic rocks.” |

Evidence: USGS describes metamorphism as creating new minerals and identifies foliated/banded and non-foliated textures. The British Geological Survey notes that fossils are typically found in sedimentary rocks and occasionally in some fine-grained, low-grade metamorphic rocks.
- USGS, “What are metamorphic rocks?” https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-are-metamorphic-rocks?qt-news_science_products=0
- British Geological Survey, “Fossils”: https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/fossils-and-geological-time/fossils/
- British Geological Survey, “Relief”: https://www.bgs.ac.uk/discovering-geology/geological-processes/relief/

## Overall findings and delivery recommendation
1. **Clear errors:** sedimentary rocks “are not crystalline” (462) and metamorphic rocks “are not in form of crystals” (483). Both should be corrected in learner-facing explanations.
2. **Overgeneralizations:** glassy appearance, crystal presence, no layers, hardness/impermeability and erosion resistance for igneous rocks; universal layering, fossils and low erosion resistance for sedimentary rocks; “layers/strata” and fossil occurrence for metamorphic rocks. Replace absolutes with “often”, “some”, “commonly”, “may” and conditions where appropriate.
3. **Keep valid tendencies:** fossils are typical of sedimentary rocks; many sedimentary rocks show bedding; igneous rocks commonly form by crystallization; metamorphic rocks may be foliated/banded and vary in texture/colour.
4. **Recommended correction approach:** retain the source's original text unchanged in source records, but use the precise qualified statements above in a future teacher-approved delivery correction. Do not create a blanket three-row checklist that implies every rock type has one fixed hardness, permeability or erosion resistance.

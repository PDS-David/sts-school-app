## Findings

### 1. Caustic soda and horn-bud treatment (block 1203)

**Finding:** Caustic alkali paste is a recognised method of **disbudding** very young calves in some production systems, but the source's generic wording “caustic soda” is not a safe, complete instruction. Disbudding means destroying/removing horn-producing tissue before it attaches to the skull; dehorning refers to removal of horns after attachment. These are not interchangeable terms.

Cornell's FARM v4.0 guidance recognises caustic paste as one disbudding method for young calves and states that it is time-limited to the early neonatal period. Ontario's government guidance describes caustic dehorning paste as a corrosive chemical and emphasizes pain management and avoiding injury to the calf and unintended exposure. The product is a formulated paste, not an invitation to handle raw caustic soda. Caustic agents can cause chemical burns and can damage eyes or other skin if spread.

**Recommended wording:** “Disbudding is the removal or destruction of horn-producing tissue in young calves before the horn attaches to the skull. A formulated caustic paste is one method used in some systems, but it is a veterinary/animal-welfare procedure for trained personnel and requires appropriate pain management. It must not be taught as the casual application of caustic soda.”

**Disposition:** Correct the concept to *disbudding* when the horn bud has not attached to the skull. Do not reproduce the source as a step-by-step procedure, and do not ask learners to handle caustic soda or paste.

### 2. Cattle mange, ivermectin and “Ivomec” (block 1210)

**Finding:** Mange in cattle is caused by mites, and the relevant mite species must be identified. Merck Veterinary Manual lists several cattle mange mites and describes ivermectin as an option for particular forms of mange when the product/formulation is labelled for the relevant animal and parasite. Treatment choice varies by mite species, production status, formulation, jurisdiction and product approval.

“Invermectin” is a misspelling of **ivermectin**. **Ivomec** is a brand name for ivermectin, not a second, different medicine. Thus “invermectin or ivomec” is redundant and confuses a generic active ingredient with a brand. Official product information identifies Ivomec as ivermectin. This does not mean every Ivomec formulation is approved for every cattle class, every mange mite or every country. Meat/milk withdrawal restrictions and label indications differ by formulation and jurisdiction.

Mange diagnosis should not be made solely from appearance; skin scraping and veterinary assessment may be needed. The source's separate reference to hygiene in “pig houses” is cross-topic wording in a cattle section and is not validated by this safety review.

**Recommended wording:** “Cattle mange is caused by mites. A veterinarian should identify the mite and select a locally approved, cattle-labelled treatment. Ivermectin is one active ingredient used against certain cattle mange mites; Ivomec is a brand of ivermectin, not a separate drug.”

**Disposition:** Correct “invermectin” to “ivermectin” in a separately approved delivery correction and clarify that Ivomec is a brand. Do not give a dose, route, formulation, treatment interval or withdrawal period. Do not imply that ivermectin treats every mange species or is approved for every dairy/beef animal.

### 3. “Diazuntol,” “malathione,” benzyl benzoate and benzene hexachloride (block 1212)

**Finding:** The source's chemical list is not safe to present as a current, interchangeable list of cattle-mange treatments.

- **“Diazuntol”:** This name could not be verified as a generic veterinary active ingredient in the authoritative sources reviewed. It may be a local trade name or a spelling/transcription error, but the intended active ingredient cannot be assumed to be diazinon without a verified product label and registration.
- **“Malathione”:** The standard chemical name is **malathion**. A spelling correction is justified, but that does not establish that a particular malathion product is currently approved or appropriate for cattle mange in Nigeria.
- **Benzyl benzoate:** This is a real chemical used in some topical scabicide/ectoparasite formulations, but the source supplies no formulation, target mite, product registration or food-animal withdrawal information. Its presence in an old list does not establish current cattle approval.
- **Benzene hexachloride (BHC):** This is an older, ambiguous name for hexachlorocyclohexane (HCH) isomers; lindane is the gamma-HCH isomer. The Stockholm Convention lists alpha-HCH and beta-HCH in Annex A for elimination and lists lindane in Annex A with only a narrow specific exemption for a human-health pharmaceutical use. This is not a sound basis for recommending BHC/lindane as a routine cattle ectoparasiticide.

Modern veterinary treatment must be based on a confirmed parasite, an authorised product for the species and production class, current local registration, label instructions and withdrawal requirements. A chemical's historic insecticidal use does not make it a safe or lawful current treatment for food-producing cattle.

**Recommended wording:** “Mange treatment must use a currently authorised veterinary product labelled for cattle and the mite identified. Old chemical names or trade names in a textbook must not be treated as current treatment recommendations.”

**Disposition:** Do not reproduce this list as a treatment menu. Correct “malathione” to “malathion” only through a separately approved delivery correction. Keep “diazuntol” unresolved unless the original product label or a competent veterinary authority identifies it. Do not recommend benzyl benzoate or BHC/lindane for cattle. No chemical application instructions or concentrations are supplied.

### 4. Bovine tuberculosis and “isonicotinichydracide” (blocks 1221–1222)

**Finding:** The disease is bovine tuberculosis (bTB), caused mainly by *Mycobacterium bovis*. It is a zoonosis: infection can pass between animals and humans, including through close contact and consumption of unpasteurised contaminated animal products. Bovine TB is a regulated public-health and animal-health problem, not a condition for an informal farm drug regimen.

“Isonicotinichydracide” appears to be a misspelling or compressed rendering of **isonicotinic acid hydrazide**, commonly known as **isoniazid** (INH), an antimycobacterial drug. However, identifying the likely drug name does not validate the source's stated cattle treatment. USDA APHIS states that tuberculosis is not treated in livestock; affected herds are managed through depopulation or testing and removal of reactor animals. WOAH's 2024 livestock guidelines discuss control strategies that complement or differ from test-and-slaughter in some settings, but do not validate the source's isoniazid regimen as routine cattle treatment.

The source's stated dose and eight-week duration are not endorsed here. They are not a standard cattle-treatment instruction in the official livestock-control guidance reviewed. Treating food-producing cattle with human anti-tuberculosis drugs can create public-health, residue, antimicrobial-resistance and disease-control concerns. It may also mask infection without reliably eliminating the herd-level risk.

**Recommended wording:** “Bovine tuberculosis is a zoonotic disease caused mainly by *Mycobacterium bovis*. Suspected cases must be reported to veterinary authorities and investigated using approved diagnostic testing. Control is based on official surveillance, testing, movement controls and removal of infected animals as directed by veterinary authorities; the source's drug regimen must not be taught as a cattle treatment.”

**Disposition:** Remove the drug-and-dose statement from any learner-facing treatment guidance. Do not substitute another drug or regimen. If the intended spelling is needed for historical source transcription, record “isonicotinic acid hydrazide (isoniazid)” as a likely identification, not as a treatment recommendation. Preserve the source wording in the source archive and leave the official treatment decision to the competent veterinary authority.

4. **Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health**, “Ivomec.” Identifies Ivomec as a brand of ivermectin and describes its formulations/indications as product-dependent. https://www.boehringer-ingelheim.com/animal-health/products/ivomec
5. **US National Library of Medicine, DailyMed**, “IVOMEC (ivermectin) Injection for Cattle and Swine,” official veterinary drug label. Identifies IVOMEC as ivermectin and lists product-specific species/parasite indications; cited only for brand/active-ingredient identity, not for dosage or use instructions in Nigeria. https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=84fe39b2-0423-479a-b81f-198e2fb4e826
6. **Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants**, “Listing of POPs.” Identifies alpha-HCH and beta-HCH as Annex A chemicals for elimination and lindane (gamma-HCH) as an Annex A chemical with a narrow specific exemption for human-health pharmaceutical use. https://chm.pops.int/TheConvention/ThePOPs/AllPOPs/tabid/2509/Default.aspx

8. **World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) (22 October 2024)**, “Guidelines for the control of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex in livestock.” Provides current international guidance on bTB control strategies, including approaches to complement or differ from testing and slaughter. https://www.woah.org/en/document/guidelines-for-the-control-of-mycobacterium-tuberculosis-complex-in-livestock/
9. **USDA APHIS**, “NVAP Reference Guide: Tuberculosis (Control and Eradication).” Describes official diagnostic testing and regulatory control responsibilities for bovine tuberculosis. https://www.aphis.usda.gov/nvap/reference-guide/control-eradication/tuberculosis

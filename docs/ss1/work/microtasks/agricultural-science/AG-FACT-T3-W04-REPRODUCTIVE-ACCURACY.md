## 1. Vas deferens, uterus masculinus and sperm storage — block 485

### Source claim
The source states that the testes are connected to the “uterus masculinus” by the vas deferens, which transports sperm to the uterus masculinus, where mature spermatozoa are stored. It also says the epididymis stores sperm until they mature.

### Veterinary anatomy finding
- Sperm are produced in the seminiferous tubules of the testes.
- Sperm then pass through the rete testis and efferent ductules to the **epididymis** (head, body and tail). The epididymis is the site of sperm maturation and storage; the tail (cauda) is the principal storage region in bulls.
- The **ductus deferens (vas deferens)** continues from the tail of the epididymis and transports sperm toward the pelvic urethra, where it joins the pathway for semen. It does not lead to the uterus masculinus.
- The **uterus masculinus** is a possible vestigial remnant of the embryonic paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts in the male, sometimes visible as a midline thickening in the genital fold. It is not a functional uterus, sperm duct or sperm-storage organ.

### Evidence
- University of Minnesota College of Veterinary Medicine, *CVM Large Animal Anatomy: Reproductive Tracts and Genitalia* (revised Spring 2024), lines 74, 84, 87 and 102: defines spermatic-cord contents, shows the ductus deferens leading from the epididymal tail, and identifies uterus masculinus as a paramesonephric-duct remnant. https://open.lib.umn.edu/largeanimalanatomy/chapter/pelvis-genitalia/
- Merck Veterinary Manual, “The Gonads and Tubular Genital Tract in Animals” (reviewed by DVM specialists, February 2025), lines 61–64: describes the tract as testis → rete testis/efferent ductules → epididymis → ductus deferens → pelvic urethra, and states that the epididymis is involved in sperm maturation and storage. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/reproductive-system/reproductive-system-introduction/the-gonads-and-tubular-genital-tract-in-animals
- Oregon State University Extension, “Basics of reproductive function in bulls”, lines 19–25: explains sperm production in the testis, maturation and storage in the epididymis (especially the tail), and transport by the vas deferens. https://extension.oregonstate.edu/es/node/135281/printable/print

### Recommended learner wording
> Sperm are produced in the testes and pass to the epididymis, where they mature and are stored (mainly in its tail). The vas deferens carries sperm from the epididymis to the pelvic urethra. The uterus masculinus, when present, is a vestigial remnant of the embryonic paramesonephric ducts and is not part of the sperm pathway.

## 2. Cowper's and Bartholin's glands — blocks 486 and 492

### Source claim
Block 486 correctly calls Cowper's gland the bulbourethral gland in the male. Block 492 then says “the cowper's glands also called bartholin's gland” and attributes vaginal lubrication to it.

### Veterinary anatomy finding
- **Cowper's gland** is the male **bulbourethral gland**, an accessory sex gland. It contributes secretions to seminal fluid; the details and relative size vary among domestic species.
- **Bartholin's gland** is the human anatomical name for the female **greater vestibular gland**. In female domestic animals, the corresponding structures are described as major/greater vestibular glands, with presence and development varying by species.
- The male bulbourethral and female greater vestibular glands are **homologous structures**, but they are not the same gland and are not interchangeable names for one organ. The source's wording confuses sex-specific anatomy.
- In the male, bulbourethral secretions contribute to seminal fluid and urethral lubrication. In the female, vestibular-gland mucus helps lubricate the vestibule/vaginal opening.

### Evidence
- University of Minnesota College of Veterinary Medicine, *CVM Large Animal Anatomy*, lines 101–107 and 273: identifies male bulbourethral glands as accessory sex glands and identifies the major vestibular gland in the female bovine tract. https://open.lib.umn.edu/largeanimalanatomy/chapter/pelvis-genitalia/
- StatPearls/NCBI Bookshelf, “Anatomy, Abdomen and Pelvis: Bartholin Gland” (updated 9 November 2024): identifies Bartholin glands as the female greater vestibular glands, describes their mucus secretion for lubrication, and notes their homology with male bulbourethral (Cowper) glands. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK557803/
- Merck Veterinary Manual, “The Gonads and Tubular Genital Tract in Animals”, lines 64–65: confirms that bulbourethral glands are male accessory sex glands and that their presence varies by species. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/reproductive-system/reproductive-system-introduction/the-gonads-and-tubular-genital-tract-in-animals

### Recommended learner wording
> In male animals, the bulbourethral glands (Cowper's glands) are accessory sex glands that contribute mucus to seminal fluid. In females, the greater vestibular glands (called Bartholin's glands in human anatomy) secrete mucus that helps lubricate the vestibule. These are homologous but distinct male and female structures.

Do not describe Cowper's glands as female vaginal glands.

## 3. Vaginal length — block 492

### Source claim
The source gives a general vaginal length of **7.5–10 cm** for farm animals.

### Veterinary anatomy finding
Vaginal length is species-dependent; a single 7.5–10 cm figure is not a valid general measurement for farm animals. A comparative veterinary anatomy table (modified from Frandson et al., 2003) gives these representative measurements for nonpregnant animals:
- Mare: **20–35 cm**
- Sow: **10–15 cm**
- Cow: **25–30 cm**
- Ewe: **10–14 cm**

The cow's vaginal length is therefore substantially greater than the source's generic value. Values can also vary with breed, age, reproductive status and measurement method.

### Evidence
- *Veterian Key*, “Reproduction”, Table 19.1, “Comparative anatomy of female reproductive tracts”, modified from Frandson et al. (2003), lines 91–116: provides vagina lengths for mare, sow, cow and ewe. https://veteriankey.com/reproduction-3/
- Comparative anatomy review, “Female Genital Variation Far Exceeds That of Male Genitalia” (2022), reports cow vagina length of approximately 20–28 cm, consistent with the comparative veterinary table. https://academic.oup.com/icb/article/62/3/581/6582219

### Recommended learner wording
> The vagina is a muscular, elastic part of the female reproductive tract between the cervix and vestibule. Its length varies by species; for example, it is about 25–30 cm in a cow, 10–14 cm in an ewe, 10–15 cm in a sow and 20–35 cm in a mare.

If the source is intended to describe one species, name that species beside the measurement.

## 4. “Sex-inhibited characters” — block 482

### Source claim
The source states that hormones play an important role in reproduction and the development of “sex-inhibited characters”.

### Terminology finding
- “Sex-inhibited characters” is not a standard category in the contemporary animal-genetics glossaries consulted.
- Standard terminology distinguishes:
  - **Secondary sex characteristics:** physical/behavioural traits that develop under the influence of sex hormones, beyond the primary reproductive organs.
  - **Sex-limited traits:** genes may be present in both sexes, but the trait is expressed in only one sex (e.g. milk production in females).
  - **Sex-influenced traits:** traits expressed in both sexes but with expression/dominance affected by sex (e.g. horn expression in some sheep breeds).
  - **Sex-linked traits:** traits controlled by genes located on sex chromosomes.
- Merck Veterinary Manual states that sex hormones contribute to the development of secondary sex characteristics.
- Iowa State University's genetics glossary defines sex-influenced and sex-limited traits, but not “sex-inhibited” as a standard category.

### Evidence
- Merck Veterinary Manual, “The Gonads and Tubular Genital Tract in Animals”, lines 44–48: hormones and gonadal function in development of secondary sex characteristics. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/reproductive-system/reproductive-system-introduction/the-gonads-and-tubular-genital-tract-in-animals
- Iowa State University, “Genetic nomenclature, glossaries”: defines sex-influenced, sex-limited and sex-linked traits. https://www.genome.iastate.edu/edu/genetics/glossaries/genetic_terms
- University of California Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory, genetics glossary: defines sex-linked traits and distinguishes them from other inheritance categories. https://vgl.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/resources/genetics-glossary

### Recommended learner wording
For the sentence in block 482, if the intended meaning is hormone-driven development of male/female features, use:

> Reproductive hormones also influence the development of secondary sex characteristics.

If the lesson is specifically teaching inheritance, introduce **sex-limited** and **sex-influenced** traits separately and define them with examples. Do not silently replace “sex-inhibited” with one of these without confirming the intended genetics concept.

## Findings and disposition
1. **Clear anatomical error:** sperm are stored/matured in the epididymis, not in the uterus masculinus; the vas deferens leads to the pelvic urethra.
2. **Clear gland error:** Cowper's/bulbourethral glands are male; Bartholin's/greater vestibular glands are female. They are homologous, not synonyms.
3. **Species-qualification error:** 7.5–10 cm cannot be stated as a general farm-animal vaginal length; representative measurements differ markedly by species.
4. **Terminology issue:** “sex-inhibited characters” should be replaced by “secondary sex characteristics” if referring to hormone-driven development, or by the appropriate standard genetic term (sex-limited/sex-influenced) if referring to inheritance.

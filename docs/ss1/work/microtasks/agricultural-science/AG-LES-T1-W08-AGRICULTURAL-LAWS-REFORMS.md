## Lesson body

Week 8 Supplement: Nigerian Agricultural Laws and Reforms

## 1. Why agricultural laws and reforms matter

Agricultural laws are rules made by government that regulate matters affecting farming, land, water, credit, insurance and other agricultural activities. A reform is a change introduced to improve how a system works. Some reforms are established by Acts or Decrees; others are schemes or government programmes.

Agricultural laws and reforms can help farmers obtain land, improve water supply, access credit, manage risks and increase production. They can also set conditions that farmers, banks, companies and government agencies must follow.

## 2. The Land Use Act 1978

The Land Use Act began as **Decree No. 6 of 1978** and is now commonly called the **Land Use Act 1978**. It changed the legal system for controlling and allocating land in Nigeria.

### Main provisions

1. **Section 1 — land held in trust:** Land in each State is vested in the Governor, who holds and administers it in trust for the use and common benefit of Nigerians. This is about the State's radical title and administration of land; it does not mean the Governor personally occupies or farms every plot.
2. **Section 2 — who manages land:** Land in urban areas is under the control and management of the State Governor. Other land is under the control and management of the relevant Local Government, subject to the Act.
3. **Section 5 — statutory rights of occupancy:** The Governor may grant a statutory right of occupancy to a person for different purposes.
4. **Section 6 — customary rights of occupancy:** A Local Government may grant customary rights of occupancy over non-urban land to a person or organisation for agricultural, residential, grazing and other permitted purposes.
5. **Sections 21 and 22 — transfer and dealings:** The Act regulates the assignment, mortgage, transfer or sublease of customary and statutory rights of occupancy. The appropriate Local Government's approval or the Governor's consent is required, as applicable.
6. **Section 24 — inheritance:** Rights of occupancy may devolve on a person's death under the applicable customary law, subject to the Act and other applicable law.
7. **Section 28 — revocation:** A right of occupancy may be revoked for overriding public interest. The Act also provides rules on compensation in specified circumstances.

### Effects on landholding, agricultural access and tenure

- The Act places ultimate title and land administration under the State Governor, while dividing management responsibilities between State and Local Government.
- People can still hold legally recognised interests in land. These include statutory and customary rights of occupancy held by individuals, families or organisations. The Act does **not** simply prevent individuals from holding or using land.
- Farmers may obtain customary rights of occupancy over non-urban land for agriculture and grazing, subject to the Act and the relevant authority's procedures.
- Transfers, mortgages and leases are regulated by approval or consent requirements. These procedures can affect how easily a farmer transfers land rights or uses them in a credit transaction.
- The Act recognises inheritance of rights of occupancy. Existing customary and other land interests were also addressed by transitional provisions when the Act began.
- Government may revoke a right of occupancy for overriding public interest, subject to the Act's rules, including applicable compensation provisions.

### Correcting a misleading statement

The original Week 7 source says that the 1978 land-use decree “prevents individuals from owning land”. This is too broad and can mislead learners.

A more accurate explanation is:

**The Land Use Act 1978 vests radical title to land in each State in the Governor, to be held in trust. Individuals, families and communities may hold statutory or customary rights of occupancy. These rights may be inherited and may be transferred, mortgaged or subleased subject to the Act's applicable consent or approval requirements.**

The important distinction is between **absolute freehold/radical title** and a **legal right of occupancy**. The Act changes the former but provides for the latter. It does not mean that farmers and families have no legal rights in land.

The Act does not automatically give every farmer land. Access depends on the applicable right, allocation or recognition, local circumstances and administrative procedures. In practice, the time and difficulty involved in obtaining documentation or the required consent can affect agricultural access and investment.

## 3. Other important Nigerian agricultural laws and statutory reforms

### A. National Agricultural Land Development Authority Act 1992

The National Agricultural Land Development Authority Act is **1992 No. 92**. It established the National Agricultural Land Development Authority (NALDA) to provide strategic public support for land development.

The Act's objectives include improving the use of rural land for food and fibre production, supporting viable farm holdings, consolidating scattered or fragmented farm plots, promoting sustainable land management, providing extension support and helping to improve rural incomes and employment.

**Importance to agriculture:** It provides a legal framework for public support to develop suitable land and make farming more productive and sustainable.

### B. River Basin Development Authorities Decree 1976

The River Basin Development Authorities were established under **Decree No. 25 of 1976**. Their original purpose was to develop Nigeria's water resources for agricultural, domestic and industrial use.

Their work included dams, irrigation infrastructure, boreholes, watershed management, erosion control and flood control. Their responsibilities have changed over time; the authorities now focus more broadly on water-resource potential and watershed management, rather than directly carrying out all agricultural production.

**Importance to agriculture:** Reliable water resources and irrigation infrastructure can support crop production, especially where rainfall is insufficient or irregular.

### C. Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund Decree 1977

The Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme (ACGS) was established by **Decree No. 20 of 1977** and became operational in April 1978. The Central Bank of Nigeria manages the scheme.

The scheme provides a guarantee in respect of eligible loans granted by participating banks for agricultural purposes. It is intended to reduce some of the risk banks face when lending to farmers and agricultural businesses, and thereby encourage more agricultural lending.

**Importance to agriculture:** It can make it easier for eligible farmers and agricultural businesses to obtain bank credit. It is a loan-guarantee scheme, not a free grant; borrowers remain responsible for repaying their loans.

### D. Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation Act 1993

The Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation (NAIC) was established under **Act No. 37 of 1993**. The Act provides for agricultural insurance for agricultural and agro-allied projects against selected natural hazards.

NAIC offers insurance for eligible crop and livestock risks and may pay an indemnity when an insured loss occurs under the policy. The risks covered, premium, exclusions and conditions depend on the insurance product and policy.

**Importance to agriculture:** Insurance can reduce the financial impact of certain farm losses and support farmers and lenders when an insured event occurs. It does not mean that every loss is automatically covered.

## 4. Comparing the five instruments

- **Land Use Act 1978:** regulates land administration and rights of occupancy.
- **NALDA Act 1992:** provides for public support for agricultural land development and viable farm holdings.
- **River Basin Development Authorities Decree 1976:** provides a framework for developing water resources and irrigation-related infrastructure.
- **Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund Decree 1977:** supports bank lending to eligible agricultural activities through loan guarantees.
- **Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation Act 1993:** provides an institutional and legal framework for agricultural insurance against selected risks.

These instruments do different jobs. A land law does not itself provide a farm loan; a loan guarantee is not insurance; and an insurance policy does not grant a farmer a right to occupy land.

## 5. Quick revision

1. The Land Use Act 1978 vests radical title in the State Governor but allows individuals and organisations to hold statutory or customary rights of occupancy.
2. NALDA supports agricultural land development and viable farm holdings.
3. River Basin Development Authorities develop water resources and infrastructure that can support irrigation.
4. The Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme helps participating banks lend to eligible agricultural activities by guaranteeing part of the risk.
5. NAIC provides insurance against selected agricultural risks under the terms of an insurance policy.

Remember: identify whether an instrument is an **Act, Decree, authority or scheme**. They are related, but they are not all the same kind of legal instrument.

### P2 — Land Use Act 1978 (8 marks)

**Question:** Explain four important effects or provisions of the Land Use Act 1978 on landholding, agricultural access and tenure.

**Answer:** The Act vests radical title to land in each State in the Governor, to hold in trust for the common benefit of Nigerians. Urban land is managed by the Governor, while other land is managed by the relevant Local Government. The Governor may grant statutory rights of occupancy and Local Governments may grant customary rights of occupancy for agricultural and other permitted uses. Transfers, mortgages and leases are subject to the applicable approval or consent requirements; rights of occupancy may also devolve on death under applicable customary law. Therefore, the Act changes the system of ultimate title but does not prevent individuals, families or communities from holding rights of occupancy.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains that radical title is vested in the State Governor to be held in trust.
- 2 marks — Distinguishes State management of urban land from Local Government management of other land.
- 2 marks — Explains that persons/organisations may hold statutory or customary rights of occupancy, including for agriculture.
- 2 marks — Explains transfer consent/approval or inheritance of occupancy rights and rejects the claim that individuals cannot hold land rights.

### P3 — ACGSF and NAIC (4 marks)

**Question:** Distinguish the Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund from the Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation.

**Answer:** The Agricultural Credit Guarantee Scheme Fund reduces some of the risk to participating banks when they lend to eligible agricultural activities; it is a loan guarantee and the borrower must still repay the loan. The Nigerian Agricultural Insurance Corporation provides insurance against selected agricultural risks and may pay an indemnity when an insured loss occurs under the policy.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains that ACGSF supports bank lending through a guarantee and is not a grant or loan waiver.
- 2 marks — Explains that NAIC provides insurance against selected risks and indemnity under policy conditions.

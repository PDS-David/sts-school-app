## What the statute actually provides

### Section 1 — vesting of radical title
- All land comprised in the territory of each State is vested in the Governor of that State, to be held in trust and administered for the use and common benefit of all Nigerians.
- This changes the legal structure of ultimate title/control; it does not mean the Governor personally owns every parcel or that individuals cannot hold enforceable land rights.
- Source: [Land Use Act 1978, official Gazette copy hosted by Adamawa State Lands Department](https://lands.adamawastate.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/NIGERIA-LAND-USE-ACT-GAZZETED-1978.pdf), section 1, lines 277–288.

### Sections 2, 5 and 6 — administration and rights of occupancy
- Section 2 places urban land under the control and management of the State Governor and other land under the relevant Local Government, subject to the Act.
- Section 5 authorises the Governor to grant **statutory rights of occupancy to any person for all purposes**.
- Section 6 authorises a Local Government to grant **customary rights of occupancy to any person or organisation** for land outside an urban area, including agricultural, residential and grazing purposes.
- These express grants contradict a blanket claim that individuals cannot hold land.
- Sources:
  - [Official Gazette copy — section 2](https://lands.adamawastate.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/NIGERIA-LAND-USE-ACT-GAZZETED-1978.pdf), lines 289–298.
  - [Official Gazette copy — sections 5–6](https://lands.adamawastate.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/NIGERIA-LAND-USE-ACT-GAZZETED-1978.pdf), lines 363–376 and 414–431.

### Sections 21 and 22 — transfer is restricted, not prohibited
- A customary right of occupancy may be alienated only with the approval of the appropriate Local Government, subject to the Act's exceptions (section 21).
- A statutory right of occupancy may be assigned, mortgaged, transferred or subleased only with the Governor's prior consent (section 22).
- The Act therefore regulates transactions and prevents unauthorised alienation; it does not say that holders have no rights or can never transfer them.
- Source: [Official Gazette copy — sections 21–22](https://lands.adamawastate.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/NIGERIA-LAND-USE-ACT-GAZZETED-1978.pdf), lines 742–773.

### Section 24 — rights can devolve on death
- Section 24 provides for the devolution of rights of occupancy on death. Customary rights generally devolve under the customary law of the locality; statutory rights generally devolve under the customary law of the deceased occupier, unless another applicable law applies.
- This confirms that rights of occupancy can pass to successors; inheritance is not extinguished by the Act.
- Source: [Official Gazette copy — section 24](https://lands.adamawastate.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/NIGERIA-LAND-USE-ACT-GAZZETED-1978.pdf), lines 819–840.

### Sections 34 and 36 — existing rights at commencement
- The transitional provisions address land held before the Act came into force. Developed urban land and qualifying non-urban land were treated as held under statutory or customary rights of occupancy, rather than being automatically rendered ownerless.
- The Supreme Court has applied these provisions to recognise pre-existing holders/occupiers as continuing under the relevant right of occupancy.
- Source: [Official Gazette copy — transitional provisions](https://lands.adamawastate.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/NIGERIA-LAND-USE-ACT-GAZZETED-1978.pdf), sections 34 and 36.

## Nigerian Supreme Court interpretation

### *Joshua Ogunleye v Babatayo Oni* [1990] NGSC 40
- The Supreme Court recognised that a holder or occupier of rural land under a recognised customary tenure before the Act continued to hold and enjoy the land as if a customary right of occupancy had been granted by the Local Government.
- The decision demonstrates that the Act did not simply extinguish individual or customary landholding rights.
- Source: [NigeriaLII — Joshua Ogunleye v Babatayo Oni, Supreme Court of Nigeria, 27 April 1990](https://nigerialii.org/akn/ng/judgment/ngsc/1990/40/eng%401990-04-26).

### *Garuba Abioye & Others v Sa’adu Yakubu & Others* [1991] NGSC 2
- The Supreme Court considered whether sections 1 and 36 of the Act abolished the rights of customary owners in relation to customary tenants of agricultural land.
- The judgment distinguishes a **holder** entitled to a right of occupancy from an **occupier** lawfully using land under customary law. It recognises the continued importance of customary interests, subject to the Act's statutory controls.
- Source: [NigeriaLII — Garuba Abioye & Others v Sa’adu Yakubu & Others, Supreme Court of Nigeria, 10 June 1991](https://nigerialii.org/akn/ng/judgment/ngsc/1991/2/eng%401991-06-10).

## Legal finding
The Act has two effects that must not be conflated:
1. **It removes radical/absolute freehold title from private persons and vests radical title in the State Governor as trustee.**
2. **It preserves and provides for individual, family and community interests through statutory and customary rights of occupancy.** Those rights may be granted, held, inherited and transferred, subject to the Act's consent/approval requirements and other conditions.

Accordingly, “the Land Use Act prevents individuals from owning land” is too broad. If “owning” means holding absolute freehold title, the Act changes that position. If it means holding a legally recognised interest in land, individuals and organisations can hold rights of occupancy. The source sentence wrongly collapses these two meanings.

## Recommended correction
Replace the Week Seven statement with:

> **The Land Use Act 1978 vests radical title to land in each State in the Governor, to be held in trust for the use and common benefit of Nigerians. Individuals, families and communities may hold statutory or customary rights of occupancy. These rights may be inherited and may be assigned, mortgaged or subleased subject to the Act's applicable consent or approval requirements.**

This is more accurate than saying the Act prevents individuals from owning land. It also preserves the important distinction between absolute freehold title and rights of occupancy.

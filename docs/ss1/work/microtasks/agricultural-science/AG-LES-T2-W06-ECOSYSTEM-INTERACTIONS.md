Week Six Supplement: Ecosystem Interactions in Fish Ponds, Forests and Savannas

## 1. What is an ecosystem interaction?

An ecosystem contains living things and the non-living environment around them. The living parts are called **biotic components**. Examples include plants, fish, insects, birds, mammals, fungi and bacteria. The non-living parts are called **abiotic components**. Examples include sunlight, water, air, temperature, soil, minerals and dissolved oxygen.

An interaction occurs when organisms affect one another or when living things affect and are affected by non-living conditions. Organisms may feed on one another, compete for resources, provide shelter, pollinate flowers, disperse seeds or decompose dead material.

The source already teaches interactions in mono-cropping, mixed cropping and mixed farming. This supplement adds two settings requested by the assessment: fish ponds and forest/savanna ecosystems.

## 2. Interactions in a fish-pond ecosystem

A fish pond is an aquatic ecosystem. Its living organisms interact with the water, sunlight, temperature, dissolved oxygen and nutrients.

### A. Producers, consumers and decomposers

- **Producers:** Phytoplankton (tiny floating algae) and other aquatic plants use sunlight, water and nutrients to make food by photosynthesis. They form the base of many pond food chains and release oxygen into the water during photosynthesis.
- **Primary consumers:** Zooplankton, insect larvae, snails and some small fish feed on phytoplankton, aquatic plants or other small organisms.
- **Larger consumers:** Larger fish may feed on smaller fish, insects or other aquatic animals. Birds and other animals may also feed on fish.
- **Decomposers:** Bacteria and fungi break down dead plants, dead animals and organic waste. This process releases nutrients back into the water and pond bottom, where producers can use them again.

### B. Example of a pond food chain

One possible food chain is:

**Sunlight → phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → larger fish**

Another pathway may be:

**Aquatic plants or insect larvae → fish → fish-eating bird**

These are examples, not the only feeding relationships in a pond. A food web contains several connected food chains because an organism may have more than one food source or predator.

### C. How living things interact with water conditions

Sunlight affects photosynthesis by algae and aquatic plants. During daylight, photosynthesis adds oxygen to the water. Fish, plants, bacteria and other organisms also respire and use oxygen. At night, photosynthesis stops but respiration continues, so dissolved oxygen can fall.

Temperature, water clarity, pH, nutrient levels and dissolved oxygen affect the growth and survival of pond organisms. For example, fish need enough dissolved oxygen to survive. Bacteria also use oxygen while decomposing organic waste.

### D. Why balance matters in a fish pond

A balanced pond can support natural food organisms and healthy fish. However, too much uneaten feed, manure, dead algae or other organic waste can increase decomposition. Decomposition uses dissolved oxygen. Excessive algal growth can also cause large day-and-night oxygen changes. If oxygen becomes too low, fish may become stressed or die.

Good pond management therefore includes avoiding overfeeding, preventing excessive waste, observing fish behaviour and maintaining suitable water quality. Do not assume that adding more manure or feed will always produce more fish.

## 3. Interactions in a forest ecosystem

A forest contains trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers, animals, insects, fungi and microorganisms. The canopy formed by taller trees affects how much sunlight reaches plants below it.

Examples of forest interactions include:

1. **Competition for resources:** Trees and smaller plants may compete for sunlight, water, space and soil nutrients. A dense canopy may shade plants growing underneath it.
2. **Food relationships:** Herbivores feed on leaves, fruits, seeds or other plant parts. Predators feed on some of those animals. This creates connected food chains and food webs.
3. **Pollination and seed dispersal:** Insects, birds and other animals may carry pollen between flowers or move seeds away from parent plants. This helps plants reproduce and spread.
4. **Decomposition and nutrient recycling:** Fungi, bacteria, termites and other decomposers break down fallen leaves, dead wood and animal remains. Nutrients return to the soil and can be used by plants.
5. **Soil and water protection:** Tree roots help hold soil in place. Forest cover and leaf litter reduce direct exposure of soil to heavy rain and can help protect water resources.

### Forest interactions and agriculture

Trees can be combined with crops and livestock in **agroforestry**. For example, suitable trees may provide fruit, shade, fodder, timber or fuelwood while crops are grown nearby. Trees can also contribute to soil fertility, reduce erosion and provide habitat for pollinators and natural enemies of some crop pests.

The correct combination depends on the crop, tree species, spacing, light requirements and local conditions. Too much shade or competition can reduce the growth of some crops, so trees and crops must be managed together.

## 4. Interactions in a savanna ecosystem

A savanna is a tropical ecosystem in which grasses are dominant, with scattered trees and shrubs. Many savannas have seasonal rainfall and a pronounced dry season. The amount of grass, tree cover and wildlife varies with rainfall, soil, grazing and fire.

Examples of savanna interactions include:

1. **Grass and grazing animals:** Cattle, antelope and other herbivores feed on grasses. Grazing reduces the amount of grass and can influence which plant species remain common.
2. **Trees and browsing animals:** Goats, antelope, giraffes and other browsers feed on leaves, shoots or young branches of trees and shrubs. Browsing can affect how young trees grow and survive.
3. **Grass, fire and tree growth:** Dry grass can provide fuel for fires. Fire can affect young trees and grasses, while grazing animals may reduce the amount of dry grass available to burn. The balance differs with rainfall, soil and the timing and intensity of fire.
4. **Decomposition and nutrient recycling:** Termites, fungi, bacteria and other decomposers break down dry grass, dung, leaves and wood. Nutrients are returned to the soil and become available to plants.
5. **Rainfall and soil:** Rainfall and soil conditions influence which grasses and trees can grow. Plants also affect the soil through roots, shade and the return of organic matter.

### Savanna interactions and agriculture

Savannas support crop farming and livestock grazing. Crop residues may provide feed for animals, while animal manure can return nutrients to fields when managed properly. However, excessive grazing can remove too much plant cover, leaving soil exposed to erosion. Poorly managed fires can damage vegetation and young trees.

Farmers and herders therefore need to manage grazing, crop residues, trees, soil cover and fire carefully so that the ecosystem can continue to support production.

## 5. Comparing the three ecosystems

| Ecosystem | Important living components | Important non-living factors | Examples of interactions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fish pond | Algae, aquatic plants, zooplankton, insects, fish, bacteria and fungi | Water, sunlight, temperature, nutrients, pH and dissolved oxygen | Food chains, predation, photosynthesis, respiration and decomposition |
| Forest | Trees, shrubs, herbs, insects, birds, mammals, fungi and bacteria | Sunlight, rainfall, air, soil, water and temperature | Competition for light, pollination, seed dispersal, feeding and nutrient recycling |
| Savanna | Grasses, trees, shrubs, grazing animals, browsers, termites and microbes | Seasonal rainfall, sunlight, soil, temperature and fire | Grazing, browsing, grass-tree competition, fire effects and decomposition |

## 6. Quick revision

- A fish pond is an aquatic ecosystem in which producers, consumers and decomposers interact.
- Phytoplankton use sunlight to make food and release oxygen during photosynthesis.
- Fish and other organisms need dissolved oxygen; excessive organic waste and decomposition can reduce it.
- Forests contain many layers of vegetation and food-web relationships. Pollination, seed dispersal and decomposition help maintain them.
- Savannas are dominated by grasses with scattered trees and shrubs. Grazing, browsing, rainfall, fire and decomposition influence the ecosystem.
- Agroforestry combines trees with crops or livestock; crop-livestock systems connect plant and animal production.

Remember: **an ecosystem stays productive when living organisms and non-living conditions remain in a workable balance.**

## Practice and complete marking guide

### P1 — Biotic and abiotic components (4 marks)

**Question:** Give two examples of biotic components and two examples of abiotic components in a fish-pond ecosystem.

**Answer:** Biotic components: fish, phytoplankton/algae, zooplankton, insects, aquatic plants, bacteria or fungi. Abiotic components: water, sunlight, temperature, dissolved oxygen, nutrients or pH.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Gives two correct living/biotic components, one mark each.
- 2 marks — Gives two correct non-living/abiotic components, one mark each.

### P2 — Fish-pond food web and oxygen (8 marks)

**Question:** Describe how producers, consumers and decomposers interact in a fish pond. Include one food chain and explain why dissolved oxygen is important.

**Answer:** Phytoplankton and aquatic plants are producers because they use sunlight and nutrients to make food. Zooplankton and insect larvae feed on phytoplankton or other small organisms. Small fish feed on zooplankton or insects, and larger fish may feed on smaller fish. Bacteria and fungi decompose dead organisms and organic waste, returning nutrients to the pond. One food chain is sunlight → phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → larger fish. Dissolved oxygen is needed by fish and other organisms for respiration; photosynthesis adds oxygen during daylight, while respiration and decomposition use it. Excess organic waste can reduce oxygen.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Identifies phytoplankton/aquatic plants as producers and explains photosynthesis.
- 2 marks — Explains how zooplankton or insect larvae feed on producers/smaller organisms.
- 2 marks — Explains fish feeding relationships and gives a valid pond food chain.
- 2 marks — Explains decomposers' nutrient-recycling role and the importance of dissolved oxygen.

### P3 — Forest interactions (6 marks)

**Question:** Explain three interactions that occur in a forest ecosystem.

**Answer:** Trees and smaller plants compete for sunlight, water and nutrients. Insects and birds may pollinate flowers or disperse seeds. Fungi, bacteria and termites decompose leaf litter and dead wood, returning nutrients to the soil. Herbivores feed on plants and predators feed on some herbivores.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains competition among plants for light, water, space or nutrients.
- 2 marks — Explains pollination, seed dispersal or a feeding relationship involving animals.
- 2 marks — Explains decomposition and nutrient recycling, or tree-root/forest-cover soil protection.

### P4 — Savanna interactions (6 marks)

**Question:** Explain three interactions that occur in a savanna ecosystem.

**Answer:** Grasses provide food for grazing animals such as cattle and antelope. Browsing animals feed on tree leaves and shoots and may affect tree regeneration. Fire and grazing influence the balance between grasses and trees. Termites and microbes decompose dry grass, dung and wood, returning nutrients to the soil. Rainfall and soil conditions influence the vegetation that can grow.

**Marking:**
- 2 marks — Explains the interaction between grasses and grazing animals.
- 2 marks — Explains browsing or the interaction of fire/grazing with tree and grass growth.
- 2 marks — Explains decomposition/nutrient recycling or the role of rainfall and soil.

### P5 — Apply savanna ecology (4 marks)

**Question:** A savanna grazing area has too many animals feeding on the grass for a long period. Explain two possible effects and give one management response.

**Answer:** Heavy grazing may remove too much grass and reduce ground cover. Bare soil may then be more exposed to wind and rain erosion, and less grass may remain for future grazing. A management response is to reduce stocking pressure or use rotational grazing/rest periods so vegetation can recover.

**Marking:**
- 1 mark — Explains that excessive grazing reduces grass/vegetation cover.
- 1 mark — Explains a consequence such as soil exposure, erosion or reduced future fodder.
- 2 marks — Suggests a suitable management response such as controlled/rotational grazing or resting the land.

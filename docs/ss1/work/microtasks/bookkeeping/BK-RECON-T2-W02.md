## Topic
The general journal (journal proper)

## Learning outcomes
By the end of the lesson, learners should be able to:
1. Define the general journal and state its uses.
2. Identify its main columns.
3. Distinguish journal entries from entries in specialised books of original entry.
4. Prepare simple journal entries with dates, accounts, amounts and narrations.
5. Apply debit and credit correctly to simple non-routine transactions.

## Prerequisite
Learners should know the basic debit-and-credit principle and the purpose of books of original entry from Week 1.

## 1. Meaning and purpose
The general journal, also called the journal proper, is a book of original entry used for transactions and adjustments that are not first recorded in a more specialised book. It provides a dated explanation of the accounts to be debited and credited.

It is not a replacement for the sales day book, purchases day book or cash book. Those books are used for their specific transaction classes.

Common uses include:
- opening entries when the books of a business are first set up;
- correction of certain accounting errors;
- transfer entries between accounts when a journal entry is appropriate;
- recording certain non-routine transactions that do not belong in another specialised book.

Some topics, such as detailed correction of errors and suspense accounts, are studied later. This lesson introduces the journal format only; it does not replace the later correction-of-errors lessons.

## 2. Format of the general journal
A simple journal has these columns:

| Date | Particulars | Folio | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Date of entry | Account debited and account credited, with a short explanation | Ledger page reference | Amount | Amount |

The debit account is written first, usually on the left. The credit account is written on the next line and indented. A short narration follows to explain the transaction. The folio column is completed when the entries are posted to the ledger.

## 3. Preparing a journal entry
Use this sequence:
1. Read the transaction and identify what the business received or what benefit it obtained.
2. Identify what the business gave, owed or used.
3. Select the accounts affected.
4. Apply the debit-and-credit rules.
5. Enter the debit account first and the credit account second.
6. Write a concise narration.
7. Check that total debit equals total credit.

## 4. Worked examples

### Example A — Owner introduces equipment as capital
On 1 September, the owner introduces office equipment valued at 45,000 into the business as additional capital. No cash changes hands.

| Date | Particulars | Folio | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Sept. 1 | Office Equipment | — | 45,000 | |
| | Capital | — | | 45,000 |
| | Being office equipment introduced by the owner as additional capital. | | | |

Explanation: the business receives an asset, so Office Equipment is debited. Capital increases, so Capital is credited. Debit and credit are both 45,000.

### Example B — Transfer an amount from one expense account to another
On 30 September, 6,000 wrongly included in Repairs Expense is identified as Insurance Expense. The correcting journal transfers the amount to the correct expense account.

| Date | Particulars | Folio | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Sept. 30 | Insurance Expense | — | 6,000 | |
| | Repairs Expense | — | | 6,000 |
| | Being the amount reclassified from repairs to insurance. | | | |

Explanation: Insurance Expense is increased by a debit; Repairs Expense is reduced by a credit. This is a simple reclassification example. Detailed error correction methods are taught in the later term.

### Example C — Record an opening position
A new business begins with cash of 20,000 and furniture of 30,000 introduced by its owner as capital.

| Date | Particulars | Folio | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Oct. 1 | Cash | — | 20,000 | |
| | Furniture | — | 30,000 | |
| | Capital | — | | 50,000 |
| | Being the opening assets introduced by the owner. | | | |

Check: total debits = 20,000 + 30,000 = 50,000; total credit = 50,000.

All examples use fictional figures and are project-authored.

## 5. Important distinctions
- A cash sale belongs in the cash book, not the general journal.
- A credit sale of goods for resale belongs in the sales day book, not the general journal.
- The general journal is not used simply because a transaction is difficult; first check whether a specialised book already exists for it.
- A journal entry is not complete merely because the two amounts are equal. The accounts and sides must also be correct.
- The folio is a cross-reference to the ledger; it is not an amount column.

## 6. Guided practice
Prepare the journal entry for each transaction.

A. The owner introduces a computer valued at 80,000 as additional capital.
B. A business reclassifies 2,500 wrongly charged to Telephone Expense as Internet Expense.

### Guided answers
A. Debit Computer 80,000; credit Capital 80,000. Narration: computer introduced by owner as additional capital.
B. Debit Internet Expense 2,500; credit Telephone Expense 2,500. Narration: amount reclassified from telephone to internet expense.

## 7. Independent assessment

**Question 1 (3 marks).** Define the general journal and state two uses.

**Answer:** The general journal is a book of original entry for transactions and adjustments not first recorded in a specialised book. Uses include opening entries, appropriate correction/reclassification entries and other non-routine entries.

**Marking:** Definition, 1 mark; two uses, 1 mark each.

**Question 2 (5 marks).** State five columns commonly found in a simple general journal.

**Answer:** Date, particulars, folio, debit and credit.

**Marking:** 1 mark for each correct column.

**Question 3 (6 marks).** Journalise: on 1 November, the owner introduces a delivery machine valued at 120,000 as capital.

**Answer:**
- Debit Delivery Machine: 120,000
- Credit Capital: 120,000
- Narration: Being a delivery machine introduced by the owner as capital.

**Marking:** Correct debit account, 2 marks; correct credit account, 2 marks; correct equal amount on both sides, 1 mark; suitable narration, 1 mark.

**Question 4 (4 marks).** Explain why a cash sale should not normally be recorded in the general journal.

**Answer:** The cash book is the specialised book of original entry for cash and bank receipts and payments. A cash sale is therefore recorded there, avoiding unnecessary duplication and keeping cash transactions together.

**Marking:** Identifies cash book, 2 marks; explains specialised purpose/avoids duplication, 2 marks.

**Total: 18 marks.**

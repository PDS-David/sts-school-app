## General field and laboratory precautions

1. Obtain permission before collecting soil. Record site, date, sample depth and sample code.
2. Do not collect from roadsides, dumps, recently sprayed areas, industrial sites, sewage-affected ground or unknown contaminated sites.
3. Wear gloves for sampling and sorting. Avoid touching the face; wash hands and clean tools after the practical. Do not taste or smell samples closely.
4. Keep samples labelled and separate. Use clean containers and avoid mixing tools between samples.
5. A trained teacher/technician must operate ovens, furnaces and hot apparatus. Use heat-resistant gloves/tongs and allow containers to cool in a safe place before weighing.
6. Do not use an open flame to dry or burn soil. Do not carry out the loss-on-ignition procedure as an unsupervised student activity.

## Practical 1 — Soil moisture content (gravimetric comparison)

**Aim:** Estimate the mass of water in a soil sample relative to its dry-soil mass.

**Apparatus:** Trowel or auger; labelled airtight sample bag; pre-weighed drying tin/container; balance; drying oven controlled at about 105 °C; heat-resistant gloves/tongs; desiccator or protected cooling area; notebook and calculator.

**Procedure:**
1. Collect a representative sample at the agreed depth. Seal and label it immediately to limit moisture loss.
2. Weigh the empty drying container and record its mass.
3. Add a representative portion of moist soil. Weigh container plus moist soil.
4. The teacher/technician dries the sample at approximately 105 °C until the mass is stable. Cool it safely before weighing.
5. Weigh the container plus dry soil. Repeat drying and weighing if needed until the mass is effectively constant.
6. Subtract the container mass to obtain moist-soil and dry-soil masses.
7. Calculate gravimetric water content on a dry-mass basis:

   `Water content (%) = [(moist soil mass − dry soil mass) ÷ dry soil mass] × 100`

8. Compare samples only when collection depth, container method and drying endpoint are consistent.

**Record:** Site/depth/sample code; container mass; moist soil mass; dry soil mass; calculated water content; observations.

**Interpretation:** The result is water mass per unit dry-soil mass. Do not confuse it with water content expressed on a wet-mass basis or with the percentage of pore space filled by water.

**Safety/limitation:** A 105 °C oven is teacher/technician operated. Air-drying may be used only for a qualitative comparison; it is not equivalent to a standard oven-dry endpoint.

**Evidence:** FAO Global Soil Doctors Programme, *Physical Properties — Exercise P06b: Soil water content, gravimetric method* (sample handling, drying and dry-mass calculation): https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/user_upload/GSP/GSDP/Field_exercises/NEW_Field_exercises/P06b-gravimetric-soil-water-EN-1-2.pdf. GLOBE, *Soil Moisture — Gravimetric*: https://www.globe.gov/do-globe/globe-teachers-guide/soil-pedosphere/soil-moisture-gravimetric.

## Practical 2 — Soil porosity (simple saturation method)

**Aim:** Compare the proportion of a known soil volume occupied by pore spaces.

**Apparatus:** Equal-volume transparent cups or measuring cylinders; graduated cylinder; water; tray; three labelled soil samples; ruler/marker; notebook and calculator.

**Procedure:**
1. Select and label the soil samples. Use equal container volumes for each sample.
2. Measure a fixed volume of dry, loose soil into each container. Do not compress one sample more than another.
3. Measure the volume of water in the graduated cylinder.
4. Add water slowly to the soil, allowing it to enter the pore spaces. Stop when the sample is saturated and the water just reaches the top; avoid leaving a deep free-water layer.
5. Record the amount of water used. Repeat for each sample using the same method.
6. Estimate porosity:

   `Estimated porosity (%) = [water volume entering the saturated sample ÷ initial soil sample volume] × 100`

7. Compare the samples and explain why texture, aggregation, compaction and organic matter can affect pore space and drainage.

**Record:** Soil type/source; sample volume; water volume added; estimated porosity; notes on trapped air, settling and drainage.

**Interpretation and limitation:** This is a comparative classroom estimate, not a substitute for a soil laboratory measurement using bulk and particle density. Trapped air, soil swelling, incomplete saturation and compaction can affect the result. Keep sample preparation and packing consistent.

**Evidence:** University of Georgia Extension, *Soil and Water Relationships* (student method for estimating porosity and water-holding capacity): https://extension.uga.edu/content/dam/extension/programs-and-services/science-behind-our-food/documents/soilwaterrel.pdf. Texas Water Development Board, *Raising Your Water IQ — Teacher Guide* (measuring pore volume by water saturation): https://www.twdb.texas.gov/conservation/education/kids/RaisingYourWaterIQ/doc/RIQ_TeacherGuide_2020_fall.pdf.

## Practical 3 — Capillary movement of water in soil

**Aim:** Observe and compare the upward movement of water through different soil samples.

**Apparatus:** Two or more identical transparent tubes; fine mesh or filter paper to retain soil; elastic bands; shallow tray; water with a small amount of food colouring; ruler; stopwatch; equal, labelled soil samples.

**Procedure:**
1. Secure mesh over the lower end of each tube so soil cannot escape.
2. Fill each tube with the same dry soil volume and use the same gentle packing method. Do not compact one tube more than another.
3. Stand the tubes upright in a shallow tray. Add coloured water until the lower ends are immersed to the same shallow depth.
4. Start timing when water first contacts the soil. Keep the water level in the tray constant.
5. At agreed intervals (for example, 2, 5, 10 and 20 minutes), measure the height of the visibly wet front above the water level.
6. Record the observations for each soil sample. Repeat once if time and sample supply permit.
7. Compare the rate and height of capillary rise. Relate differences cautiously to pore-size distribution, texture and packing.

**Record:** Soil sample; initial packing depth; immersion depth; time; wet-front height; visible differences.

**Interpretation and limitation:** This is a comparative demonstration. It does not by itself establish a universal ranking for all soils: aggregation, packing, initial moisture and pore continuity influence the result. Do not use the height from one short trial as a field irrigation value.

**Evidence:** Spasić et al. (2023), *Determination of physical properties of undisturbed soil samples according to V. Novák*, MethodsX 10, 102133 (describes capillary water saturation and gravimetric observations): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10034492/.

## Practical 4 — Observation of soil organisms

**Aim:** Observe visible organisms and organic material in soil samples from different approved locations.

**Apparatus:** Trowel; gloves; labelled shallow trays; hand lens; white sheet/card; soft brush or forceps used carefully; notebook; camera (optional).

**Procedure:**
1. With permission, take small samples of surface soil and leaf litter from two or more safe locations. Label each location and return point.
2. Place each sample in a separate tray over a white sheet. Do not mix samples.
3. Observe gently with a hand lens. Look for visible animals, burrows, casts, roots, leaf fragments and decomposing material.
4. Record organisms only to the level that can be identified confidently (for example, earthworm, ant or other small invertebrate). Do not guess a species name.
5. Compare the samples. Record the number and type of visible organisms, visible organic material, moisture and other obvious differences.
6. Return living organisms and the remaining sample to the exact collection location unless the teacher has specified an approved alternative.

**Record:** Location; sample description; organism observed; count; visible organic material; moisture; limitations.

**Safety/limitation:** Wear gloves, avoid handling unknown organisms, wash hands and tools, and do not collect from contaminated locations. Failure to see an organism in a small sample does not prove that the soil contains no organisms.

**Evidence:** Natural History Museum, *Practical observation: What’s in soil?* (sample collection, tray/hand-lens observation, gloves, discussion and return of soil): https://www.nhm.ac.uk/schools/teaching-resources/key-stage-2/rocks-fossils-and-dinosaurs/practical-observation-whats-in-soil.html.

## Practical 5 — Observation/estimation of soil organic matter

**Aim:** Compare visible organic material in different soil samples and understand the limits of a visual estimate.

**Student-safe qualitative procedure:**
1. Collect equal, labelled soil samples from approved locations. Include the upper soil and any visible leaf litter as separate observations.
2. Spread each sample thinly in a labelled tray. Use a hand lens to identify visible roots, leaf fragments, decomposed plant material and dark humus-like material.
3. Remove and record recognisable plant residues separately from mineral particles. Do not claim that colour alone measures organic-matter percentage.
4. Compare the samples and explain how organic residues contribute to soil structure, nutrient cycling and organisms.
5. Record observations as qualitative categories only (for example, few / some / many visible residues), with the method and limitations stated.

**Optional laboratory-only quantitative method:** Loss on ignition can estimate mass loss after drying and controlled heating, but it requires a suitable muffle furnace, accurate balance, crucibles, tongs, cooling equipment and trained supervision. It is not an ordinary student bench experiment. If the school lacks this equipment and a trained operator, do not attempt it. A qualified laboratory may use a validated local method; the University of Georgia method is an example, not an instruction to improvise a furnace procedure in class.

**Record:** Sample location; visible plant residues; colour/structure observations; qualitative category; whether a laboratory method was actually used.

**Safety/limitation:** Do not burn soil over a flame or use household cooking equipment. A visual observation cannot produce a reliable organic-matter percentage. Loss-on-ignition can be affected by soil composition and the laboratory method.

**Evidence:** Natural History Museum, *What’s in soil?* (visible organic and inorganic material and observation): https://www.nhm.ac.uk/schools/teaching-resources/key-stage-2/rocks-fossils-and-dinosaurs/practical-observation-whats-in-soil.html. University of Georgia Soil, Plant and Water Laboratory, *Soil Organic Matter — Loss on Ignition Method* (laboratory apparatus and controlled-heating method): https://aesl.ces.uga.edu/soil/OMLOI.pdf.
